## Chapter 2: Father and Son

Tomas Vane lived at the top of the town, in two rooms under the eaves of a tall, crooked house on Ropewalk Lane, where the street got so steep that the cobbles turned into steps. He had lived there for thirty years, since the year he married, and he had stayed there after his wife died and his knees went and the sea no longer wanted him, because the window looked out over the whole of the harbour, and from it a man could see every ship that came and went.

He had seen the *Gull's Promise* come round the light at dawn. He had seen her flag at half-mast. And he had sat down very suddenly on his one good chair, and stayed there for the whole of the morning with his hands folded in his lap and his eyes on the ship, because his legs would not take him down the hill to the quay and he could not, from the window, see the faces on her deck.

When the door banged open and his son came through it, he could not stand up. He held out his arms instead.

"It was the captain," Edric said into his shoulder. "Not me. Halloran. Father, it was Halloran. I'm sorry, I should have sent a boy up, I should have thought—"

"Hush," said Tomas. "Hush. You're here." He held his son's face in his two old, hard hands and looked at it. "You're brown as a nut. You've grown. Have you grown? You can't have grown, you're nineteen." He laughed, and it turned into a cough, and the cough went on a little too long.

Edric held him until it passed. And holding him, he noticed what he had been too happy to see: how light the old man was. How the shoulders under the shirt were all bone. How the shirt itself, which Edric remembered as his father's best, was frayed at the cuffs and patched at the elbows and hung on him like a sail on a calm day.

He looked around the room. It was very clean, as it always was; Tomas had been a sailor, and sailors keep things squared away. But the shelf by the hearth, where there were always jars of beans and oats and a twist of salt pork hanging from the hook, was bare except for a single jar a quarter full. There was no fire in the grate, though the morning was cool. The bread crock on the table was empty.

"Father," Edric said slowly. "I left you money. Before I sailed. Forty silver marks, I left. And my wages were to come to you from Master Moren's office every month."

"So they did," said Tomas. "So they did." He looked away, out of the window. "Don't fuss, boy."

"Where is it?"

The old man sighed. He had never been able to lie to his son; he had never been able to lie to anyone, which was one of the reasons he had never risen above bosun. "There was a debt," he said. "An old one. Before you sailed. I didn't want to trouble you with it."

"A debt to who?"

There was a step on the stair outside, a heavy, shuffling step, and the door, which Edric had left open, filled up with a man.

"Well, if it isn't the young captain himself!" said Cade Rusk. "I heard the news down at the Anchors! Captain Vane! Tomas, didn't I always say? Didn't I say he'd go far? The very first day I met him, a little lad on the stairs this high, I said to my Agna, that boy'll go far."

Cade Rusk was the Vanes' neighbour, on the floor below. He was a tailor by trade and a drinker by vocation, a big, soft, loose-limbed man of forty with a face like a pudding left out in the rain and small, quick, wet eyes. He had a wife named Agna, who was thin and sharp and hated him, and a shop on Ropewalk Lane where the cloth gathered dust in the window because nobody in Saltmere trusted Cade Rusk to measure them sober. He was always very friendly. He was friendly now, coming forward with his hand out and his face beaming, and Edric shook the hand because he had been raised to.

"Neighbour Rusk," he said. "My father was just telling me about a debt."

The beam flickered, just slightly. "Ah," said Cade. "Well. Well, now. That's between your father and me, lad, and all very friendly, all very friendly. Just a few marks, from years back. I'd not have mentioned it, not on a happy day like this."

"How much?"

"Edric," said his father.

"How much, neighbour?"

Cade spread his hands. "Thirty-eight marks," he said. "From the winter your mother was sick. You'd not remember. You were away on your first ship, the old *Heron*. I lent your father the money for the physician, and the medicines, and the burying, after. Thirty-eight marks. And he's been paying it back, a little and a little, and he's very nearly done. Very nearly. I said to him, Tomas, I said, there's no hurry, not with your boy away at sea. But he would insist. Proud man, your father. Proud."

Edric looked at his father, and his father would not look back.

"I paid it," Tomas said to the window. "I paid it all, out of the forty you left, the week you sailed. I didn't want it hanging over you. And I wouldn't touch your wages, not those, they were for the wedding. For you and the girl. I've been putting them in the box, every month. They're all there. You count them." He jerked his chin at a little iron-bound box on the shelf. "Every mark."

"Then what have you been eating?" said Edric, very quietly.

His father said nothing.

"Eight months," said Edric. "Eight months, and you've been living on what? On oats and water? Father, there's no fire. There's no *bread*."

"There's work at the rope-sheds," Tomas said. "Picking oakum. A few coppers a week. It does well enough. A man doesn't need much, at my age."

Edric turned on Cade Rusk, who was edging back towards the door, and something in his face made the big man stop.

"You knew," Edric said. "You live downstairs. You knew he was starving."

"Now, now," said Cade. "Now, lad, that's not fair, that's not fair at all. I offered! I said to him, Tomas, come down and take a bit of supper with us, Agna's made a stew, and he'd say no, no, he'd eaten. What was I to do, force it down him? He's a grown man. Proud. I told you he was proud."

It might even have been true. That was the thing about Cade Rusk: it was always just possible that he was telling the truth, and impossible ever to be sure. He had a way of sliding his eyes sideways, as if the truth were a coin he was keeping in his other hand.

Edric breathed out. His anger went out of him with the breath, because his father had put a hand on his arm, and because it was his homecoming, and because Cade Rusk was not worth it.

"Thank you for the loan, neighbour," he said. "In the winter my mother was sick. It was kind of you. I didn't know about it."

Cade brightened at once. "Ah, think nothing of it. Nothing. Neighbours." His eyes drifted, the way they did, to the little iron-bound box on the shelf. "And a captain now, eh! Captain Vane! That'll be a fine wage. Twice what a mate gets, I'd think. Three times."

"I wouldn't know yet," said Edric. "I haven't signed the papers. Good day, neighbour."

Cade took the hint. He went, still beaming, still talking, *a fine day, a fine day for the Vanes*, and they heard his heavy step going down the stairs and the door of his rooms banging below, and then, faintly, his voice raised and his wife's raised back.

Edric closed the door and leaned against it.

"Don't," said his father. "Don't look at me like that. I'm not dead."

"You might have been."

"I've been hungrier," said Tomas. "In the war, on the northern blockade. Six weeks on a biscuit a day, and the biscuits walking about on their own with the weevils in them. This is nothing to that." But his voice cracked a little on the end of it, and he put his hand over his eyes. "I wanted you to have a start. That's all. A proper start, with the girl. Not like your mother and me. We started with nothing, and we kept on having nothing, and I don't regret a day of it, not a day, but I wanted better for you. That's all."

Edric crossed the room and knelt down by the chair and put his arms around his father's knees, as he had done when he was six years old and afraid of the thunder.

"I'm a captain," he said. "Do you understand? A captain. I'll have a share in every cargo. In two years we'll have a house on the hill with a garden, and you'll have a fire in every room and a cook to shout at. And you'll never pick oakum again as long as you live. I swear it."

"Don't swear," said his father. "Never swear, son. The sea hears." But he was smiling, and he put his hand on Edric's hair.

---

Edric went out an hour later and came back laden like a mule.

He had bought bread, two great round loaves still warm from the bakehouse on Mill Street, and cheese, and a whole smoked ham, and a sack of potatoes and a sack of oats, and onions and carrots and a crock of butter and a crock of honey, and a bottle of good red wine and a bottle of bad brandy, and a bundle of firewood so big that he had to come up the stairs sideways. He built a fire in the grate and set a pot over it and made a stew the way his mother used to, with too much pepper, because pepper was the one thing a sailor's family never lacked. He made his father eat two bowls of it, and then a third, and then a slab of bread and honey, until the old man held up his hands and begged for mercy.

The light went gold, and then red, and then blue. The fire crackled. The harbour below them filled up with lamps.

They talked the way they had not talked since Edric was a boy: of the voyage, of the Cape of Storms, of Port Jasmine with its towers of green glass and its spice markets where you could buy anything in the world, of a sea-serpent's skeleton they had seen washed up on a beach in the far south, forty yards long, its bones as white as chalk. Of Captain Halloran. Of the way he died.

"He was a good man," said Tomas. "A good, plain man. I sailed with him once, years ago, before you were born. Never cursed a man he didn't have to. Never flogged one at all." He was quiet a moment. "Did he say anything, at the end?"

Edric thought of the oilskin packet at the bottom of his sea-chest, which was now at the foot of the stairs where the porters had left it. *Don't ask. Better you don't know.*

"He said to tell Master Moren he owed him a bottle," he said. "And he asked me to do him a favour. To carry a message, after the wedding. Just a message."

"Carry it where?"

"To Solace."

His father looked at him, and for a moment the old sailor's eyes were sharper than they had been all evening.

"What kind of message?"

"A private one. I gave my word I wouldn't open it."

Tomas looked at the fire for a long time.

"Halloran was a good man," he said at last. "But he had his own ideas about some things. About the King. About the sea." He spoke slowly, choosing every word, as if each one were a stone he had to test before he stepped on it. "There's a lot of talk, these days, in the back of the Anchors. About the Ebb. About the old days, before the Drying. Talk's cheap. But talk gets men hanged, son, and it gets the men who carry their letters hanged too, and the King's justice doesn't much care whether you opened the letter or not."

"It's just a letter."

"Nothing is ever just a letter." His father sighed. "But you gave your word. To a dying man. I'd not ask you to break that; I'd not have raised you to. Only be careful. Give it, and forget it, and don't speak of it to anybody. Not even to the girl. Promise me that."

"I promise."

"And don't—"

"Don't swear," said Edric, smiling. "I know. The sea hears."

His father smiled back, and looked out of the window at the dark harbour and the darker sea beyond it, where the light on the Teeth blinked, far off, like a red eye.

"It does, you know," he said. "Your mother used to say so. She was Weir-folk, from the marshes, same as your girl. The Weir-folk have stories about the sea that the rest of us have forgotten. She used to say that once, long ago, the sea had a voice, and people could hear it if they listened. And then something happened, and it went quiet." He shook his head. "Old stories. You'd better go. You'll want to see your girl before the whole town tells her you're back, and she'll want to box your ears for not running straight to the Weir the minute you landed."

Edric laughed and got up. At the door he turned.

"Father. The box. My wages. Spend them. Please. Buy coal. Buy meat. I'll have more."

"I'll buy coal," said Tomas. "The rest is for the wedding. Go on."

---

Below them, in two cramped rooms that smelled of old beer and older cloth, Cade Rusk sat at his table in the dark and listened to the young captain's footsteps going down the stairs, past his door, and out into the street.

He had heard most of it. The ceiling between the two floors was thin, and he had been sitting directly under the fire, with his head cocked and a mug in his hand, for most of the evening. He had heard about the house on the hill with a garden, and the cook, and the share in every cargo. He had heard about Port Jasmine, and the sea-serpent, and the dead captain, and the message for Solace.

He sat with that last one for a while, turning it over the way he turned over coins.

A message for Solace, from a dead captain, that the boy had promised not to open. That the old man said could get a man hanged.

Cade did not know what to do with that, exactly. He was not a clever man, and he knew it, which made him more careful than clever men are. But he knew that some things were worth money, if you knew who to sell them to, and that some things were worth more than money. He knew that the young captain had been very rude to him today, in front of his own father, with that look in his eyes, *you knew he was starving*, as if Cade Rusk were nothing but dirt on the floor. And he knew that the young captain would be living in a house on the hill with a garden in two years' time, and that Cade Rusk would still be here, in two rooms on Ropewalk Lane, with a wife who hated him and a shop nobody came into.

He thought about all of that, in the dark, and drank, and thought some more.

"Cade," said his wife from the other room. "Come to bed."

"In a bit," said Cade. "In a bit." And he got up, and put on his coat, and went out into the night, down the hill towards the harbour and the lights of the Three Anchors, where a man could always find someone to drink with, and someone to talk to, and someone who might know what a thing was worth.
