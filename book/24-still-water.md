## Chapter 24: Still Water

The first death in the house of the High Justicar came in the autumn, a fortnight after the Lord Marshal fell on his sword, and nobody thought anything of it at all.

The old Marquis of Aubray, the father of Valtor's first wife, Lady Celestine, had come up from his estates in the north for the season, as he did every year, with the old Marquise, to stay with his son-in-law and see his granddaughter. He was seventy-eight. He had a weak chest. He caught a cough in the first week of the autumn rains, and it went to his lungs, and the physician said it was the blight-cough, which was everywhere now, even in Solace, even in the great houses on the Street of Lanterns. Within a month the old man was dead.

The old Marquise followed him three weeks later. Grief, people said. They had been married fifty years. It happened sometimes, with the old: one went, and the other simply stopped. The physician said it was the blight-cough again, caught nursing her husband. Very sad. Very common.

Both of them had left everything to their granddaughter, Lyse.

---

Edric heard about the first death at a reception, and about the second from Maxim Moren, who came to the house with the blue door in his white and gold, very pale, and sat in the hall of green glass and could not keep still.

"She's frightened," he said. "Lyse. She won't say so. She never says anything. But I've known her for two years, and I've never seen her like this." He had been courting Lyse Valtor for two years, it turned out, in secret, across the garden wall at the back of the High Justicar's house, which ran along an alley where a young Guard captain could stand at night in the shadows and talk to a girl at a window. The High Justicar knew nothing about it. The High Justicar intended his daughter for a Radiant lord of fifty with estates in the north and the ear of the King, and the marriage was to be in the spring. "She says the house feels wrong. She says she lies awake at night and listens to it. She says her stepmother watches her." He looked at the Count with his honest grey eyes. "My lord, I don't know why I'm telling you this. I don't know what you could possibly do. But I don't know who else to tell."

Edric did not answer for a long moment. He was looking at the fountain, at the water dancing in the black marble pool.

He knew what it was. He had known since the old Marquis fell ill. He had known since the evening in the stillroom at the top of the grey house, when a small, pretty, plump woman with pale bright eyes had held a phial to the lamp and asked, quite lightly, about a sleep that did not end.

*Blackwater.* A few drops, in a glass of wine, every day, for a month. It fills the lungs with water, slowly, the way the blight-cough does. A physician would say it was the blight-cough. There's no way to tell the difference.

He had told her that. He had told her the name, and the effect, and how long it took, and that there was no cure. He had told her because he had felt the want in her, cold and hard and patient, pulling her fortune towards her fat solemn son; and he had thought of Valtor, holding a letter in the fire; and he had wanted, for a moment, just a moment, to see what she would do.

He knew now.

"Tell her," he said at last, very quietly, "not to eat or drink anything in that house that she hasn't seen made with her own eyes. Not anything. Tell her to take her meals from the kitchen herself, from the common pot, the one the servants eat from. Tell her to draw her own water from the well." He looked at Maxim. "And tell her grandfather the same."

Maxim stared at him. All the colour went out of his face.

"My lord," he said. "My lord, what are you saying?"

"I'm saying," said the Count of Cristal, "that there's a poisoner in the house of the High Justicar. And that the two old people were not the end of it. And that the next will be your Lyse."

---

The third death came a week later, and this time everybody thought something of it.

It was old Noll, Severin Valtor's servant, who had served him for fifty years and spoke for him with the board. He came down to the kitchen one morning to fetch his master's breakfast, as he had every morning for fifty years, and while he was waiting for the cook, he drank the glass of wine that had been poured for the old man and left on the tray. He was thirsty, he said. The old master never drank it anyway. It was a thing he did, every morning, when no one was looking; the cook had seen him do it a hundred times and said nothing.

He was dead by noon. Not slowly, like the old Marquis and the Marquise, over weeks. In three hours. His lungs filled with water as if he were drowning on dry land, and he choked, and turned blue, and died on the kitchen floor with the cook screaming.

It had not been a few drops. It had been the whole of a phial, all at once, in a single glass, meant for an old paralysed man who could not move his hands to push it away, and who was taking too long to die.

The physician who came was not the family's usual man, who was away in the country. He was a younger man, a Sardhi, who had studied in Mazrah. He knelt over old Noll's body on the kitchen floor and looked at his blue face, and at the froth on his lips, and at the glass on the tray, and went very quiet. And then he went upstairs to the High Justicar's study, and shut the door, and told him.

---

Edric watched it.

He had not meant to. He had told himself, after the night in the stillroom, that he would not watch the grey house. That what happened there was Heloise Valtor's sin, not his. That he had done nothing but answer a question.

But after Maxim came to him, he took the bowl of still water out of the corner of his desk, in the study that had been Valtor's bedroom, and set it in front of him, and breathed, and found the stillness, and looked.

It was the hardest thing the books taught. *On the Discipline of Still Water*, the chapter called *The Far Seeing*. *As a still pool shows the sky above it, so the stillest water shows what is far away, to one who knows how to ask. But the asking costs. For to see far, thou must send thyself far, a little, into the water; and what is sent is not always all returned.*

He sent himself.

He felt it go out of him: a thread of himself, cold and thin, going down into the water in the bowl and out through it, through all the water in the world, the way the Deep was in all water, until it came up again in a glass on a tray in a kitchen at the other end of the Street of Lanterns. He saw the kitchen, wavering, as through a sheet of rain. He saw old Noll on the floor. He saw the Sardhi physician kneeling. He followed the physician up the stairs, through a jug of water on a landing, through a vase of flowers in a corridor, through the ink in an inkwell on a desk.

And he saw Valtor.

The High Justicar sat at his desk in his study with his hands flat on the leather, and his face like a sheet of grey paper, and listened to the physician tell him that three people in his house had been poisoned. That the old Marquis and the Marquise had not died of the blight-cough. That it was a Sardhi poison, very rare, called blackwater, that mimicked the blight-cough exactly, and that the physician had seen it twice before, in Mazrah, in the houses of the rich, where it was known as *the inheritance draught*. That old Noll had died of a single great dose meant for old Severin. That there was a poisoner in the house. And that the physician was obliged, by law, to report it to the Crown.

"Who?" said Valtor. His voice was quite steady. "Who benefits?"

The physician looked at him with pity.

"Your daughter, my lord," he said gently. "Mistress Lyse. The old Marquis and the Marquise left everything to her. And your father, I believe, has left his own fortune to her also. If old Severin had died this morning, she would have been the richest young woman in Valemont."

Valtor did not move.

"I don't say she did it, my lord," said the physician. "I say only that the law will ask. And that I must report it."

"No," said Valtor.

"My lord—"

"No," said the High Justicar of Valemont. "You will not report it. You will say that my father's servant died of a seizure. He was old; it happens. You will sign the certificate to that effect. And you will say nothing, to anyone, ever, about any of this. In return, you will have my protection, and my patronage, and a position at court, and more money than you've ever seen." He looked up, and his eyes were like stones. "Or you will not, and you will find that a foreign physician who makes accusations against the family of the High Justicar has a very short career in Solace. Choose."

The physician looked at him for a long time.

Then he picked up a pen.

---

Edric took his hands away from the bowl, and the water went still, and the image went out of it. He sat back in his chair.

He was very cold. Colder than he had ever been, even under the Teeth. The thread he had sent out had come back to him, mostly, but something had not come back, some small part, left behind in the water, in the ink, in the glass on the tray. And something else had come back with it that had not been his. He could taste it: salt, and rot, and the sickness in the sea. He could feel it in his chest, like water in the lungs.

*Your daughter,* the physician had said. *Who benefits.*

And Valtor had not asked whether it was true. Valtor had not gone to his daughter, or to his wife, or to his father. Valtor had made it go away. Because the truth did not matter. The truth was a private matter, between a man and the Sun. What mattered was order. What mattered was that no one ever knew.

He thought: *She's next. Lyse. Heloise will try again. She has to now. The old man's too stubborn to die, and the girl will be the richest heiress in Valemont the moment he does. And Valtor will let it happen, and sign the certificate, because the alternative is a trial, and the trial would ruin him.*

And he thought, after a long time, very quietly, the thing he had not let himself think since the night in the stillroom:

*I did this. I gave her the name.*

---

He bought the house next door to the Valtors' the following day.

It was not difficult. The house was owned by an old widow who lived in the country and rented it out, and she was very happy to sell it, at once, for twice what it was worth, to a quiet black-browed man called Corso who paid in gold and asked for the keys that afternoon. And it had something the house with the blue door did not: a wall. A shared wall, three feet thick, of old grey stone, between its back bedroom on the second floor and the back bedroom on the second floor of the High Justicar's house. Which was Lyse's.

In the old days, the two houses had belonged to a single family, and there had been a door in that wall. It had been bricked up a hundred years ago. Bastien found it in an hour, behind a panel, and opened it in a night, with a chisel and a great deal of patience, from the empty house's side, until there was nothing left between the two rooms but a single thickness of plaster and a tall painted screen that stood against the wall on Lyse's side.

Edric went through it on the third night.

---

She was not asleep. She was sitting up in bed, in the dark, with her knees drawn up and her arms around them, listening. She had been listening to the scraping in the wall for three nights. She had told no one. She had sat up in the dark, every night, and listened, and waited.

When the screen moved, and a tall figure in black stepped round it, she did not scream. She went very still, and stared at him.

"Mistress Lyse," he said, very softly. "Don't be afraid. It's the Count of Cristal. Maxim Moren's friend."

"I know who you are," said Lyse Valtor. Her voice was quite steady, though he could feel her heart racing. "Maxim told me you'd come. He said you'd find a way. He said the Count of Cristal could find a way into anywhere." She looked at the dark gap behind the screen where the wall had been. "I didn't think he meant through a wall."

"I'm sorry for the intrusion."

"Don't be." She pushed back her dark hair. "My grandfather told me too. With the board, today. Letter by letter. He said: *the man who lives in our old house will come. Trust him. He is the tide.*" She looked at him with her grave dark eyes, her grandfather's eyes. "I didn't know what that meant."

He sat down on the edge of the chair by her bed.

"How much do you know?" he said.

She looked at him for a long time.

"I know that my grandparents were poisoned," she said. "I know that old Noll was poisoned. I know that it was meant for Grandfather. I heard the cook screaming. I heard the physician on the stairs. I heard my father's door close." She was quiet. "And I know who did it. I've always known, I think. Since I was ten, and she came into the house, and looked at me for the first time. She looks at me the way a cat looks at a bird." She pressed her lips together. "And I know my father knows. And that he won't do anything. Because if he did, there would be a trial, and the High Justicar's wife would hang, and the High Justicar would be finished."

"Yes," said Edric.

"He'd rather I died," said Lyse Valtor, quite calmly. "Wouldn't he? Than that."

He did not answer. He did not need to.

She put her face down on her knees for a while, and he let her. When she lifted it again, her eyes were dry.

"What do I do?" she said.

---

He came every night after that, for eleven nights.

He brought her food: bread and fruit and cheese from the market, that he had bought himself, and water from the fountain in his own courtyard. She ate it in the dark, and every morning, at breakfast, she sat at her father's table and pushed the food around her plate and drank nothing, and her stepmother watched her with her pale bright eyes. Every morning, the glass of wine at Lyse's place was poured, and every morning Lyse lifted it and pretended to sip, and set it down, and left it. And every night, when he came through the wall, Edric laid his hand over the glass on her bedside table, which her stepmother's maid had brought up with a candle, and felt the water in it, and felt the blackwater, cold and grey and wrong, and drew it out, the way he had drawn the blight out of a child's lungs at Grey Point, and poured it down the drain.

It was there every night. A little more every night.

"She's patient," said Lyse, watching him do it, on the fifth night. "Isn't she."

"Yes."

"How long can we go on like this?"

He did not answer. He did not know.

They talked, those nights. In the dark, in whispers, with the candle out, so that no one passing in the corridor would see a light under the door. She told him about her mother, whom she barely remembered: a tall, kind, quiet woman who had smelled of lavender and died of a fever when Lyse was five. She told him about her grandfather, who had taught her to read when she was four, before the palsy, and who had told her stories, at night, about a time long ago when the sea had a voice. She told him about Maxim, and the alley behind the garden wall, and the first night he had stood there in the rain in his white and gold and called up to her window, and how she had nearly not answered, and how glad she was that she had.

"He talks about you," she said. "Maxim. All the time. About his father, and the debts, and the ship that came in from nowhere. *The Tide remembers.* He says it the way other men say their prayers." She turned her head on the pillow and looked at him in the dark. "It was you. Wasn't it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He thought about how to answer.

"Because his father was kind to me once," he said at last, "when I was young, and had nothing. And because I owed him. And because he deserved better than he got."

"Is that why you're helping me? Because you owe Maxim's father?"

"No," said Edric.

She waited.

"I'm helping you," he said slowly, "because I'm the reason you need help. Your stepmother learned about blackwater from me. I told her. In her stillroom, the first night I dined at your father's house. She asked, and I told her. The name. What it did. That there was no cure." He could not look at her. "I wanted to see what she'd do. That's the truth. I knew what she was, and I told her anyway, because I hate your father, and I wanted to see what would happen to his house. And your grandparents are dead, and old Noll is dead, and you're lying here in the dark eating bread I bring you through a hole in the wall, because of me."

There was a long silence in the dark room.

"You hate my father," said Lyse at last, very quietly. "Why?"

He told her. Not all of it. But enough. A letter, and a fire, and a Sun Warrant, and a cell under the sea. Fourteen years. A boy who had trusted a magistrate's face.

When he had finished, she lay very still for a long time.

"My father did that," she said. "To you."

"Yes."

"And you came here to destroy him. To take everything from him. That's what all this is. The Lord Marshal. Baron Quell's bank. All of it."

"Yes."

"And me?" said Lyse Valtor. "Was I part of it? Was taking me from him part of it?"

"No," said Edric. "Never. Not for a moment."

She was quiet again. Then she reached out, in the dark, and found his hand on the coverlet, and held it. Her fingers were warm. He could not remember the last time someone had held his hand who was not Hayda.

"My grandfather says," she said, "that a man is not the worst thing he has ever done. He says that's what the old Tidewardens believed. That a man is like the sea: what's on the surface changes, every day, every hour. But what's underneath is always there, if you go deep enough." She squeezed his hand. "I think you're very deep, my lord Count. I think you've been very far down for a very long time. But I don't think you're as cold as you'd like everyone to believe."

He could not answer. He sat in the dark beside her bed, holding her hand, and the Tidemark burned cold on his face, and something in his chest, under the cold, under the ice, under the sea, moved, very slightly, like a fish turning in deep water.

---

On the eleventh night, he told her what they had to do.

"It's not going to stop," he said. "She'll keep trying. And one day, one morning, at breakfast, you won't be careful enough, or she'll find another way, a way I can't draw out. In your food. In the air. In the candle. And then you'll die." He looked at her in the dark. "Unless she thinks you already have."

Lyse looked at him. "What do you mean?"

"There's a thing the old Tidewardens could do," he said. "The ones who were strong in Still Water. They could make a stillness in someone. A sleep so deep that it looks like death. No breath. No heartbeat that anyone could find. Cold skin. Nothing. The body goes still, like a pool on a windless day, and stays still, for as long as the Warden holds it. And then the Warden wakes them, and they get up, and they're unharmed."

"You can do that?"

"I've never done it," said Edric. "But I've read how. I think I can."

She stared at him.

"You want me to die," she said slowly. "To pretend to die. So she'll stop."

"I want her to think she's won. I want your father to sign the certificate. I want them to put you in the family tomb at the Temple of the Noon, and close the door, and go home, and believe it's over. And then, that night, I want to open the tomb and take you out, and put you on a ship, and send you somewhere she'll never find you. And in a month, or two, when this is finished, when I've finished it, I'll come and wake you. And you'll be free."

"And Maxim?"

"Maxim will think you're dead," said Edric. "He has to. Everyone has to. If even one person knows, it won't work. Your stepmother isn't a fool. She'll be watching."

Lyse lay very still in the dark.

"He'll grieve," she said. "Terribly. He'll want to die too. I know him. He's like that." Her voice cracked. "I can't do that to him."

"I'll look after him," said Edric. "I promise. I won't let him do anything foolish. And when it's done, I'll take him to you." He leaned forward. "Lyse. It's this, or you die for real. Maybe not this month. But soon. I can't stay in this room forever, drawing poison out of your water. And your father won't save you. You know he won't."

She was silent for a long time.

"What happens," she said at last, "if something goes wrong? If you can't wake me?"

He had been afraid she would ask that.

"The books say," he said carefully, "that the stillness can be held for forty days. Not longer. After forty days, the body forgets how to wake. If the Warden doesn't come within forty days, the sleep becomes the real thing." He held her eyes. "I'll come. Long before forty days. I swear it."

"Don't swear," said Lyse Valtor, and almost smiled. "Grandfather says you should never swear. He says the sea hears."

"I know," said Edric. "That's why I'm swearing it."

She looked at him for a long, long time in the dark.

"All right," she said.

---

He did it the next night.

She lay on her back in her bed, in her white nightgown, with her dark hair spread on the pillow and her hands folded on her breast, and her eyes open, looking at him. He sat beside her with a bowl of still water on the bedside table, and his hand flat on her forehead, and breathed.

*On the Discipline of Still Water. The Stilling.* *Find the stillness in thyself first, as deep as thou canst go. Then find the waters in the other: the blood, the breath, the tears, the water in every part. And make them still. Not by force; never by force. By example. As a still pool on a windless day makes the air above it still, so thy stillness, held over the other, will still their waters, until they lie as quiet as thine own.*

He found the stillness. He went down into it, deeper than he had ever gone, deeper than in the Keep, deeper than under the Teeth, down and down into the cold dark quiet at the bottom of himself, where the sea was. And he held it there. And he reached out with it, through his hand, into her.

He felt her waters. Her blood, moving, warm, fast with fear. Her breath, rising and falling. The tears standing in her eyes. He held his stillness over them, the way the book said, and waited.

Slowly, slowly, they began to slow.

Her breathing slowed. Her heart slowed. The warmth went out of her blood, a little at a time, as if the tide were going out of her. Her eyes, looking up at him, grew heavy, and dim, and closed. The tears on her lashes stopped. Her breath grew shallower, and shallower, and stopped. Her heart beat, and beat, and beat, slower, and slower, and stopped.

She lay on the white pillow with her hands folded on her breast and her face perfectly still, perfectly peaceful, perfectly white, like a girl carved in marble on a tomb.

He kept his hand on her forehead. He could feel her, very faintly, under the stillness: a single thread of life, very thin, very deep, held quiet by his own. It ran from her into his hand, and up his arm, and into his chest, and lay there, beside his heart, beside the Tidemark, like a second, smaller heart, not beating. Waiting.

He would feel it, now, every moment, for forty days. If he let it go, she would wake. If he lost it, if he let himself be carried too far, if the sea in him rose too high and swept it away, she would not.

He took his hand away. She did not stir.

He sat beside her in the dark for a long time. Then he got up, and pulled the coverlet up over her folded hands, and went back through the gap in the wall, and closed the screen behind him.

---

Her maid found her in the morning.

The house woke screaming. The physician came, the Sardhi one, and knelt by the bed, and felt for a pulse, and found none, and held a mirror to her lips, and found no breath on it, and stood up, slowly, very slowly, and looked across the bed at the High Justicar in the doorway, and did not need to say anything.

Valtor stood in the doorway of his daughter's room for a long time, in his black robe, looking at her.

Then he said, quite steadily, "The blight-cough. She had a cough, these last weeks. I heard it."

"My lord," said the physician.

"The blight-cough," said Valtor. "Sign it."

And behind him, in the corridor, a small, pretty, plump woman in a pale grey morning gown stood with her hand at her throat and her pale eyes very bright, and said, softly, "Oh, the poor child. The poor, poor child."

---

They buried her three days later, in the Valtor family tomb in the gardens of the Temple of the Noon, under the Sunspire. Half of Solace came. Her father stood at the door of the tomb in his black robe with his face like stone. Her stepmother wept prettily into a lace handkerchief, with her fat solemn son beside her. Her grandfather was carried in his chair to the very edge of the tomb, and sat there in the sun with his ruined face, and his dark eyes open, staring at the coffin, and did not blink once, not once, the whole time.

Maxim Moren stood at the back of the crowd, in his white and gold, and did not move. He did not weep. Edric, standing beside him, felt the water in him, and it was like standing beside a frozen lake: all of it, every drop, gone still and hard and cold.

When it was over, when the door of the tomb had been closed, and the priests had sung, and the crowd had gone home, Maxim did not go home. He stayed in the gardens of the Temple, walking among the tombs, until it was dark. Edric stayed with him.

At midnight, Maxim stopped in front of the Valtor tomb, and stood looking at the closed bronze door, and took his officer's pistol out of his belt, and looked at that.

"Don't," said Edric.

Maxim did not turn round. "Go away, my lord. Please."

"No."

"She's dead." Maxim's voice was very quiet. "She's in there. In the dark. She was afraid of the dark, did you know that? She told me once. Ever since she was small. She always slept with a candle." His hand tightened on the pistol. "And I wasn't there. I was in the alley, the night before, under her window, and she didn't come to the window, and I thought she was asleep, and I went home. And she was dying. And I went *home*."

"Maxim."

"I don't want to live in a world she isn't in," said Maxim Moren. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, my lord. I know you mean well. But I don't."

Edric looked at the young man's back, at the pistol in his hand, at the closed bronze door. He felt the thread of Lyse Valtor's life lying beside his heart, still and quiet, waiting. Forty days.

*Everyone has to believe. If even one person knows, it won't work.*

He thought of Pieter Moren, reading a card by his bed every night. *The Tide remembers.* He thought of a girl in the dark, saying *he'll want to die too. I know him.*

"Maxim," he said. "Look at me."

Something in his voice made the young man turn round.

Edric let the stillness go.

He let it go all at once, the whole of the Count of Cristal, the face and the voice and the foreign lilt, and stood there in the dark garden of the Temple of the Noon, in the light of the lamps on the tombs, as himself. Edric Vane. Nineteen years older, and scarred, and hard, with the Tidemark glowing faint and blue on his throat and climbing his cheek. But himself.

"Your father had a captain once," he said quietly. "Twenty years ago. A boy, nineteen years old, called Edric Vane. Your father gave him the *Gull's Promise*, on the quay at Saltmere, the morning he brought her home. You were there. You were five. You were sitting on your father's shoulders, and you had a wooden sword, and you pointed it at me and said I was a pirate."

Maxim stared at him.

"Three days later, I was arrested at my wedding feast," said Edric. "Your father went to the magistrate every day for a month to plead for me. It cost him everything. It cost him his contracts, and his ships, and his house, nearly. Because he was kind to me, once, when I had nothing." He took a step closer. "Five years ago, I paid his debts. I sent your sister a diamond. I sent him a ship. I wrote a card. *The Tide remembers.*" He held out his hand. "I'm asking you, Maxim, for the sake of your father, who saved my life once without knowing it: give me the pistol. And give me one month. Thirty days. And then, if you still want to die, I won't stop you."

Maxim looked at him for a long time, white-faced, with the pistol in his hand.

"Why?" he whispered. "What can possibly change in thirty days?"

"Everything," said Edric Vane. "Wait, Maxim. And hope."

The young man's face crumpled. He went down on his knees in the dark garden, in front of the tomb, with the pistol falling out of his hand onto the grass, and wept, at last, the way Edric had wept in Faro's cell twenty years before.

Edric picked up the pistol, and put it in his coat, and knelt down beside him, and put his arm around his shoulders, and held him.

---

At three in the morning, when Maxim had been taken home by Bastien and put to bed with a sleeping draught, a closed carriage drew up at the back gate of the gardens of the Temple of the Noon. Two men got out of it, one tall and one thick-set, with lanterns, and a crowbar, and a key that the thick-set man had made from a wax impression of the lock on the Valtor tomb.

They were inside for a quarter of an hour. When they came out, the tall man was carrying something in his arms, wrapped in a cloak: something long and white and still.

By dawn, the carriage was at the harbour below the cliffs, where a sleek black two-masted cutter with grey and brown sails was waiting at the quay, with a short red-haired woman at the gangway.

"Forty days," said Edric, putting the still white shape into Jac Teague's arms. "Cristal. My rooms in the library. Keep her warm. Keep her safe. Don't let anyone touch her. If anything happens to me, if I'm not there in forty days—"

"You'll be there," said Jac. She looked down at the girl's white, peaceful face. "Gods, she's young." She looked up at him. "And you look like death yourself. When did you last sleep?"

He did not know.

"Go," he said. "Please."

The *Slip* cast off on the morning tide, and slipped out of the harbour of Solace under the white cliffs, and turned west, and was gone.

He stood on the quay and watched until her sails were out of sight. Beside his heart, the thread of Lyse Valtor's life lay still and quiet, going away from him across the sea, growing fainter with every mile, but holding. Holding.

Forty days.

*Thirty-nine,* he thought, and turned, and walked back up through the waking city to the Street of Lanterns, to finish what he had started.
