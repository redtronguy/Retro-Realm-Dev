# Part Five: The Sun King

## Chapter 27: The King Notices

Aurelian, by the Grace of the Sun King of Valemont, Eternal, had not been surprised by anything in a hundred and forty years.

He thought about that, sometimes, in the long golden afternoons at the top of the Sunspire, sitting in the light. He had tried to work out, once, exactly when it had been: the last time something had happened in the world that he had not foreseen. He thought it had probably been the great earthquake in the south, in the hundred and sixtieth year of his reign, when the city of Port Amber had slid into the sea in an afternoon. He had not expected that. He remembered the feeling quite clearly: a kind of lurch, like missing a step in the dark. He had rather enjoyed it.

Since then, nothing. Wars, and famines, and plagues, and rebellions; lords who rose and lords who fell; Tidewardens' grandchildren creeping into his archives at night; Radiant priests who grew ambitious and had to be quietly sent to the far provinces; empires in the south that rose and fell like tides. All of it foreseen. All of it managed. All of it, in the end, the same. Men wanted the same things, and feared the same things, and died, and were replaced by other men who wanted and feared the same things, and he went on, and on, and on, in the light at the top of the tower, drinking the sea.

He was very bored.

And then, a month ago, on a bright cold noon in the autumn, the sky over Solace had gone black.

---

He had felt it before it happened. That was the extraordinary thing. He had been sitting in his chair at the top of the Sunspire, in the great round room with walls of glass where the light of the Lock came up through the floor, from the chains, from three hundred miles away, through the sunfire that bound them to him, and he had felt the sea move.

Not the way it usually moved. The sea always moved; that was its nature. It heaved against the chains, every few heartbeats, and he felt each heave as a faint, distant tug, like a fish on a line, and had felt it every day for three hundred years, and long ago stopped noticing. This was different. This was the sea *rising*. All of it, everywhere, at once, as if something had called it. And he had felt, through the chains, something he had not felt in three hundred years: the Deep, awake. Turning. Looking. Not at him. At something else. At someone.

And then the storm.

He had stood at the glass wall of the great round room and watched it come in off the sea, black and boiling, and pour over the cliffs, and swallow the city. He had watched the lightning. He had watched the sea climb the face of the cliffs in great slow swells, like something trying to get out. He had felt, under it all, a mind. A human mind, small and furious and grieving, at the centre of the storm, like the eye of it. And he had felt the Deep pouring into that mind, through it, out of it, the way water pours through a breach in a dyke.

*Tide-taken*, he had thought, and for the first time in a hundred and forty years, Aurelian, Eternal, had been surprised.

He had flared the light at the top of the tower, then. Just once. Not to fight; he did not need to fight. Only to look. To see where it was coming from. And he had seen: a house on the Street of Lanterns. An old grey house, behind a high grey wall, at the far end of the street from a house with a blue door.

And then, as he watched, the mind at the centre of the storm had done something he had never seen a tide-taken Warden do, in three hundred years of watching them. It had stopped.

Not been stopped. Stopped itself. He had felt it: the small furious grieving mind reaching down, down, through the flood, to something very small and very deep, some still place at the bottom of itself, and holding on. And the storm had broken off, like a wave breaking on a rock. And the sky had cleared. And the mind had gone quiet.

He had sat in his chair at the top of the tower for a long time after that, in the light, thinking.

And then, last night, he had felt it again. Faint, this time. Very far, on the coast to the north, under the Pale Cliffs. The same mind, standing in the sea, doing something even stranger than before. Giving the Deep *back*.

He had turned the light then, and looked, and found it, lying on a beach. And it had looked back.

Aurelian smiled, in his chair, in the golden light.

He sent for the High Radiant.

---

Cassia Vell came to the house with the blue door at nine the next morning, alone, on foot, in the white and gold armour of the Radiant Order, with the sun of Aurelian blazing on her breastplate.

She was forty, tall and broad-shouldered, with short iron-grey hair and a plain, square, weathered face, and pale eyes that had looked at a great many things without flinching. She had been a Radiant knight for twenty-two years and High Radiant for six, commander of the whole Order, the King's sword in the way the Lord Marshal had been the King's army: closer, more personal, the blade he kept by his own hand. People said she had never lost a fight. People said she prayed for six hours a day. People said she was the only person in Valemont who could look at the King without lowering her eyes.

She stood in the courtyard of the house with the blue door, beside the black marble pool, and looked at the fountain, which had stopped dancing, and at the walls of green glass, and at the Count of Cristal coming down the stairs from the gallery in black, very pale, with his collar buttoned to the throat.

"My lord Count," she said. "The King requests the pleasure of your company. At the Sunspire. At noon."

Edric looked at her.

He had not slept. He had come back from the Pale Cliffs at dawn, on a horse that could barely walk, and sat in the study at the back of the house with the bowl of still water in front of him, and not touched it. He was cold, and weak, and empty, emptier than he had been since the Keep, and beside his heart the thread of Lyse Valtor's life lay very faint, very far, holding. *Twelve*, he thought. *Eleven, now.*

"And if I decline?" he said.

Cassia Vell looked at him with her pale eyes.

"No one has ever declined," she said. "I don't know what would happen." She paused. "I don't think either of us wants to find out."

---

He went.

Hayda begged him not to. Bastien stood in the door of the study with his arms folded and said nothing, which was worse. Maxim Moren, who had been sleeping in the house with the blue door since the night in the gardens of the Temple of the Noon because Edric did not trust him alone, said he would come too, and was told no, and argued, and was told no again.

"He won't kill me," said Edric. "Not in his own house. Not yet. If he wanted me dead, he'd have sent the whole Order, not one knight with an invitation." He buttoned his coat. "He wants to look at me. That's all. He's curious."

"And when he's finished looking?" said Hayda.

Edric did not answer.

"Get the ship ready," he said to Bastien. "The *Faro*. In the harbour, below the cliffs, with her sails loosed and the crew aboard. Everything we need. Everyone. If I'm not back by sunset, sail without me." He looked at Maxim. "Take him. Take him to Cristal. Whatever happens."

"Why Cristal?" said Maxim. "What's at Cristal?"

Edric looked at him for a long moment, at his honest grey eyes, his father's eyes, red-rimmed from a fortnight of grief.

"Wait and hope, Maxim," he said. "I promised you thirty days. Hold me to it."

---

The Sunspire rose from the highest point of the highest cliff of Solace like a needle of white stone. It had no door at its foot that anyone had ever seen. It had no windows, except at the very top, where the great round room with walls of glass sat on its summit like a crown. It was a thousand feet high, people said, though no one had ever measured it. It had been built in a single night, people said, three hundred years ago, by the King himself, with sunfire.

Cassia Vell took him in through a door that was not there until she laid her hand on the stone, and the stone remembered it was a door, and swung open on darkness, and then on light.

There were no stairs. There was a shaft, going straight up, through the heart of the tower, a thousand feet, white stone, and in the middle of it, a column of light. Gold light, solid, burning, like the chains under the Keep. Cassia stepped into it, and it took her, and lifted her, and she rose up the shaft as gently as a leaf rising on warm air.

"It won't hurt you," she said, looking down at him. "Not much."

He stepped into the light.

It hurt. It hurt the way the noon sun always hurt now, at the foot of the tower, the way a spike hurts, driven into the chest. But a thousand times worse. It was sunfire, the King's own, the oldest and strongest working in the world, and the Tidemark on his chest flared against it, cold against hot, the way water flares on a forge, and he felt himself scald. He gritted his teeth and did not cry out. And the light took him, and lifted him, and he rose up through the heart of the Sunspire, a thousand feet, in the burning gold, with the High Radiant above him, looking down.

"You're in pain," she said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

He looked up at her through the burning light.

"Because I'm what you were made to kill," he said.

---

The great round room at the top of the Sunspire was the most beautiful room Edric had ever seen, and the most terrible.

It was round, and very large, and its walls were glass, all the way round, so that standing in it you could see the whole of Valemont spread out below you: the white city on its cliffs, and the hills, and the coast running away north and south, and the sea, the endless glittering sea, all the way to the edge of the world. The floor was white stone. The ceiling was a dome of gold. And in the centre of the floor, there was a hole: a round well, ten feet across, going down into the tower. And out of the well came light.

It was the light of the chains. He knew it at once. He would have known it anywhere. Seven lines of gold light, coming up out of the well, taut and burning and shuddering, every few heartbeats, *boom*, with the same terrible heave he had felt through the floor of his cell for fourteen years. They came up out of the well and went up into the gold of the dome, and through it, and out, and he understood that they went up to the point of light at the very summit of the tower, the one that burned day and night, the one he had seen from the crest of the hill on the road from the Greyhills. And he understood that they went down, too: down the well, down through the tower, down through the cliffs of Solace, down under the sea, three hundred miles, to a cave under a black rock called the Teeth, where they plunged into black water and went on down, and down, and down, into the dark.

The King was sitting beside the well, in a chair of white wood, in the light. Drinking.

---

He was beautiful. Faro had said so, and Faro had been right, and Edric had not believed him.

He looked about thirty. He was tall and slender and golden: golden hair, cut short, golden skin, golden eyes, as if the light he sat in had soaked into him over three hundred years and stained him. He wore a plain white robe, without ornament, and no crown. His face was the most beautiful face Edric had ever seen: fine-boned, grave, gentle, perfectly calm, the face of a young saint in a temple painting. It hurt to look at. It hurt the way the sun hurt.

And behind the eyes, so old that it made the beauty unbearable, there was something else. Something that had been sitting in this chair, in this light, for three hundred years, watching the world go by below it like a river, and had long ago forgotten what it was to be part of it.

He was holding a cup. A plain white cup, like a cup for milk. And he was dipping it, gently, into the light coming up out of the well, the way you would dip a cup into a stream, and lifting it to his lips, and drinking.

Edric felt it. He felt it go through the chains, three hundred miles, down into the dark: a tug, a pull, a draining. And far away, at the bottom of the world, something vast flinched.

"You must be the Count of Cristal," said Aurelian, Eternal, and smiled. "Do sit down. Would you like some tea?"

---

He did not sit. Cassia Vell stood by the shaft of light, with her hand on her sword, and watched them both.

The King did not seem to mind. He set down his white cup on a small table beside his chair, where there was a teapot, and two other cups, very fine and very old, and poured, and held one out.

"No?" He put it down again. "Never mind. I don't get many visitors. I'm afraid I've forgotten how it's done." He looked at Edric with his golden eyes, with grave, gentle, perfect interest. "You're the one who made the storm. A month ago. I felt you. And last night, on the beach under the Pale Cliffs." He tilted his head. "You gave it back. Some of it. Didn't you?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I didn't want to become what was in it."

Aurelian looked at him for a long moment. Then he smiled again, with real pleasure, the way a scholar smiles at a new specimen.

"Do you know," he said, "in three hundred years, I have met eleven Tidewardens. Grandchildren of the ones who survived the Drying. They come to Solace, every forty or fifty years, one by one, very clever, very brave, and creep into my archives, looking for the Lock. And I find them, and we talk, and I send them somewhere to be quiet. Eleven of them." He picked up his white cup again, and turned it in his fingers. "Not one of them was tide-taken. Not one. You're the first since the Drying. The Deep hasn't had the strength for it, you see. I've been very careful about that." He dipped the cup in the light, and drank. "And now here you are. And you're giving it *back*. It's the most interesting thing that's happened to me in a hundred and forty years."

Edric felt the drink go down the chains. Felt the Deep flinch.

"Stop doing that," he said.

The King looked at him, mildly surprised.

"Doing what?"

"Drinking. While I'm watching." Edric's hands were fists. "I can feel it. Every time. I can feel it hurt."

"Ah." Aurelian looked down at the cup in his hand, as if he had forgotten it was there. "Yes. I suppose you can. I'm sorry. Habit." He set it down. "Three hundred years. You stop noticing."

---

"Who taught you?" said the King. "Someone must have. Tide-taking isn't enough on its own. You'd have burned out in a month, or drowned. Someone taught you the disciplines. The stillness. The breathing." He considered. "The old man on the Teeth. The last of the eleven. Faro. I sent him there myself, nearly fifty years ago. I thought he'd died long since."

"He died six years ago," said Edric. "In his cell. In the dark. Listening to you drink."

"Yes," said the King thoughtfully. "Yes, I thought that would be fitting. He was proud. He wanted so badly to free his god." He looked at Edric. "And he taught you, in the dark, in the cell next to his. And then you got out somehow, and the Deep took you. And now you've come to Solace with a great deal of money and a great deal of anger, and in one season you've destroyed my sword, and my purse, and my law." He smiled. "The Three Hands. Morrow, and Quell, and Valtor. I liked them, you know. They were very useful. Very afraid. That's the best kind of servant."

"They put me in the Keep," said Edric. "Fourteen years. For a letter I never read."

"I know," said Aurelian. "I've read the file. Valtor's Sun Warrant. Edric Vane, master of the *Gull's Promise*, of Saltmere. Treason, the details of which are a danger to the Throne. Natural life." He shook his head gently. "Poor Valtor. He was always so frightened of his father." He looked up. "Is that what this is? Revenge? For the Keep?"

"It was," said Edric.

"And now?"

Edric looked past him, at the seven chains of gold light coming up out of the well, taut and burning and shuddering. *Boom.*

"Now I'm going to break those," he said.

---

The King laughed.

It was a beautiful laugh, warm and golden and genuinely delighted, and it went round the great round room of glass like music. Cassia Vell, by the shaft, did not move. Her face did not change. But Edric, who could feel the water in her, felt something go through her at the sound of it: a shiver, very deep down, like a bell struck far away.

"Oh," said Aurelian, wiping his eyes. "Oh, I'm sorry. Forgive me. It's only that they all say that. All eleven. Every one. *I'm going to break the chains.*" He shook his head. "You can't, you know. I made them. They're the strongest working of sunfire there has ever been. I made them when I was young, and terrified, and I put everything I had into them, and I've been feeding them for three hundred years. Nothing in the world can break them. Not a Tidewarden. Not a hundred Tidewardens. Not the Deep itself."

"Faro said," said Edric, "that the Deep's own strength could. If a Warden took all of it, and carried it to the Lock."

The King stopped smiling.

He looked at Edric for a long moment, with his golden eyes, and for the first time, there was something in them that was not interest, or amusement, or boredom. Something very old, and very cold, and very careful.

"Faro said a great many things," said Aurelian quietly. "He was a scholar. He read old books." He leaned back in his chair. "Do you know what would happen, if you did that? If you took all of it? Everything I've been holding down there for three hundred years?"

Edric did not answer.

"You'd die," said the King. "That's the first thing. No mortal body can hold that much. It would burn you out like a candle in a furnace. But before you died, for a little while, a day perhaps, an hour, you'd *be* it. The Deep. All of it. Three hundred years of rage, in a human body, standing at the Lock. And then the chains would break." He leaned forward. "And do you know what the Deep would do, the moment it was free?"

"Tell me."

"It would drown the world," said Aurelian, Eternal. "Every coast. Every harbour. Every city by the sea. Solace, and Saltmere, and your little island in the west, and Port Jasmine, and every fishing village from here to the end of the earth. It would rise up out of the dark like a hand, and come in over the land, and take back everything it's lost. Every life I've drunk. Every year. With interest." His golden eyes held Edric's. "You've felt it. In the storm. You've heard it talking to you. *Drown them all, the guilty and the innocent.* Haven't you?"

Edric said nothing.

"That's what I'm holding down," said the King softly. "That's what the chains are for. Not just my life. Everyone's. I'm not a tyrant, Edric Vane. I'm a *dam*. I've been standing between the land and the sea for three hundred years, holding back a flood that would kill millions, and the price of it is that I drink, and I live, and the sea gets a little sicker every year. And yes, the fish are thinner, and the blight comes ashore, and children die of the blight-cough. I know. I'm sorry. But it's a better world than the one where the sea is free. Believe me. I was there, at the beginning. I saw what it could do."

---

Edric stood in the great round room at the top of the Sunspire and looked at the beautiful golden man in the white chair, and did not know what to say.

It was a good argument. It was the best argument he had ever heard. He could feel the truth in it, in the King's voice, the way he felt the truth in any voice now: no ripples. No lie. The King believed every word.

And he had heard the Deep. In the storm, in the stillroom. *Drown them all.* He had felt what it wanted.

"Join me," said Aurelian.

Edric looked at him.

"I mean it," said the King. "Truly. I've been alone up here for three hundred years. I'm so tired of it. And you're the first person in all that time who could understand. Who could feel what I feel." He gestured at the well, the chains, the light. "There's enough for two. More than enough. You could drink too. You'd never age. You'd never die. You'd have Solace, and Valemont, and whatever else you liked. Morrow's army. Quell's Treasury. Valtor's courts. Your island. Anything." His golden eyes were very gentle. "And you'd never be lonely again. Neither of us would."

Edric looked at the white cup on the little table. At the light coming up out of the well. At the chains, shuddering. *Boom.*

He thought about an old man in a cell, raising a single drop of water, and weeping.

"No," he said.

---

The King sighed.

He did not seem angry. He seemed, if anything, a little sad, the way a man is sad when a guest leaves a party too early.

"No," he repeated. "No, I didn't really think you would. None of them ever did." He picked up his white cup, and looked at it, and set it down again, without drinking. "You know, you're weaker than you were. Since last night. Since you gave it back. I can feel it. You came up the shaft in my light and it nearly took the skin off you. A month ago, you'd have walked through it." He shook his head. "That was foolish, Edric. Very noble, and very foolish. You had the strength of a god, and you threw half of it into the sea because you felt guilty about a child."

"Yes," said Edric.

"Well." Aurelian folded his hands. "I won't kill you. Not here. Not in my house. It's discourteous, and besides, I've enjoyed our talk. You may go back down the shaft, and walk out of the Sunspire, and go home. You have until dawn to leave Solace. After dawn, the High Radiant will find you, wherever you are, and bring you back here, and I'll send you somewhere quiet. Somewhere you can listen to the sea." He smiled. "I'm told there's an empty cell on the Teeth. Two, actually."

Edric did not move.

"And your island," said the King, conversationally. "Cristal, isn't it? In the far west, past the Reach. I'd never heard of it until a month ago. You were shining so brightly, in the storm, that I could see right through you. All the way back to where you came from. The harbour. The terraces. The citadel on the hill. The library." His golden eyes rested on Edric's face. "Four thousand people, I think? Smugglers, and runaways, and Weir-folk, and Sardhi refugees. And the last Tidewarden books in the world. Quite a nest."

"Leave them alone," said Edric. His voice came out very quiet.

"Oh, I'm afraid I can't," said the King. "A city of people who listen to the sea? With a library full of Tidecraft? With a tide-taken Warden at its head? No, no. That's exactly the kind of thing I'm here to prevent." He picked up his cup again. "I sent the fleet this morning. Twelve ships of the Radiant Order, with sunfire mounted on every deck. They sailed at dawn, from the harbour below the cliffs, before you'd even got out of bed. With a fair wind, they'll be at your island in ten days." He dipped the cup in the light, and lifted it, and smiled at Edric over the rim. "I'm afraid you won't."

He drank.

Three hundred miles away, at the bottom of the world, something vast flinched in the dark.

---

Cassia Vell took him down the shaft.

She did not speak. She stood in the column of burning gold light beside him as it lowered them, a thousand feet, through the heart of the Sunspire, and watched him, with her pale eyes, as the sunfire scalded him and the Tidemark flared against it and he set his teeth and did not cry out.

At the bottom, she laid her hand on the stone, and the stone remembered it was a door, and swung open on the bright noon. She did not step aside. She stood in the doorway, blocking it, and looked at him.

"What are you?" she said.

He looked at her. He was shaking so hard from the light that he could barely stand.

"A Tidewarden," he said. "The last one. Tide-taken."

"The Tidewardens were sorcerers," said Cassia Vell. "They worshipped a sea-demon. They drowned children. The Order purged them, in the Drying, three hundred years ago, by the Sun's command. Every Radiant learns it. I've taught it, myself, to a hundred novices." She did not move. "Is it true?"

He looked at her plain, square, weathered face, and her pale eyes, and felt the water in her. Still, and deep, and very steady, like a well in a stone courtyard. And at the bottom of the well, something stirring. A doubt. Small, and old, and very cold. As if it had been lying down there for a long time, and was only now beginning to move.

"No," he said. "It's not true. The Tidewardens kept the balance between the land and the sea. For a thousand years. And then a prince of your Order, who was afraid of dying, chained the sea and drank it, and burned every Tidewarden in the world so that no one would ever be able to set it free. And then he wrote the histories." He held her eyes. "He's sitting at the top of that tower now, drinking, while the fish die and the blight comes ashore and children drown in their beds from the blight-cough. You heard him. You heard every word."

Cassia Vell stood very still in the doorway.

"I heard him," she said at last, very quietly. "I heard him laugh." She looked up, at the tower, at the white stone going up and up into the sky, at the gold light at its summit. "Twenty-two years. Six hours a day, on my knees, to the Sun." She looked back at him. "You have until dawn, Warden. Then I'll come for you. I'll have no choice."

And she stepped aside.

---

He did not wait until dawn. He went down through the city to the harbour below the cliffs at a run, with the noon sun on his back like a hand pressing him into the earth, and Bastien met him at the foot of the steps with a face like stone, and said, "The fleet sailed at dawn. Twelve ships. West. We saw them go."

"I know," said Edric. "Is everyone aboard?"

"Everyone. Hayda. The Moren boy. The crew." Bastien hesitated. "And the old man."

Edric stopped.

"Severin Valtor," said Bastien. "Lyse's grandfather. His people brought him down to the quay an hour ago, in his chair. With a note. In Lyse's hand, from before. She must have written it for him, with the board, and left it in his room." He took a paper from his coat. "It says: *If anything happens, take me to the sea. I want to see it free before I die.*"

Edric looked down the quay. At the end of it, at the foot of the gangway of a long, lean, black-hulled ship with a single curling wave painted on her bow and the name *Faro* on her stern, an old man sat in a wheeled chair, with a rug over his knees and a servant behind him, and his ruined face turned towards the sea, and his dark eyes open.

They turned, as Edric came down the quay. They found him. And the old man blinked. Once.

*Yes.*

---

The *Faro* cleared the harbour of Solace an hour after noon, under every stitch of canvas she could carry, and turned west.

Edric stood at her stern, beside the wheel, with the *Faro*'s captain, a lean grey Sardhi called Rashid, at the helm. He looked back once, at the white city on its cliffs, and the Sunspire rising above it, and the gold light at its summit, burning. He felt it watching him. He did not look again.

"Ten days," said Rashid, at the wheel. "The fleet's ten days to Cristal, if the wind holds. They've seven hours on us. We're faster than them, but not that much faster. Twelve days for us, if we're lucky. Eleven if the gods love us."

"Ten days," said Edric. "Not eleven. Not twelve. Ten."

Rashid looked at him. "My lord, the wind—"

"Leave the wind to me."

He went forward, to the bow, alone. He stood there with the spray coming over him, and the whole of the western sea in front of him, grey and endless, and closed his eyes, and breathed.

He was weak. The King was right; he had felt it, coming down the shaft, and he felt it now. Half of what he had been. Less. The silt of five years, gone into the sea under the Pale Cliffs, and with it, half his strength. He reached for the sea, and it came, as it always came: eager, cold, waiting. But slower. Fainter. Like a voice across a wider stretch of water.

*Ten days,* he thought. *The fleet will reach Cristal in ten days. And on the eleventh, Lyse wakes or never wakes.*

He reached down into the sea beneath the *Faro*'s keel, and took hold of it, the way he had taken hold of it on the night of the squall, and *pushed*.

The ship leapt.

He felt the current gather beneath her, behind her, like a team of horses taking up the slack. He felt the swell lift her stern and drive her forward. He felt the wind, the wind he had told Rashid to leave to him, swing round and come in hard from the east, straight up her wake, filling every sail. And the *Faro* began to run: faster than any ship had a right to run, faster than the wind, with her bow throwing up great white wings of spray and her wake boiling out behind her like a mill-race, west, across the grey sea, towards Cristal.

The cold came into him. The salt. He held it. He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight.

Behind him, on the deck, he heard Rashid swear, softly, in High Sardhi, and then begin, very quietly, to pray.

He did not turn round. He stood at the bow with his eyes closed and his hands on the rail and the sea pouring through him, cold, cold, colder than anything, and drove the ship west through the afternoon, and into the night, and did not stop.
