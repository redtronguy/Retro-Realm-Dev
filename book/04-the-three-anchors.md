## Chapter 4: The Three Anchors

Dorran Quell did not drink, as a rule. Drink made men careless, and carelessness was the one sin Dorran had never been able to forgive in himself or anyone else. But he had come to the Three Anchors that night because he could not stand to be alone in his narrow rented room on Chandler's Row with his ledgers and his thoughts, and because a man sitting in a tavern with a mug in front of him is invisible in a way that a man sitting in a tavern without one is not. He had bought a mug of small beer two hours ago. It was still three-quarters full.

He had spent the two hours thinking about the pepper.

He had gone over it again and again, the way he went over a column of figures that would not balance. The aft hold. The casks of sand. The boy coming up the ladder with his thoughtful face. The six weeks of silence since. The handshake on the quay.

There were only two possibilities. Either Edric Vane had seen what was in the casks and understood it, or he had seen it and not understood it. If he had not understood it, there was nothing to fear. If he had understood it, then he had said nothing for six weeks for one of two reasons. Either he was a soft-hearted fool who would never say anything, out of some sailor's notion of loyalty to his shipmates, or he was saving it. Saving it for the right moment. For the moment when he was captain, and could go to Moren with the ledgers and say, *Master Moren, there's something you should know about your purser*, and have Dorran Quell dismissed, and disgraced, and perhaps worse, and put some friend of his own in Dorran's place.

Dorran did not believe in soft-hearted fools. In his experience, there was no such thing. There were only people who had not yet found the right moment.

So: the boy would be captain. The boy would go over the voyage accounts, as any new captain must. The boy would find, or already had found, the gaps. Eight years of careful work, of never once being wrong, of building himself up from a clerk's stool in the back of Moren's office, would end. Dorran would be thrown out into the street with no reference, and no reference in Saltmere meant no work anywhere on the coast.

Unless the boy were not captain.

Unless something happened to the boy.

He had got that far, two hours ago, and stopped, and had been sitting with it ever since, the way a man sits with a stone in his shoe and does not take it out. Dorran was not a violent man. He had never struck anyone in his life. The idea of hiring some dockside thug to knock the boy on the head and throw him in the harbour made him feel slightly sick, not from conscience exactly, but because it was so crude, so obviously the kind of thing that went wrong, that left traces, that got the man who paid for it hanged.

No. If something were to happen to Edric Vane, it would have to be something clean. Something the boy brought on himself. Something nobody could ever trace.

And then the door had opened, and the soldier had come in.

---

Dorran knew him by sight. Everyone on the quays knew Fenrick Morrow by sight: the Weir boy who'd gone for a soldier, who came home on leave and drank and brooded and picked fights with sailors twice his age. And everyone on the quays knew why. It was the kind of story harbour people loved: the girl, the cousin, the sailor. Dorran had heard it a dozen times without ever caring.

He cared now.

He watched the soldier come in, and stand in the doorway with that look on his face, the look of a man who has just walked away from something he cannot bear to look at, and Dorran smiled very slightly and raised a hand.

"Corporal Morrow, isn't it?"

Fenrick looked at him blankly. "Who's asking?"

"Dorran Quell. Purser on the *Gull's Promise*. We've never been introduced, but I know your cousin's intended rather well. I sailed with him eight months." Dorran nudged the empty chair across from him with his foot. "Sit, if you like. You look like a man who could do with a drink, and I look like a man who's bad at drinking alone."

Fenrick hesitated. Then he sat, heavily, and Dorran raised a hand to the potboy and ordered a jug of the black rum the sailors drank, which he knew from long observation went down easily and came up mean.

"You sailed with him," said Fenrick, when the rum had come and he had swallowed the first cup without seeming to taste it. "With Vane."

"Eight months."

"What's he like?"

Dorran considered. "Lucky," he said. "Very, very lucky. You know the kind."

"I know the kind." Fenrick poured himself another. "Some men are born with the sea at their backs. Everything goes their way. They don't even know it. They think it's because they're good."

"He thinks he's good," said Dorran. "I'll grant you that. He thinks he's the best man on the coast. You should hear him talk about the girl. About your cousin." He let that sit a moment. "About what he'll do with her, once they're married. Take her up the hill. Make a lady of her. Teach her to talk properly, he said once. Get the marsh out of her, he said."

It was a lie. Edric had never said any such thing; in eight months at sea, he had never said one word about Mira Solen that was not soft and foolish and adoring. But Dorran had been watching the soldier's face, and he had a clerk's eye for a weak seam, and he knew where to put the knife.

Fenrick's hand closed on the cup until his knuckles cracked.

"Get the marsh out of her," he said.

"His words. Not mine." Dorran shrugged. "I'm sure he didn't mean it unkindly."

"I'll kill him," said Fenrick, quite calmly, to the table.

"I'm sure you'd like to," said Dorran. "But I'd advise against it. You'd hang, and then where would she be? And besides," he added, refilling the soldier's cup, "there are better ways."

Fenrick looked up.

"What ways?"

"I don't know," said Dorran. "Yet. I'm only saying that a man who's as lucky as Edric Vane must have something he's hiding. Lucky men always do. Something that, if the right people knew of it, might make his luck run out." He turned his own untouched mug in his fingers. "It would be a pity, wouldn't it, for a boy like that to marry your cousin, and then turn out to be something other than he seemed? It would almost be a kindness, to find it out before the wedding. To save her from it."

Fenrick stared at him for a long time, with the rum and the hate working behind his eyes.

"What are you?" he said at last. "What do you want out of this?"

"Me?" said Dorran. "I'm the ship's purser. I want what every purser wants. For the accounts to balance."

---

They were still sitting there an hour later, and the jug was almost empty, and Fenrick was drunk enough to have stopped talking and Dorran was sober enough to have stopped wondering whether this would come to anything, when Cade Rusk came in from the night and saw them.

Cade knew Dorran from the quays, distantly, as he knew everyone, and he knew Fenrick from the Weir, distantly, as he knew everyone, and he knew that the black rum in the jug on their table was paid for and that neither of them looked like a man who would grudge a neighbour a cup. So he came over.

"Gentlemen! Gentlemen! A fine night, eh? A fine night for Saltmere! Have you heard the news? A new captain on the *Gull*! Young Edric! My neighbour! I've known him since he was this high!"

"We've heard," said Dorran.

"A great day," said Cade, sitting down without being asked. "A great day for the Vanes." He eyed the jug. "I just came from them, you know. From the old man's rooms. Right above mine, they are. Such a feast! Ham, and cheese, and two kinds of bread, and wine. You'd think the boy was a lord already."

Dorran poured him a cup of rum, because it was the fastest way to make Cade Rusk talk, and the fastest way to make him stop.

Cade drank it, and smacked his lips, and leaned in.

"I'll tell you something, though," he said, lowering his voice to what he believed was a whisper. "I'll tell you something, between ourselves. That boy's going places, and not all of them the places you'd think."

"What do you mean?" said Dorran.

"I mean," said Cade, delighted to be asked, "that our young captain's got business in Solace. Private business. A message, from the old captain, Halloran. Halloran gave it him when he was dying, and made him swear not to open it, and he's to carry it to Solace after the wedding and give it to some old judge in the capital. I heard him tell his father. Through the floor." He tapped the side of his nose. "And the old man said, I heard him, he said: *careful, son. That kind of letter gets men hanged.*"

There was a silence at the table.

Fenrick had lifted his head. Dorran had gone perfectly, perfectly still.

"Hanged," Dorran repeated softly.

"That's what he said. The old man. Talk about the Ebb, he said, and the King, and the Drying. Talk gets men hanged, he said, and the men who carry their letters too." Cade looked from one face to the other, and something in them seemed to penetrate even his rum-soaked skull, because his eyes went suddenly uneasy. "Here, I don't mean anything by it. I'm only saying. It's only gossip. I shouldn't have said."

"No," said Dorran. "No, you're quite right, neighbour Rusk. You shouldn't have said." He poured Cade another cup, a very full one. "But you have, and it's said. Drink up."

---

It took him a quarter of an hour to do it, and he did it beautifully. Afterwards, in the long years when he was Baron Quell and then Lord Treasurer of Valemont, with a house of white marble on the Street of Lanterns and forty servants and a seat in the Chamber of Lords, Dorran would sometimes take the memory out and look at it, the way another man might look at a medal, and admire the craftsmanship. It had been his masterpiece. It had been the moment he learned what he was.

He called for pen and ink and paper; the potboy brought them, because sailors were always writing letters home in the Anchors, or having them written. He laid the paper on the table, and took up the pen, and then, smiling, as if it were a parlour game, he passed the pen into his left hand.

"What are you doing?" said Fenrick.

"Playing," said Dorran. "Only playing. Let's suppose. Just suppose. That a loyal subject of the King, a man who loves his country, hears that a certain young sea-captain is carrying letters for the rebels. What would that loyal subject do? Why, he'd write to the Crown Magistrate, of course. But he'd be frightened, wouldn't he? Frightened of revenge. Of the rebels. He wouldn't want his name known. So he'd write with his wrong hand, so no one would know his writing." The pen moved across the paper, slow and blotted and childish, in a hand that looked nothing at all like the neat clerk's copperplate in which Dorran Quell had kept Moren's books for eight years. "Like so."

He wrote:

> *To the Crown Magistrate at Saltmere, from a friend to the Throne.*
>
> *Know that one Edric Vane, master of the merchant ship* Gull's Promise*, now in port, is an agent of the traitors called the Ebb. He carries letters from the rebel Halloran, now dead, to a conspirator in Solace. The proof will be found in his sea-chest, or upon his person, or in his father's rooms on Ropewalk Lane. Act swiftly, for he means to leave for the capital within days.*
>
> *Long live the Sun King, who is Eternal.*

He blew on the ink, and turned the paper round so that the other two could read it.

Cade read it, moving his lips, and went pale under his flush.

"Here," he said. "Here, now. That's, you can't send that. That's a hanging, that is. That's the Drowned Keep. He's a good lad. He's my neighbour."

"Send it?" said Dorran. "Who said anything about sending it? It's a game, neighbour. A joke. Just an exercise in style." And he picked up the paper, and crumpled it in his fist into a tight ball, and tossed it carelessly into the corner of the room, behind the settle, among the sawdust and the spilled beer and the bones the dogs had left. "There. Gone. You see? Nothing at all."

Cade let out a long breath, and laughed shakily, and reached for the rum.

Fenrick said nothing. He was looking at the corner of the room, at the dark place behind the settle where the ball of paper had gone.

Dorran stood up, and laid a coin on the table for the jug, and put on his hat.

"It's late," he said. "I've accounts to do in the morning. Goodnight, Corporal. Goodnight, neighbour Rusk." He looked at Fenrick, just once, and their eyes met, and something passed between them that did not need words. "I'm sure everything will turn out exactly as it should."

He went out into the night, and did not look back, and walked the long way home to Chandler's Row, along the quays where the water slapped against the stones. He walked slowly, and his hands were perfectly steady, and he found that he was humming.

He had not sent anything. He had not posted anything. He had not given anything to anyone. He had played a parlour game in a tavern, before witnesses, and thrown the result away. Whatever happened now, if anything happened at all, it would have nothing whatever to do with him.

That was the beauty of it.

---

Cade Rusk drank until the jug was empty and then drank from Fenrick's cup too, because the soldier did not seem to want it, and at some point he put his head down on the table, just for a moment, to rest his eyes.

When he woke up, it was past midnight, and the fire had gone to embers, and the potboy was stacking chairs, and the soldier was gone.

Cade sat up, blinking. His head hurt. His mouth tasted like a dog's bed. Something was bothering him, at the bottom of his mind, something he had seen or heard or said, but he could not quite get hold of it.

He looked at the corner behind the settle.

The sawdust was scuffed, as if someone had been kneeling there. The bones were where the dogs had left them. The spilled beer had dried to a sticky stain.

The crumpled ball of paper was gone.

Cade looked at the place where it had been for a long time. Then he looked at the door, and at the dark street beyond it, where Fenrick Morrow had walked out into the night with something in his pocket, towards the Crown Magistrate's house on the hill and the box beside its door where loyal subjects left their letters.

Then he looked at the empty jug, and at his own shaking hands.

*You could go after him,* said something at the bottom of his mind, very small and very faint. *You could go up the hill to the magistrate's house and say there's been a mistake, a joke, a drunken joke, it's not true. You could go up Ropewalk Lane and knock on the boy's door and warn him. You could do something. You could do anything.*

It would mean going out into the cold. It would mean saying he had been there, at the table, when it was written. It would mean that the soldier, who had a sword, and the purser, who had a look in his eyes that Cade did not like, would know that he had talked. It would mean trouble, and Cade Rusk had spent forty years of his life learning, above all things, how to stay out of trouble.

And after all, he had not done anything. He had not written anything. He had not posted anything. He had only been sitting there. And the soldier might not have taken it. Perhaps the potboy had swept it up. Perhaps the soldier had thrown it in the harbour on his way home. Perhaps it would come to nothing at all.

Cade Rusk got up, and put on his coat, and went home to bed, and lay awake until dawn listening to the young captain's father snoring in the room above, and said nothing, to anyone, ever.

It was the only thing he was ever really good at.
