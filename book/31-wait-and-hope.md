## Chapter 31: Wait, and Hope

Severin Valtor died at dawn on the day the sea turned blue.

Lyse was with him. She told Edric afterwards, on Cristal, weeks later, when he was well enough to sit up and listen. She had been sitting beside his chair in the library at the top of the citadel all that night, holding his hand, because he had not slept for three days, and she had not wanted him to be alone. Towards dawn, he had grown very restless. His eyes, which had been fixed on the window and the grey harbour all night, had begun to move, quickly, back and forth, as if he were watching something she could not see. She had thought he was trying to speak, and fetched the board. But he did not look at it.

And then, just as the sun came up over the edge of the sea, he had gone very still. And Lyse, looking where he was looking, out of the window, down at the harbour of Cristal, had seen the water change.

"It went blue," she said. "All at once. Like a lamp being lit. The whole harbour, and the sea beyond it, all the way out to the horizon. Grey, and then blue. And the dead fish, they just, they sank. All of them. As if something had taken them back. And then the gulls went up off the cliffs, every one, thousands and thousands, screaming, and went down to the water, and there were live fish in it. Silver ones. Everywhere." She looked down at her hands. "I looked at Grandfather. His eyes were open. He was looking at it. And he blinked. Once."

"Yes," said Edric.

"Yes," said Lyse. "And then he closed his eyes, and he didn't open them again." She was quiet for a while. "I think he waited. I think he made himself wait. He told you he would."

Edric looked out of the window of the library at the blue sea, and thought of a young judge, thirty-five years ago, in a house with a blue door on the Street of Lanterns, risking everything to get a Tidewarden into the archives under the Sunspire. And of a letter, carried by a dead man's honest boy, that had never reached him.

"He did," he said. "He told me. *I will wait.*"

---

He was a long time getting well.

They brought him back to Cristal in the *Faro*, from the place where the Teeth had been, across a sea so blue and so full of life that the crew kept leaning over the rail to stare at it. He did not see much of it. He lay in the captain's cabin, and slept, and woke, and slept again. The ship's surgeon said he had nothing wrong with him that anyone could find, except the burn over his heart, which was healing, and an exhaustion so deep that it frightened him. Jac said the surgeon was a fool. Jac said he had carried the whole of the sea through a door the size of a man, and put it back, and anyone who did that would want a lie-down after.

On Cristal, they put him in the library, on the bed in the corner where Lyse had lain for forty days, by the fire. He slept for a week. When he woke, it was spring.

He had not known it was spring. He had lost count of the days, for the first time since the night he put his hand on Lyse Valtor's forehead in the dark. There was nothing beside his heart to count them any more. He lay in the bed in the library and looked up at the broken dome, and saw blue sky through it, and heard gulls, and smelled something he did not recognise at first, and then did: green things. Growing things. The smell of the island in spring, which he had smelled for five springs and never, he realised, really noticed.

He reached for the sea. It was there: a whisper, a breath, the sound in a shell held to the ear. Singing. He lay and listened to it until he fell asleep again.

---

Valemont did not end. Kingdoms rarely do. It only changed.

The news reached Solace by the mirror towers on the second day after the Teeth fell: a garbled, impossible, terrified message from the tower at Grey Point, where a new keeper had seen, at dawn, the sea go out along the whole coast for two miles and come back as a tide, gentle as a breath, and lie down at the foot of the cliffs. And then another, from a fishing boat that had been near the Teeth, that the Drowned Keep was gone. And then, at noon, the light on the top of the Sunspire, which had burned day and night for three hundred years, flickered, and went out.

The Sunspire did not fall. It was too well built for that. But the door at its foot, that only a Radiant's hand could open, opened for anyone now; and the shaft of gold light that had lifted visitors a thousand feet to the great round room at the top was gone, and anyone who wanted to see the room had to climb a thousand feet of spiral stair that no one had known was there. Very few people wanted to. The ones who did said that the room was empty, and cold, and that in its centre there was a well going down into the dark, and nothing coming up out of it at all. And beside the well, a white chair. And on a small table beside the chair, a white cup, like a cup for milk, and a teapot, and two other cups, very fine and very old, that nobody had ever drunk from.

The Chamber of Lords met in emergency session for a month. They argued about everything. There were lords who wanted a new King, and lords who wanted no King at all, and lords who wanted to be King themselves; and in the end, mostly because they could not agree on anything else, they agreed to govern Valemont themselves, as a council, for a year, and see. The year became two. The two became ten. Nobody ever quite decided to make it permanent, but nobody ever quite decided to stop.

Some of the old members of the Ebb, the ones who were still alive, came out of the shadows and took seats in the Chamber, and the first thing they did was send to Cristal for copies of every book in the library, and the second was to have the histories of the Drying rewritten, in every school in Valemont, as they had really happened. It took a long time. People did not like it. But the fish were back, and the blight was gone, and the blighted things had stopped coming ashore, and the children who had been dying of the blight-cough were getting up out of their beds, all over the kingdom, and breathing. And people found, after a while, that it was hard to go on believing that the sea was a demon when the sea was giving them so much.

---

Cassia Vell lived.

Her hands did not. The King's fire had burned them past healing; the surgeons on Cristal took off what was left of them at the wrist, and she lay in the hospital on the second terrace for a month, grey-faced, saying nothing, and then got up one morning and walked down to the harbour and asked to be put to work. Jac found her something. She could not hold a tool, or a sword, or a line. But she could talk. And she could stand on the quay at the inner end of the harbour, where the Weir-folk were rebuilding their stilt-houses, and tell the eleven hundred Radiant knights who had surrendered on the Long Bank, and had been brought to Cristal in their own boats to rebuild what they had burned, what the Order had really done in the Drying, three hundred years ago. And they listened to her, because she had been their High Radiant, and because she had no hands.

She never called down sunfire again. She said she did not want to. She said the Sun had never needed anyone to burn things in its name, and that she intended to spend the rest of her life making sure that nobody ever did again. She went back to Solace the following year, and took what was left of the Radiant Order, the ones who would follow her, and made of it something new, which tended no beacons and burned nothing and taught the true histories in the schools; and when people asked her what it was called, she said it did not need a name.

She came back to Cristal every spring, after that, for the Midsummer lanterns. She stood at the back of the crowd on the quay, in a plain grey coat, with her sleeves pinned up over her empty wrists, and watched the Weir-folk float their candles out on the black water for the souls of the drowned. And on the third terrace, where a little whitewashed temple had once stood, she paid for a new one to be built. It was very ugly, and very square, and it had a golden sun painted over its door, and under the sun, a single curling wave. Nobody ever went in it, much. But every morning, somebody did, and sang. Not well. But loudly.

---

Of the others, the news came to Cristal in pieces, over the years, the way news does.

Corvan Valtor lived on in the grey house on the Street of Lanterns, with a keeper, for eleven more years. He did not know anyone. He did not speak, much. He spent his days in the little walled garden behind the house, in all weathers, digging: slowly, carefully, with a small spade, all over the garden, a hole here and a hole there, and filling them in again, and starting another. His keeper said he was looking for something. He said that once, the keeper said, quite clearly, on a wet afternoon in the autumn of the third year: *I'm looking for my sons. They're here somewhere. I put them in the dark. I have to find them before they climb out.*

Lyse went to see him once, in the spring after the Teeth fell, with Maxim. She stood in the garden in the rain, and he looked up at her from his digging, with his ruined grey face, and did not know her. She knelt down beside him in the mud, and took his dirty hand, and held it for a while. Then she got up, and went away, and did not go back. She told Edric afterwards that she had forgiven him, kneeling there in the mud. She said she did not know if he had felt it. She said it did not matter; it had not been for him.

Andric was condemned by the Great Court for the murder of Cade Rusk, and sentenced to the galleys for life. He laughed when the sentence was read, and bowed to the judges. On the way to the galleys, the prison carriage stopped at an inn in the hills to change horses, and when the guards came back out, the carriage was empty, and the chains were lying on the floor of it, unlocked, and nobody ever saw him again. There were stories, afterwards. That he was in Port Jasmine, calling himself a prince. That he was in the Sardh, running a gambling house. That he was dead in a ditch. Bastien Corso, when he heard, said nothing at all for a long time. Then he said: *I dug him up. Whatever he does, I'll carry it.* And went back to work.

Dorran Quell went south, as he had said he would, to a city on the coast of the southern empires where nobody knew his name, and took a small room above a ships' chandler's, and found work as a clerk in a counting-house, keeping someone else's books. He was very good at it. He never rose. He did not try. Every month, for the rest of his life, a small sum of money arrived at the hospital in Saltmere, by a ship from the south, with no name attached. Not a large sum. A clerk's wage, very nearly all of it. The hospital used it for medicine. With it, every month, there was a single line, in a neat, small, careful hand, that the matron of the hospital puzzled over for years and never understood: *To the account.*

Elsabet Quell played the harp in the great concert halls of Port Jasmine, and never married, and was very happy.

And Pieter Moren came to Cristal.

---

He came in the summer, in the *Gull's Promise*, with his daughter Julie and her husband and their three children, for Maxim's wedding. He was seventy now, and walked with a stick, and his square honest face had grown very lined. He came up the processional stair slowly, with Julie on one side and Maxim on the other, stopping on every terrace to rest, and look at the harbour, and shake his head.

Edric was waiting for him at the top, in the Court of Tides, under the tree.

He had thought about how to do it. He had thought about wearing the face of the Count of Cristal one last time, or the foreign banker, Master Wilde, or no face at all. But he could not wear faces any more; there was not enough of the sea left in him for Still Water. And in the end, he did not want to. He stood under the tree in the Court of Tides in a plain coat, with his own face, and the white scar through his eyebrow, and the grey at his temples, and waited for the old man to come.

Pieter Moren came up the last step of the stair, and into the court, leaning on his son, and stopped.

He looked at the man under the tree. He looked for a long time, with his head on one side, the way he had looked at Master Wilde once, in an office on Harbour Street with a cracked window, like a man trying to see something at a great distance in bad light.

Then his face changed.

"Edric," said Pieter Moren.

He let go of his son's arm. He let go of his daughter's. He dropped his stick on the stones of the Court of Tides, and walked across it without it, slowly, unsteadily, an old man, and Edric came to meet him, and caught him before he fell.

"Edric," said Pieter Moren, into his shoulder. "Edric. Edric, my boy. My captain." He was weeping. "I knew. I always knew. The card. *The Tide remembers.* I always knew it was you. They told me you were dead. They told me you died in the Keep. And I said no. I said, he's not dead, he's out there, somewhere. I said, that boy came home through the Elbow against the tide without a pilot at nineteen, and he'll come home again." He held Edric's face in his two old hands and looked at it. "And you did."

"I did," said Edric. His voice was not working. "I'm sorry I was late."

The old man laughed, through his tears, and clapped him on the shoulder, the way he had clapped him on the shoulder on the quay at Saltmere twenty years before.

"I owe you a bottle," said Pieter Moren. "The green Halvar. From the wager about the whale. Halloran never collected. I've been keeping it for twenty years. I thought you might want it."

---

Maxim Moren married Lyse Valtor on Cristal at midsummer, in the Court of Tides, under the tree, with the whole island watching from the terraces. Lyse wore white, and her mother's pearls, which her grandfather had left her. Maxim wore his Guard captain's white and gold, which he had resigned the month before, and was the last time he would ever wear it; he had taken a post as master of the harbour on Cristal, under Jac. The council of the twelve terraces stood witness. Tam, the schoolmaster, read the vows, in the common tongue and in the Weir-tongue, from one of the old books in the library: a Tidewarden wedding, the first in three hundred years.

And afterwards, when the feasting was done and the sun had gone down, the Weir-folk took the couple down to the harbour, to the inner end of the hook where the new stilt-houses stood in the shallow water, and put them in a small boat garlanded with sea-lavender and white shells, a boat with no oars. And everybody stood on the walkways and the quay, in the dark, with lamps, and waited.

The tide was coming in. It came up the harbour, silver under the moon, and lifted the little boat at its rope. And Old Bettan, the Weir-folk grandmother, untied the rope, and let go.

The boat sat at the landing for a long moment. Nobody breathed.

Then, very gently, it began to move. Out. On the tide. Away from the landing, out into the harbour, turning slowly, with the two of them in it holding hands, out towards the harbour mouth and the open sea under the moon, where the sea was singing.

And the whole of Cristal cheered.

Edric stood at the back of the crowd on the quay, and watched it go, and found that he was laughing.

---

He went to Saltmere in the autumn, at the spring tide of the full moon, the luckiest tide of the year.

He went alone, in a skiff, with a small sail. He did not need the sea to carry him; he could not have asked it to. He sailed it himself, the way Halloran had taught him, reading the wind and the water and the sky, and it took him four days, and he enjoyed every one of them.

He came into Saltmere harbour at dusk on the fourth day, through the narrows against the tide, without a pilot. The harbour was full of ships now: fishing boats, more than he had ever seen there, riding low in the water with their catches, and gulls screaming over them, and the quays crowded with people unloading silver fish into baskets, laughing. Nobody looked at him. He was only a thin, grey, tired man in a small boat, and they did not see him.

He tied up at the end of the long quay, where a ship's owner had once stood with his coat buttoned wrong and his hat in his hands, waiting for the *Gull's Promise*. And he walked through the town, up the hill, past the end of Ropewalk Lane, where a tall crooked house still stood with a window at the top looking out over the harbour, and he looked up at it, and nodded to it, the way you nod to an old friend. And then down the hill again, to the Weir.

---

She was on the Solens' landing stage.

Not the end of the walkway, where Old Ysolde had sat. The landing stage of the house where she had grown up, where her mother had coughed through the winters in the damp, where she had sat with her feet in the water mending a net on the night he came home from the south, twenty years ago, and made him wait. The strangers who had lived in it had gone west, to Cristal. She had bought it back in the spring, with what Aren earned on the boats. It was very small, and very old, and it leaned.

There was a boat tied to the landing stage. A small boat, with no oars, old, its paint long gone, its timbers grey. But garlanded. With sea-lavender, fresh, purple, and white shells.

She was sitting beside it, on the edge of the landing stage, with her feet in the water.

She had heard his step on the walkway a hundred yards away, as Weir-folk always did. She did not look up.

"You're late," said Mira.

"I sailed myself," said Edric. "It took four days."

"Four days." She shook her head. "I remember when you could do it in one."

He sat down beside her on the edge of the landing stage. He took off his boots, and put his feet in the water. It was cold, and clear, and full of small silver fish that came and nosed at his ankles, curious, and darted away.

They sat for a while without speaking, the way the Weir-folk sit: side by side, with their feet in the water, watching the tide come in.

"That's the boat," he said at last.

"That's the boat." She did not look at it. "Gran kept it. Under the house, on the mud, all these years. Wrapped in sailcloth. I found it when I came back in the spring." She was quiet a moment. "I had it painted. And then I took the paint off again. It looked wrong, painted. Like it was pretending."

"And the garland?"

"I made that this morning." She turned her head, at last, and looked at him: her dark green eyes, the colour of deep water in shade, and her freckles, brown and clear now across her nose, from a summer on the boats with her son. "It's the spring tide. The luckiest wedding tide of the year."

---

The tide came up the channels of the Weir, silver under the full moon, higher than it had come in years.

It filled the reed-beds and lifted the boats at their moorings and lapped at the very edges of the walkways. It came all the way up to the Solens' landing stage, and lifted the little grey boat with no oars, garlanded with sea-lavender and white shells, so that it rocked gently at its rope.

They sat beside it, and watched it, and did not move.

"It would take us out," said Mira. "Now. I think. If we got in. I think it would." She was looking at the boat. "I can hear it. The sea. Every night, on the end of the walkway. It's not crying any more. It's singing." She smiled a little. "Gran always said it would agree, one day. If we asked it nicely."

"I think it would," said Edric.

They sat, and looked at the boat, and the tide rose, and reached its height, and hung there, silver and still under the moon.

"I loved you all my life," said Mira, quite quietly, to the water. "Since the Midsummer lanterns, when I was fifteen and you came down the walkway with a candle in your hand and fell over Old Bettan's net. I loved you every day you were in the Keep and I didn't know it. I loved you every night I sat here with my feet in the water and watched the light on the Teeth. I loved you the day I married Fenrick in grey, and every day after, for eighteen years, and I never told a living soul." She turned her head. "And I'll love you for the rest of my life. I want you to know that. I want you to know it before you go."

He looked at her for a long time. At the girl from the Weir, thirty-nine years old, with her feet in the water and the moon on her face and the freckles coming back.

"I know," he said. "I've always known. I knew it in the Keep, in the dark, when I couldn't see your face any more. I knew it at the dinner, when you looked across the room and saw me. I knew it on the ledge, in the temple, when you put the crown on the water and sang." He took her hand. It was rough with net-cord, and cold, and it fitted his exactly. "And I loved you every day, Mira. Even the days I couldn't remember how."

She held his hand very tightly.

"But we're not nineteen," she said.

"No."

"And you're going."

"Yes," said Edric. "West. Past the Reach. Past Cristal. There's a lot of sea out there I've never seen. I want to see it. Now I can only hear it a little. Now it's not screaming." He looked out at the dark water beyond the Weir, where the channels opened to the sea. "And Cristal doesn't need me any more. It's got its council, and Jac, and Tam, and Maxim and Lyse. And the books. And the children."

"The children?"

"There are children on Cristal who can hear it now," said Edric. "Weir-folk children. Sardhi children. Three of them, last month, and two more since. They hear it the way I used to. The way Faro did. They can raise a drop of water off a stone, and it doesn't hurt them, because the sea's not chained any more, and it's not screaming." He smiled. "Tam's teaching them to read first. Then arithmetic. Then history. Faro would approve."

"And you won't stay. To teach them."

"I've nothing left to teach them," said Edric. "Not about the sea. It's all in the books." He was quiet a moment. "And I think I'd like to find out who I am, now. When I'm not angry. When I'm not the Warden, or the Count, or going to Solace. Hayda asked me that once, a long time ago. I didn't know the answer." He looked at the boat. "I think I'd like to find out."

Mira looked at him for a long time.

Then she let go of his hand, and reached out, and untied the rope that held the little grey boat with no oars to the landing stage.

And let go.

---

The boat sat at the landing for a long moment, on the high silver tide, rocking gently, with its garland of sea-lavender and white shells.

Then, very slowly, it began to move.

Not in towards them. Out. Away from the landing stage. Out into the channel, turning slowly, on the tide, the way a leaf turns on a stream. Out past the stilt-houses, past the walkways, past the reed-beds, out towards the mouth of the Weir and the open sea under the moon. Empty. Carrying nothing at all. Only the garland, and the moonlight, and twenty years.

The sea had agreed. Not to take them out. To take the boat.

They sat on the landing stage, side by side, with their feet in the water, and watched it go, until it was a dark speck on the silver sea, and then until it was nothing at all.

Mira was weeping, quietly, without a sound. So was he.

"There," she said at last, wiping her face with the back of her hand, the way she used to when she was nineteen and had been crying and did not want anyone to know. "There. That's done. Gran would be pleased. She always said the sea knows things."

"It does," said Edric.

She leaned her head on his shoulder, and they sat there together on the Solens' landing stage in the Weir until the tide had turned and gone out, and the moon had set, and the sky in the east was going grey. And then she got up, and kissed him once, on the forehead, the way the Weir-folk sealed a vow, with her fingers wet from the sea. And went into her house, and closed the door.

He did not look back, walking along the walkway in the dawn. He did not need to. He had her in his heart, behind no scar at all.

---

He sailed from Cristal on the first day of the following spring, in the *Faro*, with a crew of twelve, and Rashid at the helm, and Hayda.

She had not asked to come. She had simply been there, on the morning he told Jac he was going: standing on the quay at the inner end of the harbour with a sea-chest at her feet and her chin up, in plain Sardhi travelling clothes, with her black hair braided down her back and Bastien's long knife at her belt. When he looked at her, she looked back, with her desert-well eyes, and did not say anything at all.

"You don't have to," he said.

"I know."

"You could stay. You could have anything you want on Cristal. A house. A ship. A seat on the council. Tam says you're the best student he's ever had. You could—"

"I have until the spring," said Hayda bint Tariq. "I told you, on the deck of the *Slip*, in Mazrah. I would stay until the spring." She picked up her sea-chest. "It is the spring. I have decided to stay longer." She walked past him up the gangway of the *Faro*, and stopped at the top, and turned. "Besides. Somebody has to make sure you laugh."

And she went below, and did not come up again until they had cleared the harbour.

Jac Teague stood on the end of the quay, one-armed, with her red hair blowing, and watched them go. She did not wave. She had said goodbye the night before, in the harbour tower, with the last of the good brandy, and had told him, at length and in detail, exactly what she thought of men who ran off to see the world when there was perfectly good work to be done at home, and had wept, and hit him on the arm with her good fist, and told him to come back or she would come and find him and drag him home by the collar.

Bastien Corso stood beside her, with his black brows and his face like a closed door, and did not wave either. But he raised one hand, once, as the *Faro* came abreast of the new harbour tower at the mouth of the hook, where a light burned at the top: a plain oil light, nothing more, that anyone could tend.

And behind them, on the terraces, the people of Cristal stood and watched. Four thousand of them, and more now. Smugglers, and runaways, and Weir-folk, and Sardhi, and Radiant knights in plain grey coats who had come to burn the island and stayed to rebuild it. Children, three of them, five, seven, who could hear the sea. And on the third terrace, outside a very ugly, very square little temple with a sun and a wave painted over its door, somebody singing. Not well. But loudly.

---

He wrote a letter before he went, and left it with Jac, to give to Maxim and Lyse on the day of their first child's naming. It was not long. Lyse kept it all her life, in a box of cedar wood, with her grandfather's board, and her mother's pearls, and a card that had once stood on the table by an old man's bed in Saltmere, that said *The Tide remembers*. And when her own children were grown, she gave it to them, and they to theirs, and it is said that it is still on Cristal today, in the library at the top of the citadel, under the broken dome, where anyone can read it.

It said:

> *My dear Maxim, my dear Lyse,*
>
> *I am going west, and I do not know when I will come back, or if. Don't grieve for it. I have been in the dark a long time, and I find that I would like, for a while, to be in the light, and to see what the sea looks like now that it is singing.*
>
> *I have done a great many things in my life that I am not proud of. Some of them you know. Some of them, I hope, you never will. I set out to be the hand of justice, and I found, too late, that I could not tell where justice ended and the sea began. A child is dead because of it, and an old man's servant, and an old lord and his wife. I will carry them. I would not have you think I don't.*
>
> *But I have learned one thing, and it is the only thing I have to leave you that is worth more than the money, so I will set it down.*
>
> *There is no such thing as a man who is only what he has done. There is only the sea, and what is underneath it. What is on the surface changes, every day, every hour, with every wind. A man can be a storm one day and a calm the next. He can drown his enemies or lift them. He can take, and take, until there is nothing left of him but the taking; or he can give it back. And what is underneath is always there, if he goes deep enough to find it. My teacher told me that, in a cell under the sea, a long time ago. I did not understand him. I think I do now.*
>
> *Live, then. Be happy. Love each other, and your children, and the island, and the sea. And when the dark comes, as it will, as it comes to everyone, remember what an old man told me, at the end, when he could barely speak: that all the wisdom of the Tidewardens, a thousand years of it, every book in that library, comes down in the end to two words.*
>
> *Wait, and hope.*
>
> *Your friend,*
>
> *Edric Vane*

---

The *Faro* cleared the harbour mouth of Cristal on the morning tide, and turned west, into the open sea.

He stood at her bow, alone, with his hands on the rail and the spray coming over him, and looked out at the blue water stretching away to the edge of the world. It was very bright. The sun was warm on his face, and it did not hurt at all.

He closed his eyes, and breathed, in for eight, out for eight, and listened.

Very faint. Very far. A whisper. A breath. The sound in a shell held to the ear. But there.

The sea was singing.

And somewhere in it, so deep that it might have been the bottom of the world, something vast and old and free turned, slowly, in the light that came down now through clear water, and looked up at a small ship running west under full sail, and at the man standing in her bow. And remembered.

Behind him, he heard a step on the deck, light and quick, and a voice, low and clear, in High Sardhi, saying something about the gull circling over the mainmast and its probable intentions towards the bread in her hand.

And Edric Vane laughed.

---

*THE END*
