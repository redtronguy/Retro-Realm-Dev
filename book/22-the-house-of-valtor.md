## Chapter 22: The House of Valtor

Corvan Valtor came to call on the Count of Cristal on a hot afternoon in early summer, unannounced, on foot, alone.

The High Justicar of Valemont did not make calls. Everyone knew it. He did not dine out; he did not go to balls; he did not attend the theatre or the races or the endless receptions of the court. He went from his house on the Street of Lanterns to the Hall of Justice under the Sunspire every morning at six and came home every night at ten, and in between he sat in judgement on the kingdom, and in thirty years nobody had ever seen him laugh. People said that the King himself was a little afraid of him. People said a good many things about Corvan Valtor, and most of them were said in whispers.

So when Bastien came into the study at the back of the house with the blue door and said, in his flat, silent way, with his black eyes burning, "Valtor. At the gate. Asking for you," Edric sat very still at his desk for a long moment before he answered.

"Show him into the green hall," he said. "Give me a minute."

When Bastien had gone, he took the bowl of still water from the corner of the desk and looked into it, and breathed, and found the stillness, and held it until his face in the water blurred and softened and became the face of the Count of Cristal: grave, foreign, unreadable. Then he went down.

---

Valtor was standing in the middle of the hall of green glass when he came in, with his hands clasped behind his back, looking up at the walls.

He was forty-seven. He had grown thin, and grey, and very upright, like a heron. He wore the plain black robe of the High Justicar, without ornament, except for a heavy gold chain of office with a sun at its centre. His face had become more still than ever, and more careful, the face of a man who had spent thirty years making sure that nothing he felt ever reached it; but the stillness had begun to set, the way plaster sets, into lines. There were deep grooves from his nose to the corners of his mouth. There was a vertical crease between his brows that did not go away when he relaxed them, because he never did.

He turned when Edric came in, and looked at him. A long, slow, measuring look, the look Edric remembered from a warm study in Saltmere, by a fire, nineteen years ago. *Do you know why you're here?*

Edric held the stillness. He held it the way you hold a rope in a gale.

"My lord High Justicar," he said, and bowed. "You honour my house."

"Your house," said Valtor. His voice had not changed at all: clear, even, precise, every word placed like a stone. "Yes. That's why I've come, my lord Count. Forgive the intrusion. I was born in this house."

Edric said nothing.

"I grew up here," said Valtor. He was looking at the walls of green glass again. "Until I was nineteen. My father lived here until he was taken ill, twelve years ago. It's been empty since; I couldn't bring myself to sell it, and I couldn't bring myself to live in it. And then the agents told me a foreigner had offered three times its value, in gold, sight unseen, and I found I couldn't bring myself to refuse." He turned. "I wanted to see what you'd done to it."

"And what do you think?"

"I think," said Valtor slowly, "that you have made it very beautiful. And very strange. This hall was my father's library. Where you have the fountain, in the courtyard, there was a herb garden; my mother grew rue and rosemary there. My bedroom was at the back of the house, overlooking the sea. I'm told you use it as a study." He paused. "I used to sit at the window there and watch the ships come in. I used to wonder where they'd been."

"It's a fine view," said Edric.

"Yes." Valtor's eyes came back to his face and rested there. "Have we met, my lord Count?"

Edric did not move. Under his collar, the Tidemark was cold as ice. He felt the stillness in his face like a sheet of glass, and behind it, something cracking.

"I don't believe so," he said. "I've lived most of my life in the south."

"No," said Valtor. "No, of course. Forgive me. It's only that you remind me of someone. I can't think who." He was quiet a moment, still looking. Then something moved in his face, very slightly, like a man shaking off a thought. "It's the house, I expect. Too many ghosts in it." He inclined his head. "Thank you for indulging an old man's curiosity. I'll leave you in peace."

"Stay," said Edric. "Please. Take a glass of wine with me, in your father's library."

Valtor hesitated. Then he sat.

---

They talked for an hour, by the fountain in the hall of green glass, with the water dancing.

They talked about the law. Valtor did not talk about anything else; he did not seem to know how. But he talked about it well, with a cold, precise, terrible clarity that Edric had not expected, and that he recognised, with a sort of horror, because it was so like Faro's.

"Justice," said Valtor, "is the King's will made orderly. That's all it is. That's all it can be. The King is eternal; the law is how his eternity reaches into the lives of men who are not. A judge is not there to find the truth. Truth is a private matter, between a man and the Sun. A judge is there to keep the order. To make sure that what the King wills is done, the same way, every time, for everyone. That's what justice is. Order. Without it, there is only the sea."

"The sea," said Edric.

"Chaos," said Valtor. "The old word for it. The Drowned Heresy believed that the sea was a god, you know, and that it had its own justice, older than the King's. Wild justice. A storm that falls on the good and the wicked alike." His mouth moved, very slightly, in what in another man might have been a smile. "Fortunately, the King put an end to all that."

"Fortunately," said Edric.

He held his glass very still. In it, the wine was beginning to tremble.

"And you, my lord Count?" said Valtor. "What do you think justice is? You've travelled. You must have seen a good many kinds."

Edric looked at him for a long moment, at the thin grey upright man in his black robe, sitting where his father's library had been, in the house where he had been born, the house to which a dead captain had once sent a letter, nineteen years ago, that a nineteen-year-old boy had carried and never read.

"I think justice is like the sea," he said. "It takes its time. Sometimes a very long time. But it comes in, in the end. To everyone." He set his glass down on the edge of the fountain. "And it doesn't care how high they've built their walls."

Valtor looked at him without expression.

"A Sardhi saying?"

"Something like that."

"Hm." The High Justicar rose, and smoothed his robe. "You must come and dine with us, my lord Count. My wife would never forgive me if I didn't ask you. She's heard you know something of the natural philosophy of the south; she has an interest in it. And my father would like to meet the man who lives in his house. He can't speak, you understand. The palsy took his voice, twelve years ago. But he hears everything." He paused at the door. "Everything."

---

The house of the High Justicar was across the street, at the other end of the Street of Lanterns: a tall, grim, grey house behind a high grey wall, with a lantern over the gate that seemed to give less light than the others. Edric went to dine there a week later.

He met the family.

He met Heloise Valtor, the High Justicar's second wife: a small, pretty, plump, fair-haired woman of thirty-five, with a soft voice and soft hands and quick, bright, pale-blue eyes that went everywhere and missed nothing. She was very charming. She asked him a great many questions about his travels, and listened to the answers with her head on one side, like a bird.

He met Pip, her son: a fat, fair, spoilt, solemn little boy of nine, who sat at the table and stared at him with his mother's pale eyes and asked him whether it was true that he had killed a sea-monster with his bare hands, and when Edric said that it was, said, "Show me," and was sent to bed.

He met Lyse, the High Justicar's daughter by his first wife, the great lady Celestine Aubray, who had died when Lyse was five. Lyse was nineteen. She was tall and slender and quiet, with her dead mother's dark hair and her grandfather's grave dark eyes, and she sat at the end of the table and said almost nothing all evening. But once, when her stepmother made a small sharp joke at her expense, about her being too thin and too pale to catch a husband, Edric saw something go across Lyse's face: not hurt, not anger, but a kind of patient, practised endurance, like a sailor bracing for a wave he has seen coming. And he looked at Heloise Valtor's pale bright eyes, watching her stepdaughter, and felt the currents in her, and went cold.

He felt them all evening. He could not help it. Undertow came to him now without his reaching for it, like a sense he could not shut off, and in that grey house, at that grey table, the currents were very strong.

He felt Valtor: cold, controlled, rigid, like water frozen solid, with something very old and very dark trapped under the ice. He felt Lyse: still, and deep, and frightened, and determined, all at once. He felt the boy: a shallow pool of wanting, nothing more.

And he felt Heloise. He felt her watching her stepdaughter. He felt her watching her husband, and the old man upstairs, and the great grey house, and the fortune that went with it, which would all go to Lyse when Valtor died, because it had been Lyse's mother's, and very little of it would go to Pip. He felt the want in her, cold and hard and patient, pulling, pulling, like an undertow. Pulling it all towards the fat solemn boy upstairs.

---

After dinner, she took him to see her collection.

It was in a small room at the top of the house, which she called her stillroom: a room full of glass jars and copper stills and bunches of dried herbs hanging from the beams, and a smell of alcohol and bitterness and something sweet underneath. She had an interest, she said, in the natural philosophy of plants. In medicines. In the distillation of essences. A lady had to have some occupation, and she had never cared for embroidery.

"And I'm told, my lord Count, that you know a great deal about such things. The medicines of the south. The Sardhi physicians are said to be the best in the world."

"They are very good," said Edric.

"Is it true," said Heloise Valtor, picking up a small glass phial and holding it to the lamp, so that the liquid in it glowed amber, "that they have drugs that bring sleep? Deep sleep? For those who suffer from pain?"

"It's true."

"And others," she said lightly, "that bring a sleep that does not end?"

He looked at her. She was smiling, her soft pretty smile, with her head on one side. The pale bright eyes were very steady.

He could have said no. He could have laughed, and changed the subject, and gone home, and never come to this house again.

He felt the currents in her. He felt what she wanted. He thought of Lyse at the end of the table, bracing for the wave. He thought of Valtor, in a warm study, holding a letter in the fire until the flame reached his fingertips. *I've done you a kindness.*

"There's a thing the Sardhi call *blackwater*," he said. "It comes from the sea, not from plants. From a creature, a kind of urchin, that lives in the deep reefs where the blight is worst. It's very rare. If you distil it, you get a clear liquid, with no taste and no smell. A few drops, in a glass of wine, will do nothing at all. But a few drops every day, for a month, will fill the lungs with water, slowly, the way the blight-cough does. A physician would say it was the blight-cough. There's no way to tell the difference."

Heloise Valtor had gone very still. The phial in her hand had stopped turning.

"How interesting," she said softly. "And is there no cure?"

"Only one. If a man takes a very small dose, every day, for years, his body learns it. Then it can't hurt him. The old Sardhi kings used to do it, for fear of their courts." He shrugged. "Otherwise, no. None."

"How very interesting," said Heloise Valtor, and set the phial down on the shelf, very carefully, among the others. "You must tell me more, another time, my lord Count. Shall we go down? My husband will think I've stolen you."

---

He went home that night and sat in his study, in Valtor's old bedroom, at the window where Valtor had once watched the ships come in, and did not light a lamp.

He told himself that he had done nothing. That she had asked, and he had answered, and what she did with the answer was her own. That a woman like that would have found it somewhere else, in a book, from an apothecary. That it was her sin, not his.

He told himself that, over and over, in the dark. And Faro, who had always had something to say about everything, said nothing at all.

---

He went back to the grey house a few days later, in the afternoon, when the High Justicar was at the Hall of Justice and his wife was out paying calls, and asked to see the old man.

Severin Valtor sat in a chair by the window of a room at the top of the house, in the sun, with a rug over his knees. He was eighty-one. He had been a big man once; Edric could see it in the frame of him, the breadth of the shoulders, the size of the hands lying useless on the rug. The palsy had taken everything else. He could not move his arms or his legs. He could not turn his head. He could not speak. His face was a ruin, slack and fallen, like a house whose roof has gone in.

But his eyes were alive. They were very dark, and very bright, and they turned towards the door as Edric came in, and fixed on him, and did not leave his face.

There was a servant with him: an old, bent, white-haired man called Noll, who had served him for fifty years and spoke for him. Noll had a board, a square of wood painted with the letters of the alphabet, and he would hold it up and run his finger along the rows, and when he came to the right letter, the old man would blink, once. In this way, slowly, letter by letter, Severin Valtor could still say anything he wished. He did not often wish to.

"Leave us, Noll," said Edric. "Please. I'll call you if he needs anything."

The old servant looked at his master. The old man blinked, once. Noll went out.

Edric sat down in the chair across from the old man. He did not hold the stillness. He let it go, entirely, and let his face be his own, and let the old man look at it.

"My lord Severin," he said. "Do you know who I am?"

The dark eyes looked at him for a long time. Then, slowly, they moved: away from his face, down, to the collar of his coat. To the silver pin he wore there, always, in the shape of a single curling wave.

And then back to his face. And then, very slowly, the old man blinked. Twice.

*No.*

"Nineteen years ago," said Edric, "a sea captain called Halloran died of a fever on the Ember Coast. Before he died, he gave a packet to a young man. A sailor. Nineteen years old. And he asked him to carry it to Solace, to a judge called Severin, in a house with a blue door, on the Street of Lanterns. And to tell him: *the tide is turning.*"

The old man's eyes went wide.

"The young man never reached Solace," said Edric. "He was arrested on the night of his wedding. On a false charge, made in an anonymous letter. And the packet was taken from him and opened, by a magistrate in Saltmere. And the magistrate read the letter, and saw who it was for, and burned it. And then he sent the young man to the Drowned Keep, under a Sun Warrant, without a trial. To make sure nobody would ever know that the letter existed."

The old man's eyes were full of tears. They spilled over, and ran down his ruined face, and he could not lift a hand to wipe them.

"I was that young man," said Edric. "I was in the Keep for fourteen years. And the letter I carried, the one your son burned, said that the last Tidewarden was alive, in the Keep, on the Teeth. And he was. He was in the next cell to mine. He was alive for thirteen years after they put me there. His name was Faro." He leaned forward. "He told me about you. He told me you were brave. He told me you got him into the archives under the Sunspire, thirty-five years ago, and risked everything to do it. He told me you had a son. A little boy. Very solemn, very quiet. Always watching."

The old man was weeping openly now, without a sound, his whole body shaking under the rug.

Edric took his hand, his big, useless, heavy hand, and held it.

"He's dead now," he said. "Faro. He died in the Keep, six years ago. But he taught me everything he knew, first. And then the sea took me, as it took the Tidewardens in the old days." He opened his collar, and let the old man see the Tidemark on his throat, glowing faintly in the afternoon light. "*The tide is turning*, my lord Severin. Nineteen years late. But it's turning."

---

It took them an hour, with the board, after he had called Noll back in. Letter by letter. The old man's eyes blinking, the old servant's finger moving along the rows, and Edric watching and saying each word aloud as it came.

*W-H-A-T. D-O. Y-O-U. W-A-N-T.*

"Justice," said Edric.

A long pause. Then: *M-Y. S-O-N.*

"Yes."

The dark eyes held his for a long time. Edric could not read what was in them. Grief. Fury. Shame. And something else, under all of it, that he did not understand until the old man began to blink again.

*H-E. W-A-S. A. G-O-O-D. B-O-Y.*

*I. M-A-D-E. H-I-M. A-F-R-A-I-D.*

*I. K-N-O-W. W-H-A-T. H-E. I-S.*

*D-O. W-H-A-T. Y-O-U. M-U-S-T.*

Edric sat with that for a long moment. Then he said: "And you? What do you want, my lord?"

The old man's eyes went past him, to the door. To the house beyond it. And his eyes changed. They went hard and bright and fierce, as bright as Faro's had ever been, and he blinked, fast, almost too fast for Noll's finger to follow.

*L-Y-S-E.*

*S-H-E. I-S. I-N. D-A-N-G-E-R.*

*T-H-E. W-O-M-A-N.*

*P-R-O-T-E-C-T. H-E-R.*

Edric felt the cold go all the way down to his heart.

"Yes," he said. "Yes. I promise."

The old man looked at him for a long moment. Then he blinked once, slowly, and closed his eyes.

On the way out, on the stairs, Edric passed Lyse Valtor coming up with a tray: a cup of broth, and a glass of wine, for her grandfather. She smiled at him shyly, and stood aside to let him pass. He stood aside too, and they did a small awkward dance on the stairs, and she laughed, a soft, surprised laugh, as if she had not done it in a long time.

He looked at the glass of wine on the tray. He felt the water in it. Clean. Quite clean.

*For now,* he thought.

---

He met Maxim Moren the following week, at a reception at the palace of the Lord Marshal, to which he had been invited, and from which Mira, he was told, was absent with a headache.

He knew him at once. He would have known him anywhere. He was twenty-four now, a captain in the King's Guard, tall and broad and fair, in his white and gold, with his father's square honest face and his father's careful grey eyes; and the last time Edric had seen him, he had been a solemn little boy of five, sitting on Pieter Moren's knee on the quay at Saltmere, watching the *Gull's Promise* come in.

Maxim did not know him, of course. But he knew the name.

"Cristal," he said, when they were introduced, and something changed in his face. "My lord, forgive me. Are you the same Count of Cristal who has a bank in Port Jasmine? Thornbury and Wilde?"

"I have an interest in it."

The young man's face went red. "Then I owe you more than I can say," he said, in a rush. "My father, sir. Pieter Moren, of Saltmere. Five years ago, your house held his debts. And he was ruined, and you gave him three months, and then on the last day—" He stopped, and swallowed. "Someone paid them. All of them. And sent my sister a diamond for her wedding. And a ship came into the harbour, a new ship, built to my father's own lines, with his old ship's name on her. Nobody ever found out who. There was only a card." He looked at Edric with his honest grey eyes. "It said: *The Tide remembers.*"

Edric said nothing.

"I've always thought," said Maxim slowly, "that your house must know. Thornbury and Wilde. Who paid them. Who it was."

"I'm afraid I can't help you," said the Count of Cristal. "My agents handle such things. I don't know the details."

"No," said Maxim. "No. Of course not." He looked at the floor. "My father thinks it was a young man he once knew. A captain of his. Who died a long time ago, in prison. He says it was the young man's ghost, come back to pay a debt. He's old, and he's not very well, and he says strange things sometimes. But he says that." He looked up. "He keeps the card on the table by his bed. He reads it every night before he sleeps. *The Tide remembers.* He says it's the only prayer he needs."

Edric looked at the young man for a long moment. Then he turned away, and looked out of the window at the gardens of the Marshal's palace, and did not say anything for so long that Maxim began to look uncomfortable.

"Forgive me," said the Count of Cristal at last, in a slightly different voice. "Tell me, Captain Moren. Do you know the Valtor family?"

Maxim went red again, all the way up to his fair hair.

"I. Yes. A little. I know Mistress Lyse." He cleared his throat. "That is. A little."

Edric turned and looked at him, and for the first time since he had come to Solace, the Count of Cristal very nearly smiled.

---

He gave a dinner at Larkmere at the end of the summer.

He had bought the house through Bastien, in the spring, before he ever came to Solace: a pretty, old, rambling country house an hour's ride from the city, with a walled garden, which had changed hands three times in twenty years and never been lived in for long. People said it was unlucky. People said a child cried in the garden at night.

He invited eight. Baron and Baroness Quell. The High Justicar and Madame Valtor. Count and Countess Morrow, who sent their regrets; the Countess was unwell. The young secretary from the Ministry of the Interior with the sharp tongue. And a newcomer to Solace, a young lord from the far south whom the Count had taken under his wing: Lord Andric Cavell.

He had found Andric in the spring, through Bastien, in a gambling den in the lowest quarter of Solace, near the docks, where a handsome young man with a southern accent had been fleecing merchants' sons at cards for a month. Bastien had stayed outside. Bastien had not gone in. Bastien had stood in the alley in the dark, with his back against the wall, and his face like stone, and his hands shaking.

Edric had gone in. And sat down at the young man's table. And lost to him, steadily, for an hour, while he watched him.

He was beautiful. That was the first thing anyone noticed about Andric, and Bastien had said so, and Bastien had been right. Twenty-three years old, tall and slim and graceful, with dark hair that curled and dark eyes that laughed, and a smile like the sun coming out. He had his father's height, and his father's stillness, and his mother's fine bones, though Edric did not know yet who his mother was. He was charming. He was clever. He was funny. Everyone at the table liked him, even the men he was robbing.

And there was nothing there.

Edric had reached for him, with Undertow, the way he reached for everyone now without trying. And found nothing. No currents. No wants, no fears, no shame, no love. Not even greed, really; greed was a current, a pull, a hunger. There was only a kind of flat, smooth, empty surface, like a mirror, reflecting back whatever was in front of it. It was like reaching into a well and finding it dry all the way down.

He had taken Andric to a private room afterwards and made him an offer. A new name: Lord Andric Cavell, heir of an old and very rich southern house, whose fortune was held in trust in Port Jasmine until his twenty-fifth birthday. A new set of clothes. An allowance of fifty thousand marks a year, paid through Thornbury and Wilde. An introduction to the best society in Solace. And all that was asked in return was that he play the part, perfectly, and never ask why.

Andric had laughed, delighted, and said yes at once. He thought it was a swindle; Edric could see him thinking it. He thought the Count was running some great confidence game on the nobility of Solace, and that he, Andric, was to be the bait, and would get his share. He did not care what the game was. It did not occur to him to care.

He had been in society for three months now. He was a great success. Baron Quell, in particular, had taken a great liking to him: to his charm, his manners, his ancient name, and above all, to the fortune held in trust in Port Jasmine, which would be his in two years. Baron Quell had a daughter, Elsabet, nineteen, who played the harp and wanted nothing to do with marriage. Baron Quell had begun to drop hints.

---

The dinner was in the long dining room at Larkmere, with the windows open to the garden and the summer night. Afterwards, when it was dark, the Count took his guests out into the garden with lanterns, to walk among the roses.

It was a pretty garden, old and overgrown, with high walls of red brick and gravel paths and a great spreading chestnut tree at the far end, in the corner, with a bench beneath it. The Count led them down the path towards the tree, talking easily, about the history of the house, about the previous owners, about his plans.

"I've had workmen in," he said. "Clearing. The garden had gone quite wild. I'm going to put in a fountain, here, by the tree. In fact," he added, stopping beneath the chestnut, with the lantern-light falling on the bench, and the roots, and the earth around them, "they found something here, last month, when they were digging the foundations. Something rather strange."

Behind him, he felt Valtor stop walking.

He did not turn. He did not need to. He felt the water in the High Justicar's body go suddenly still, every drop of it, all at once, as if it had frozen.

"They found a box," said the Count of Cristal, conversationally. "Just here. Under the roots of the tree. Quite a small box, of oak, about so long." He held his hands apart: the length of a newborn child. "Very old. Twenty years in the ground, perhaps more. They brought it to me, because they thought it might be treasure. We opened it together."

There was a silence in the garden. Somewhere, a nightingale was singing.

"It was empty," said the Count. "Quite empty. Except for a piece of linen, very old, folded in the bottom. Stained." He looked at the earth under the chestnut tree. "My steward, who was there, said it looked like old blood. He said it was the kind of box, in his country, that they bury children in." He paused. "It's very strange. It was as if someone had buried a child here, a long time ago, in the dark. And the child had got up, and climbed out of the box, and walked away."

Baroness Quell fainted.

She went down quite suddenly, without a sound, onto the gravel path, a tall, handsome, grey-haired woman in dark red silk, and lay there with her face white as paper in the lantern-light. Her husband knelt beside her, bewildered, calling her name. The secretary from the Ministry ran for water. Madame Valtor gave a little cry.

Edric did not look at any of them. He was looking at Valtor.

The High Justicar was standing on the gravel path a few yards away, quite still, with his lantern held at his side. His face had gone the colour of ash. He was not looking at his wife, or at Baroness Quell on the ground. He was looking at the earth under the chestnut tree. And Edric, with the sea in him, could feel the blood in his body hammering, hammering, like a fist on a door, and under it something very old and very dark, something that had been frozen under the ice for twenty-three years, beginning to crack.

*So,* Edric thought, very calmly. *It was her. The mistress. Hermione Quell.*

He felt, for a moment, the whole shape of it, like a chart unrolled: the cold, bored, handsome woman, thirty years younger than her first husband, alone in Solace while old Haverel was away in the south on business. The young prosecutor, unmarried then, and hungry, and climbing. A child born too early, in secret, in a pretty house with a walled garden. A box. A spade. And the child who had climbed out of the box, and grown up, and was standing now on the gravel path behind them, beside the roses, with his lantern in his hand.

He turned his head, at last, and looked at Andric.

Lord Andric Cavell was standing a little apart from the others, by the roses. He was not looking at the fainting woman, or at the High Justicar, or at the tree. He was looking at the Count of Cristal. He had been watching him all along, Edric realised, with his laughing dark eyes. And he was smiling: a small, interested, curious smile, the smile of a clever boy watching a conjuror do a trick, and trying to work out how it was done.

He did not know. Edric was quite sure of it. He did not know who his mother was, or his father, or what the box had been, or why the woman had fainted. He only knew that something was happening that he did not understand, and that it might be useful to him. And he was enjoying it.

The nightingale sang on in the dark.

---

Long after midnight, when the guests had all gone home in their carriages, and the house at Larkmere was dark, and the servants were asleep, a single figure came back into the garden through the gate in the wall.

It walked down the gravel path without a lantern, slowly, like a man walking in his sleep. It stopped under the great chestnut tree. And after a long time, it knelt down, in its black robe, on the earth beneath the tree, and put its hands flat on the ground, the way a man puts his hands on a grave.

It stayed there until the sky began to grey.

Edric watched it from an upper window, in the dark, with his hand flat on the cold glass. He did not feel sweetness. He did not feel anything at all. Only the cold, spreading through him from the Tidemark over his heart, like ice spreading across a pond in the dead of winter, until there was no water left under it.
