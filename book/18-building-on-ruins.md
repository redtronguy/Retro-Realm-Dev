## Chapter 18: Building on Ruins

Afterwards, people would tell the story of Cristal as if it had happened all at once: as if a man had sailed out of the west one spring with a ship full of gold, and raised his hand, and a city had risen out of the ruins behind him like a wave. It was not like that. It was five years of mud and stone and sweat and argument, and most of it was very dull, and a good deal of it went wrong. But it was the happiest he had been since he was nineteen, and for long stretches of it, whole weeks, he forgot to be anyone but the man who was building it.

---

They started with the harbour, because without a harbour nothing else was possible.

Jac took the *Slip* back to Corrow with a hold full of gold and came back two months later with three more ships, forty men, a master mason from the north who had been run out of his guild for drunkenness and had stopped drinking the day he saw the quays of Cristal, and a great many tools. They cleared the weed and the rubble from the old stone quays and found them sound underneath, as sound as the day they were laid. They rebuilt the fallen tower at the harbour mouth, and put a light in it, a plain oil light, not a beacon of sunfire like the ones on the mainland. They dredged the channel. They built new warehouses on the old foundations behind the quays, and put roofs on them, and by the end of the first summer, ships were coming in.

Smugglers came first, as Faro had known they would. The Reach was full of them, and every one of them had spent his life running from the King's customs cutters, with nowhere to put in for water or repairs except places like Corrow, where a man was as likely to have his throat cut as his hull caulked. Word went round the Reach that there was a harbour on Cristal now, a safe one, with a light and a quay and a chandler and no customs house, where nobody asked where your cargo came from. That the cursed island was cursed no longer. That a man called the Warden ran it, and that the Warden's word was law, and that the Warden had only three laws: *pay your dues, keep the peace, and tell no one in Valemont the way in.*

Then came the others. The ones nobody wanted.

Runaways from the King's galleys and the King's mines. Debtors who had fled their debts. Families from the coast whose fishing had failed and whose landlords had turned them out. Sardhi refugees from the wars in the east, who had nowhere to go and had heard that the Warden did not care what god you prayed to. Old soldiers with no pensions. Young women with no husbands and children who needed feeding. Thieves who wanted to stop being thieves, and a few who did not, and who were taken back to Corrow by Jac on the next ship and put ashore with a warning.

And the Weir-folk.

They came in the second year. A single family first, in a fishing boat, the Bettans, whom Faro remembered: the old woman who had spat in the water when he brought the *Gull's Promise* through the Elbow, now very old indeed, with her sons and their wives and a swarm of grandchildren. They had come, the old woman said, because the fishing in the Weir had failed completely now, and the town on the hill was talking about draining the marsh to build on it, and because Old Ysolde, before she died, had told them to. *Go west,* the old woman had said, on her last night, sitting at the end of the walkway listening to the sea. *There's a place in the west where the water's awake. Go there.*

The Bettans built stilt-houses on the shore of the harbour, at the inner end of the hook, where the water was shallow and a stream came down from the hills. Within a year there were twenty Weir-folk families there. Within three, a hundred. They brought their nets, and their boats, and their language, and their lamps, and on the first Midsummer night, they floated candles out on the harbour water to light the way for the souls of the drowned, and the whole of Cristal came down to the quays to watch, and stood in silence while the little lights drifted out on the black water towards the sea.

Faro stood at the back, in the shadows, and watched too, and did not let anyone see his face.

---

By the third year, Cristal had four thousand people.

They had rebuilt the lower terraces, the houses closest to the harbour, and were working their way up the processional stair. There was a market in the first square, every day, under awnings of sailcloth, where you could buy fish and bread and Sardhi coffee and northern brandy and southern silk and almost anything else in the world, at a third of the price you would pay in Saltmere. There was a school, in what had once been a Tidewarden hall on the second terrace, where Tam, of all people, now a lanky young man of nineteen with a great deal of dignity, taught the children of smugglers and fishermen to read. There was a hospital. There was a council, of a sort: twelve men and women chosen by the people of each terrace, who met every month in the old Court of Tides under the tree, and argued, and made decisions, and brought the ones they could not agree on to the Warden.

And there was, on the third terrace, a small, square, very ugly temple of whitewashed stone with a golden sun painted over the door, which Big Olaf had built with his own hands, stone by stone, as a thank-offering to the Sun for his good fortune. It was always empty except for Olaf, who went there every morning to sing, very loudly and very badly. Nobody had the heart to tell him.

"You know it wasn't the Sun," Faro said to him once, gently, standing in the doorway.

"I know that, Warden," said Big Olaf, surprisingly. "I'm not stupid. It was you, and the sea." He went on polishing the brass sun over the altar. "But the Sun's had a hard time of it, I reckon, being used by that lot in Solace for three hundred years. Somebody ought to be nice to it."

---

And there were the nights.

They had started in the first winter. A fisherman's boy, mending nets on the shore below the Weir-folk's stilt-houses, had come running up the beach in the dark screaming that something had come out of the water. By the time Faro got there, with a lantern and a cutlass and half the harbour behind him, the thing was gone, and so was the boy's father.

They found him in the morning, what was left of him, on the rocks at the harbour mouth.

The thing came back three nights later. This time Faro was waiting.

It came out of the sea at the inner end of the harbour, at the turn of the tide, in the dark of the moon: a long, low, glistening shape, dragging itself up the shingle on too many legs. It was the size of a horse. It looked like a crab, partly, and partly like an eel, and partly like nothing he had a name for: a mass of grey-white, blotched, swollen flesh, armoured in plates of shell that did not fit together properly, with a long thrashing tail and a mouth in the middle of its underside, full of teeth. Its eyes were on stalks, and they were filmed over, white and blind, like the eyes of a fish that has been dead a week. And it smelled. It smelled of rot, and salt, and sickness.

He felt it before he saw it. He felt it coming up through the water like a fever coming up through a body. And when he reached out to it with the sea, he felt what it was. It was sick. It was sick the way the whole sea was sick: blighted, poisoned, wrong. It had been a crab once, an ordinary crab, the kind that scuttled in the rock pools. And the sickness in the sea had got into it, and twisted it, and swollen it, and driven it mad with pain and hunger, until it had crawled up out of the sea in the dark looking for something, anything, to make the pain stop.

He tried to calm it. He reached for the water in it, the way he would have reached for a sick man's fever, and tried to still it. It screamed. It turned on him, and came at him across the shingle, and he had to kill it.

He used Riptide. He had not used it on a living thing before. He pulled the water out of the shingle and the sea and the air into a single blade, a thin hard edge of current like the one that had cut the rope under the Teeth, and drove it through the thing's armour, and it died.

And he stood on the beach in the dark, with the thing dead at his feet and the people of Cristal staring at him from the top of the shore with their lanterns, and the cold in him was very deep, and the taste of salt was very strong. And he felt, for a moment, something else. Something underneath the cold, rising. Something that had enjoyed it.

---

They came every few weeks, after that. The blighted things. Never in daylight. Always at night, always at the turn of the tide, always in the dark of the moon. Crabs and eels and sharks and things that had once been seals, and once, in the second winter, something that had once been a whale, which beached itself at the harbour mouth and lay there screaming for a night and a day until he went down and ended it.

"They come ashore all along the coast," said Jac. "The mainland too. Have done for years. Worse every year, the fishermen say. But the mainland's got the beacons." The King's sunfire beacons: towers along the coast of Valemont, every few miles, each with a Radiant priest at the top tending a light that burned all night, a light like a little piece of the noon sun. "The blighted things won't go near them. Sunfire burns them. So they come ashore in the dark places. The places the King doesn't care about."

"Places like Cristal," said Faro.

"Places like Cristal."

So they built walls. Low stone walls along the shore, around the harbour and the Weir-folk's houses, with gates in them and watchtowers at the corners. They lit lamps along the walls every night, oil lamps, which did nothing to the blighted things except let you see them coming. They trained a watch: forty men and women from the terraces, with spears and nets and fire, who walked the walls from dusk to dawn. Jac commanded them. She was very good at it.

And when the things came, they rang the bell in the harbour tower, and Faro came down.

He learned to fight them. He learned that he could not calm them, not once the sickness had got too far into them; that the only mercy was to end it quickly. He learned to feel them coming from miles out, and to be waiting on the shore when they came. He learned to use Riptide the way a swordsman uses a sword: cleanly, coldly, without wasted motion. And he learned, slowly, to keep the other thing down, the thing that rose in him when he fought, that came up out of the cold like a shark out of deep water. The thing that enjoyed it.

It got harder to keep down. Every time he fought, the cold was deeper. Every time, the salt was stronger. Every time, he came back from the shore with more of the sea in him, and less of something else, something he could not name but could feel going, like warmth going out of a room.

He stopped laughing, somewhere in the second year. He did not notice it. Jac noticed. She did not say anything. But she started finding reasons to keep him up late, in the harbour tower, drinking her brandy and listening to her stories about her father and the old days in the Reach, and every so often, when she told one that was very bad, or very rude, she would look at him sideways to see if his mouth moved.

It rarely did.

---

He read the books.

Every night, after the work was done, after the walls were walked and the council had argued and the blighted things had or had not come, he went up to the citadel on the summit, to a room he had made his own in the shell of the old library, where he had brought the twelve stone chests up from the sea-cave and unpacked them onto new shelves of new wood. He sat at a table under a lamp, with the wind coming through the broken dome above, and read.

*On the Three Disciplines.* *On the Nature of the Deep.* *On the Keeping of the Balance.* *The Book of the Masters of the House of Cristal.* *The Chronicle of the Order, from Its Founding.* *On the Signs of the Sea's Sickness, and Their Cure.* Hundreds of them. Some were dull: lists of tides, records of harvests, accounts of disputes between Wardens in cities that no longer existed. Some were extraordinary. Some he read once and put away and did not open again.

He learned what he was.

The books called it *tide-taken*. It had happened, they said, perhaps a dozen times in a thousand years: a Warden, usually in some great extremity, a storm or a shipwreck or a battle, who reached for the Deep and was answered. Not with a trickle, as Tidecraft usually was, but with a flood. The Deep would come into them and mark them, and they would become, for a time, a kind of channel: a place where the god's power could flow into the world. Their strength would be terrible. They would be strong in all three disciplines, where other Wardens were strong in one. And it would not last.

*For the vessel is mortal,* one of the books said, *and the sea is not. And what is poured into a vessel without end must, in the end, either break it or become it.*

He looked up the dozen. He read what had become of them. Two had died within a year, burned out. Four had lived long lives, but had gone strange, cold, distant, until they walked into the sea one day and did not come back. Three had become the greatest Wardens in the history of the order, and saved cities, and turned back storms that would have drowned a coast. And three had become something the books did not like to name.

*It looked out of her eyes,* one of the books said, of the last of those three, *and there was nothing human left in them. And the storm she raised drowned the city of Ys and everyone in it, the guilty with the innocent, the Warden's enemies with the Warden's children. And when it was done she walked into the sea, laughing.*

He closed that book very carefully, and put it back on the shelf, and sat for a long time with his hands flat on the table.

He was strong in all three. He knew that now. Riptide came to him as easily as breathing; he had been using it since the night of the squall. Still Water, he had learned for the disguise of the priest, and since then he had practised it until he could wear a face for days at a time, a face that was not his, so that men who had known him for a year would pass him in the street. And Undertow, which he had barely touched, he found when he finally reached for it was the strongest of all.

He reached for it one night in the library, alone, with the lamp turned low, and felt the currents.

Not the sea's currents. The others. The ones Faro had talked about. He felt the flow of people through Cristal, the market and the harbour and the terraces, the movement of want and need and fear from house to house like water through a network of channels. He felt it spread outward, beyond the island, across the sea, to Corrow, to the Reach, to the coast of Valemont: the ships coming and going, the cargoes, the money. Money most of all. He could feel it. It moved through the world like a tide, gathering in some places, draining out of others, pooling, swirling, rushing down channels that had been cut for it by laws and banks and kings. And at the centre of it, in the east, in a city on a cliff, there was a great whirlpool, a vast slow spiral into which money poured from every corner of the kingdom and from which it flowed out again in streams that went to war, and to palaces, and to a few great houses, and to the Sunspire.

He felt the houses. He felt one of them, very clearly, pulling at the current like a drain: a house in which money moved faster and colder and more cleverly than anywhere else in Valemont. He could not see it. But he knew whose it was.

He let go. He sat in the dark library, shaking, with the salt in his mouth and the cold in his heart, and for the first time since the night under the Teeth, he was frightened. Not of the men in Solace. Of himself. Of what he could do. Of how easy it would be.

---

He found Bastien Corso in the fourth year, or rather, Bastien Corso found him.

He was a smuggler captain from the north coast, a thick-set, black-browed, silent man of fifty with a scar across his throat and a face like a closed door, who brought his ship into Cristal harbour in a gale and asked to see the Warden. Faro saw him in the harbour tower. Bastien looked at him for a long time without speaking, and then said: "They say in the Reach that you're gathering people who hate the King. I don't hate the King. I hate one of his Hands. I want to know if you can help me kill him."

"Which one?"

"Valtor," said Bastien Corso. "The High Justicar."

Faro sent everyone else out of the room. And then he poured two glasses of Jac's brandy, and sat down across the table from the man, and said: "Tell me."

Bastien's story took an hour.

He had had a brother. Twenty-two years ago, in Solace, where they had both worked the docks. His brother had been a hothead, a talker, a man who said things in taverns about the King. And when a Radiant priest was knifed in an alley near the docks one night, his brother had been arrested, because somebody had to be. Corvan Valtor, newly returned from the provinces, newly married to his great lady, had been the prosecutor. Valtor had known his brother was innocent. Everybody had known; there were six witnesses who had seen him in a tavern across the city that night. Valtor had not called them. He had needed a conviction, quickly, in a case the High Justicar was watching. Bastien's brother had been hanged on the Bridge of Lamps in front of a crowd, and Valtor had been promoted.

"I swore I'd kill him," said Bastien. "In the old way, my family's way. A vendetta. I told him so, to his face, outside the court. He laughed. He had me thrown out of the city." He drank. "I waited two years. Then I came back."

Valtor had a country house in those days, at a place called Larkmere, an hour's ride outside Solace. A pretty house with a walled garden. He went there alone, sometimes, at night, without his wife, without servants. Bastien had watched it for weeks. And on a night in the spring, he had climbed the garden wall with a knife in his belt, and hidden among the trees, and waited.

"He came out of the house about midnight," said Bastien. "Alone. With a lantern, and a spade. And a box. A little box, like a child's coffin. I watched him dig a hole under the big tree at the end of the garden, and put the box in it, and fill it in. Then he stood there, looking at it." The scarred throat moved. "And I came out of the trees and put my knife in him."

"He lived."

"He lived. I didn't know it, then. I thought I'd killed him. I was sure of it. I left him lying in the garden, and I ran. But then I stopped, because I wanted to see what he'd buried. I thought it'd be money. Or papers. Something he'd want hidden." Bastien looked at the table. "I dug it up with my hands. I opened it. It was a baby."

The fire crackled in the harbour tower. Outside, the gale howled.

"A newborn. A boy. Still in his birth-blood, wrapped in a bit of linen. Somebody had put him in a box and buried him alive. And he was alive. Just. Cold as ice, and blue, and not crying, but breathing." Bastien's voice had gone very rough. "I found out later. I made it my business. Valtor had a mistress, in those days, a lady of the court, a married lady, whose husband was away at the wars. She'd had his child in secret, at Larkmere. And the child was born too early, and wasn't breathing, and the midwife said it was dead. And Valtor took it out into the garden to bury it, so no one would ever know. Only it wasn't dead." He drank. "Or maybe he knew it wasn't. I've wondered, all these years. Whether he knew."

"What did you do with it?"

"Took it home. To my brother's widow, my sister-in-law, Assunta. She'd lost her own baby the winter before, and her husband on the gallows. She took one look at him and said he was a gift from the sea." Bastien laughed, very bitterly. "She called him Andric. She raised him like her own. I helped. I sent her money, every voyage." He was quiet a long time. "He was a beautiful child. Clever. Charming. He could make anyone love him. And there was nothing behind it. Nothing at all. Like a painted door on a blank wall. He stole from her when he was eight. He set fire to a neighbour's cat when he was ten, to watch it run. When he was fifteen, he tied her to a chair to make her tell him where she kept her money, and when she wouldn't, he—" Bastien stopped.

Faro waited.

"He burned the house down," said Bastien Corso. "With her in it. And took the money, and ran. That was eight years ago. I've been looking for him ever since. I've heard things. He's been in prison. He's been a thief, a forger, a killer for hire. He's in Solace now, they say, or was. Calling himself something else." He looked up at last, with his black eyes. "That's what Valtor made, the night he buried that box. And that's what I made, the night I dug it up. I don't know which of us I hate more. But I know which one I can reach."

Faro sat with that for a long time.

"I don't need you to kill Valtor," he said at last. "But I need a steward. A man who knows Solace. A man who knows Larkmere. A man who's hated Corvan Valtor longer than I have." He held out his hand across the table. "Will you work for me, Bastien Corso? I can promise you that before the end, you'll see him fall. I can't promise you'll be the one to push him."

Bastien looked at the hand for a long time.

"Longer than you have," he said slowly. "That's a strange thing to say, Warden. As if you had a reason of your own."

"I have," said Faro.

And Bastien Corso took his hand.

---

Hayda came in the fifth year.

He had been looking for her for two. Since the night at the Dry Well, when Cade Rusk had said *something about him being in the Emir's confidence, and then the gates being opened one night, and the Emir dead*, he had been pulling on that thread, with agents and gold and Undertow, following the currents of money and rumour back through ten years and two wars to a city in the desert that no longer existed. Qasr. Emir Tariq. His wife, the Lady Vasiliki, who had died in a slave-house in Qadir. And his daughter, who had been six years old when the city fell, and had been sold, and sold again, and sold again, and was now, his agents said, seventeen, and in the household of a spice merchant in the port of Mazrah, on the Sardh coast, where she was kept as an ornament, and taught to sing, and was to be sold again in the spring to a lord in the far south who collected such things.

He went to Mazrah himself, with Jac, in the *Slip*, in the face of a lord. He bought her for a sum that made the spice merchant sit down very suddenly. And then he took her out of the merchant's house and down to the harbour and onto the *Slip*, and stood her on the deck, and said to her, in High Sardhi, the language of her father's court, which she had not heard spoken properly since she was six:

"You are free. You are no one's. You never were. Here is a purse; it's yours. Here is a paper that says you are a free woman, signed and sealed; it's yours. If you want to go somewhere, anywhere, this ship will take you. If you want to stay with me, you can. Not as a slave, or a servant, or anything else. As my ward. As a guest. For as long as you like. It's your choice. It's your choice from now on, always, everything."

She was very small, and very slight, and very straight, with a long neck and a sharp, proud, fine-boned face, and black hair down to her waist, and eyes as dark and still as a desert well. She had been dressed by the merchant in silks and jewels, and she stood on the deck of the *Slip* in all of it like a queen in a borrowed costume, and looked at him for a long, long time without saying anything at all.

Then she said, in High Sardhi, in a low clear voice: "Why?"

"Because I owe your father a debt," said Faro.

"You never knew my father."

"No," he said. "But I know the man who betrayed him."

He watched it go into her. He watched her face, so still, so proud, so careful, crack, just for a moment, like ice on a pond when a stone hits it. And through the crack, he saw something he recognised. Something he had seen in his own face, in the bowl of still water, on the night after Faro died.

"Morrow," said Hayda. "Fenrick Morrow. The pale captain. The one who came to my father's court and swore on his sword to serve him, and ate at his table, and taught me to ride. The one who opened the gates." Her voice did not rise. It went lower, and colder, and harder, until it was like a blade being drawn. "The one who killed my father in his bed, and sold my mother and me in the market at Qadir, for a hundred gold pieces each. I was there. I saw him take the money. I was six." She looked at Faro with her desert-well eyes. "You know him?"

"I know him."

"Where is he?"

"In Solace," said Faro. "He's the Lord Marshal of Valemont now. He has a palace, and a wife, and a son, and the ear of the King. And in the spring I am going to Solace, to see him."

Hayda looked at him for a long time.

"Then I'll stay," she said. "As your ward. As your guest." She took the purse, and the paper, and held them against her chest. "Until the spring."

---

She was the one who made him laugh again.

He did not expect it. She was the most serious person he had ever met: serious about everything, about her studies and her prayers and her music and her hatred, which she kept polished and bright like a dagger. She read everything in the library at Cristal, in a month, and then started again, and argued with him about it. She learned the Weir-tongue from the Bettan grandmothers and the old tongue from the books. She learned to sail from Jac, and to fight from Bastien, who taught her the knife the way his family had taught it for three hundred years. She asked questions that nobody else dared to ask, at the dinner table, in front of the council, in a clear, cool, carrying voice, and waited for the answers with her chin up.

And one night, at the end of the first winter, when he came up from the shore after a fight with a blighted thing, a big one, a shark that had come into the shallows and taken two of the watch before he could reach it, and he was so cold that his hands would not stop shaking and his lips were blue and the Tidemark was blazing on his throat above the collar of his coat, she was waiting for him in the library, by the fire.

She did not say anything. She poured him a cup of the Sardhi coffee, thick and black and scalding, and put it into his shaking hands, and sat down across the fire from him, and looked at him for a long time.

"Who are you," she said at last, "when you aren't angry?"

He looked at her. He did not understand the question.

"Jac says you used to laugh," said Hayda. "When she first knew you. On the ship. She says you used to stand at the rail and look at the sea and laugh, for no reason at all. Like a boy. Olaf says it too. Tam says it." She folded her hands in her lap. "I've been here four months, and I've never seen you laugh. Not once. I've never seen you do anything that wasn't for the work, or the city, or the fight, or the spring. I've never seen you do anything at all because you wanted to." She tilted her head. "So who are you? When you aren't the Warden? When you aren't going to Solace? When you aren't angry?"

He opened his mouth. And found that he did not know.

He sat by the fire with the coffee going cold in his hands and tried to remember. A boy on a quay, vaulting a rail, running up a hill. A boy on a landing stage with his feet in the water, laughing at a heron. A boy who had believed in endings. He could remember the facts of it. But he could not remember what it had felt like, from the inside. It was like trying to remember the face of someone who had died a long time ago.

"I don't know," he said. It came out very quietly. "I don't think he's there any more."

Hayda looked at him for a long time with her dark, still eyes.

"My father used to say," she said, "that a well is never truly dry. That the water is only very deep, and you must dig down to it." She stood up, and took the cold coffee out of his hands, and poured it on the fire, where it hissed. "I'll make you another. And tomorrow, you are going to take me sailing, in the small boat, out past the harbour mouth, for no reason at all. Not for the work. Not for the city. Just because the weather will be fine. And we are going to stay out until you laugh."

"That could take a very long time."

"I have until the spring," said Hayda.

It took four days. On the fourth day, in the small boat, a long way out past the harbour mouth, with the sun on the water and the wind in the sail and Hayda at the tiller looking grimly determined, a gull swooped down out of the sky and stole the bread roll out of her hand just as she was raising it to her mouth, and she said something in High Sardhi about the gull's mother that would have made Big Olaf blush, and he laughed.

It was a rusty sound, cracked, unused. It hurt his chest. But it was a laugh, and it went on, and on, until he had to hold onto the side of the boat, and Hayda sat at the tiller watching him with her chin up and an expression of profound satisfaction, and did not smile, because she never smiled. But her dark eyes were very bright.

---

At the end of the fifth year, on a night in the late winter, he called them together in the library at the top of the citadel: Jac, and Bastien, and Hayda, and Tam, and Big Olaf. The people he trusted most in the world. He poured them each a glass of Jac's brandy. And then he told them the truth.

All of it. Saltmere, and the *Gull's Promise*, and the Feast of Lamps. The letter. The magistrate. The Keep. Fourteen years in the dark. Master Faro. The Lock under the Teeth, and the chains, and the King. The sack, and the fall, and the eye at the bottom of the world.

It took a long time. When he had finished, the fire had burned down to embers, and nobody had touched their brandy.

"Edric Vane," said Jac Teague at last, slowly, as if trying the name on. "Edric Vane. Huh." She looked at him across the embers. "Well, that's what's been sitting on your chest all these years, then. I always knew it was something. I thought you'd murdered somebody." She picked up her brandy and drank it in one swallow. "Fourteen years. Gods."

"Valtor," said Bastien Corso, very quietly. He was staring into the fire. "Valtor did that to you too."

"Morrow," said Hayda. Her face was white.

Tam said nothing at all. He was nineteen, and he had followed the man he called the Warden across the sea for five years, and he was looking at him now as if he had never seen him before.

"And now," said Big Olaf, in his slow deep voice, "you are going to Solace."

"In the spring," said Edric. "Yes."

"To kill them?"

Edric looked into the embers for a long time.

"No," he said. "Not to kill them. Killing is quick. Killing is nothing. They took everything from me, a piece at a time, without ever once looking me in the face. I'm going to take everything from them. A piece at a time. And at the end, when there's nothing left, I'm going to look them in the face, and tell them who I am." He was quiet a moment. "And then, when they're done, I'm going to go back to the Teeth, and the Lock, and finish what Faro started."

"Alone?" said Jac.

"No," said Edric Vane. He looked at them, one by one, around the dying fire: the red-haired captain, the black-browed steward, the desert princess, the orphan boy, the giant who sang to the Sun. "Not alone. If you'll come."

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

Then Jac Teague put down her empty glass, and stood up, and stretched, and said: "Well, I'm not letting you go to Solace without me. You'd only get lost. Somebody'd better pack."

---

That night, alone in the library after they had gone, he opened his shirt and looked at himself in the dark window glass by the light of the last embers.

The Tidemark had spread. In five years, it had spread from his chest and shoulders down his arms to the wrists, and up his throat to the hinge of the jaw, and in the last year, it had begun to climb his face. A single thin branching line, pale shimmering blue, from the corner of his jaw up across his left cheek, almost to the eye. In daylight, it was barely visible: a faint shadow, a vein, a trick of the light. He could hide it with Still Water, if he had to. But it was there.

He touched it. It was very cold.

*A little more of me will come into you, every time you ask. And if you take enough, in the end, there will be more of me in you than there is of you.*

He thought of the book in the library, the one he did not open any more. *It looked out of her eyes, and there was nothing human left in them.*

He thought of Hayda in the small boat, saying: *Who are you, when you aren't angry?*

He buttoned his shirt to the throat, and went to the window, and looked out across the dark harbour of Cristal: the lamps along the walls, the lights in the terraces, the candles still burning in a few of the Weir-folk's windows. Four thousand people. A city that had not existed five years ago. A city that would not exist without him.

And to the east, beyond the dark sea, beyond the horizon, a city on a cliff, where three men sat in their palaces at the top of the world and had not thought of Edric Vane in nineteen years.

"Soon," he said to the window, to the dark, to the sea. "Soon."
