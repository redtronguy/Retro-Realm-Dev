## Chapter 9: Master Faro

They made a life.

It is a strange thing to say of two men buried alive under the sea, but it was true. Within a month they had made a life, as men in a shipwreck make one, or men on a long voyage: out of routine, and small comforts, and each other.

The guards came twice a day, at dawn and dusk, to push the bowls through the slots. They never opened the doors. In thirty years, Faro said, they had opened his door exactly four times: once when they brought him, once when the governor's inspection had fallen on a day when the governor felt unusually energetic, and twice when Faro had been so ill with fever that the guard had come in to see if he was dead yet. So the two men learned the rhythm of the guards' feet on the stairs, and in the hours between, the stone block came out of the wall, and one of them crawled through to the other.

Mostly it was Edric who crawled, because Faro's knees were bad. He would wriggle through the forty feet of crooked tunnel on his belly in the dark and come out behind the old man's pallet in a shower of grit, and Faro would be sitting there waiting, cross-legged, like a heron on a post, with the blue eyes bright.

"You're late," he would say.

"There's no such thing as late in here."

"There is if I say there is. Sit down. Today we're going to talk about the tides."

---

He talked about everything.

Edric had never met anyone like him. Halloran had been clever, in a sailor's way: he had known the stars and the currents, the winds and the coasts. Moren had been clever in a merchant's way. But Faro seemed to have every kind of cleverness there was, stored away in his wild white head like cargo in a hold, and he brought it out piece by piece, examined it, turned it to the light, and handed it to Edric, as if he had been waiting thirty years for someone to give it to.

He knew six languages. He knew the history of Valemont back before there was a Valemont, and the histories of the Sardh, and the old empires of the far south whose ruins Edric had seen on the Ember Coast. He knew mathematics, and could do in his head sums that Moren's clerks would have needed an afternoon and an abacus for. He knew the names of the stars that sailors did not use, and the movements of the planets, and how to tell the hour at night by the turning of the sky. He knew the bones of the body and the uses of herbs. He knew poetry, reams of it, in every one of his six languages, and would recite it in the dark while Edric dug, in a voice that went up and down like the sea.

And he knew people. That was the strangest thing, for a man who had been locked in a cell for thirty years. He knew how people thought, and why they did what they did, and what they wanted when they said they wanted something else. He would listen to Edric talk about Saltmere, about the crew, about Moren and Halloran and his father, and he would ask one or two questions, small, sideways questions, and then he would say something about a man he had never met that was so exactly, uncannily right that it made the hair rise on Edric's neck.

"How do you know all this?" Edric asked him once.

"I read," said Faro. "And I listened. For forty years, before they put me here. And then I had thirty years with nothing to do but think about what I'd read and heard. You'd be amazed how much a man can understand, if he has thirty years to think and nobody to interrupt him." He tugged his beard. "Mind you, it's not a method I'd recommend."

---

He did not, for a long time, talk about himself.

Edric asked, sometimes. Where he came from. Why he was here. What he had done. And Faro would wave a hand and say *another day*, and change the subject to the tides, or the stars, or the conjugation of irregular verbs in High Sardhi. Edric learned not to push. There was something in the old man's face when he was asked, a closing, like a door swinging shut in a wind, that made him not want to.

It was the sea, in the end, that made him tell.

They had been working on the new tunnel. That was the plan, the first plan, the one they had made on Edric's first day: Faro's old tunnel was useless, ending in Edric's wall, but Edric's cell was the last on the corridor, against the outer wall of the tower. If they dug from the back of Edric's cell, straight out, through the outer wall, they would come out on the face of the cliff, above the sea. Then they would need rope, which they would make from their blankets, and a night with no moon, and a great deal of luck. It would take, Faro estimated, about four years.

"Four years," Edric had said.

"Three, with two of us. Perhaps less. You're young, and stronger than me, and you dig like a man who wants to get somewhere, which I gave up doing about year four." Faro had shrugged. "What else have you got to do?"

So they dug. They took turns with the chisel, an hour at a time, while the other one slept or rested or carried the rubble back through the old tunnel to the gap behind the chimney. It was slow, filthy, back-breaking work, and Edric loved it. He loved the ache in his arms at the end of a day. He loved the sight of the dust and chips piling up beside him, each one a tiny piece of the wall between him and the sky. He loved the feeling, which he had thought he would never have again, of going somewhere.

And one night, about four months into the digging, they were both sitting in Edric's cell resting, with the stone block out of the wall and the chisel lying between them, and the storm outside was so loud that they could hear it even down there, the wind howling past the slit of the window and the waves hitting the foot of the cliff like cannon. And under it all, coming up through the floor, the sea. Booming. Booming. Louder than Edric had ever heard it: so loud that the stone itself seemed to tremble with it, and a fine dust sifted down from the ceiling.

"It's angry tonight," said Edric, without thinking.

Faro turned his head and looked at him very sharply.

"What did you say?"

"The sea. It sounds angry." Edric felt foolish. "I mean, it doesn't. It's just a storm. I only meant—"

"No," said Faro slowly. "No. You're right. It is angry tonight." He was quiet for a moment, listening, with his head on one side, the way Old Ysolde had listened in the Three Anchors. "Your mother was Weir-folk, you said."

"Yes."

"The Weir-folk remember," said Faro, half to himself. "Not much. Bits. Old songs, old sayings. But more than most. They were never quite brought to heel." He looked at Edric again, for a long time, with those very blue eyes. "All right," he said at last. "All right. It's time, I think. You'd better hear it. Sit closer. I don't want to have to shout over the storm, and this isn't a story for shouting."

---

"Three hundred years ago," said Master Faro, "the sea had a god.

"Not a god like the Sun the priests talk about up in Solace, sitting in the sky with a crown on, handing out blessings. Nothing like that. Nothing with a face, or a name, or a temple. It was simply the sea. The sea itself, all of it, everywhere: awake. Alive. Old beyond counting. It did not love us and it did not hate us, any more than you love or hate the fish in the harbour. It simply was, the way the sky is. The Weir-folk had a word for it, in the old tongue, which meant *the One Below*. In the north they called it *the Mother*. In the Sardh, where there's very little sea, they called it *the Thirst*. The scholars who came later called it the Deep. That's the name I use.

"And there were people who could hear it. Not many. One in a thousand, perhaps, born with the gift. They could hear the Deep the way you hear me now: not in words, but in feelings. In tides. And if they were trained, they could do more than hear. All water is one water, you see: the sea, the rain, the river, the tears on your face, the blood in your veins. It's all the same water, and all of it, in the end, belongs to the Deep. And people who could hear the Deep could learn to touch that water, and move it, and ask it to do things.

"They called themselves the Tidewardens. They kept the balance. That was what they believed they were for. They stood between the land and the sea, and spoke for each to the other. They calmed storms, and called the fish, and kept the harbours from silting and the dykes from breaking, and when the sea was angry, as it is tonight, they would go down to the shore and listen, and find out why, and set it right, if they could. They were a little like priests and a little like sailors and a little like doctors, and a little like something else entirely, that there's no word for any more. My mother was one. And her mother. And hers. All the way back.

"And then, three hundred years ago, there was a prince."

Faro paused. The storm howled. The sea boomed under the floor.

"He was a prince of the Radiant Order. The sun-priests. They were small, then, the Radiants: just one order of priests among many, with their own little gift. Some people can hear the sea. Some can call fire out of the sun. The prince was the strongest of them in a hundred years, and he was clever, and he was proud, and he was afraid of dying. That was the root of it, in the end. He was very, very afraid of dying.

"And somehow, I don't know how, nobody knows how, he found out a thing that only the Tidewardens had ever known. That the Deep is very old, older than the land, and very strong, stronger than anything that walks or flies. And that all that strength, all that life, could be taken. Drunk. If you could only hold it still long enough.

"So he did."

The old man's voice had gone very quiet. Edric leaned closer.

"I don't know all of it. Nobody does; they burned the books, after. But I know that he made chains. Chains of sunfire, the strongest working the Radiants have ever made, before or since. And I know that he found a place where the Deep was closest to the surface, where it rose up nearest to the land, a place the Tidewardens held sacred. And there, he bound it. He sank the chains down into the heart of the sea and wrapped them around the Deep, and pulled them tight, and put a lock on them, and built a fortress over the lock so no one would ever find it again. And through the chains, every day, every hour, he drinks. He drinks the life of the sea. And he has not aged one day in three hundred years."

"The Sun King," said Edric. His mouth was dry.

"Aurelian," said Faro. "Long may he reign." He said it the way Edric had heard men in the Three Anchors say the name of a ship that had gone down with all hands.

"And the Tidewardens?"

"They knew at once. Of course they knew. Every one of them, all over the world, the same night, felt the Deep go quiet. Felt it cry out, and then fall silent. Like a voice being strangled." Faro closed his eyes. "My mother told me that her grandmother's grandmother was a girl of nine that night, in a fishing village in the far north, and that she woke up screaming and didn't stop for three days. And then they came."

"Who?"

"The Radiants. With sunfire. Aurelian knew the Tidewardens would try to free the Deep, so he struck first. They call it the Drying, in the few books that still mention it. In the histories they teach in Solace, they call it the Purging of the Drowned Heresy, and they say the Tidewardens were sorcerers who worshipped a sea-demon and drowned children. It took eleven years. They hunted every one of them. Every Tidewarden, every apprentice, every child who showed the gift. They burned the Tidewarden halls and they burned the books and they burned the people. And then they wrote the histories, and in a hundred years nobody remembered that it had ever been different."

"But not all of them," said Edric. "Not all of them died. Your mother. Her mother."

"Not all of them," Faro agreed. "A few hid. A very few. In the north, in the marshes, at the ends of the earth. They hid, and they married, and they taught their children in secret, and their children taught theirs. Fewer every generation. The gift is rare, and it doesn't always breed true, and it's hard, very hard, to learn Tidecraft when the Deep itself is chained, and you can barely hear it, and what you can hear is screaming." He opened his eyes. "My mother was one of the last. And I was the last she trained. And as far as I know, Edric Vane, I am the last there is."

---

The storm was easing. The wind outside had dropped from a howl to a moan. Under the floor, the booming of the sea had slowed, and softened, like a great heart settling.

"Why are you here?" said Edric.

"Because I was young once, and proud, and thought I could do what nobody in three hundred years had managed." Faro smiled, without much humour. "I went to Solace. I was forty, and I'd spent my whole life studying, and I thought I'd found a way. There are archives under the Sunspire, the oldest library in the world, and I thought somewhere in it there must be a record of where the Lock was. Where the Deep was bound. I thought if I could find the Lock, I could open it." He shrugged. "I spent two years getting into those archives. I was very clever about it. And on the night I finally got in, they were waiting for me."

"The Radiants?"

"The King," said Faro. "Himself. Standing in the dark between the shelves, with a lamp in his hand, like a man waiting for a friend. He's been doing this a long time, you see. Three hundred years. Every few generations some Tidewarden's grandchild gets clever and comes looking. He always knows. He can feel us, I think, the way we feel the Deep. We're the only thing in the world he's afraid of." The old man's voice was very quiet. "He's beautiful. Did you know that? Nobody ever tells you that. Golden, and young, and so beautiful it hurts to look at him. And so old behind the eyes that it hurts more. He talked to me for an hour. Very politely. He asked about my mother. He knew her name.

"And then he told me he wasn't going to kill me. He said he never killed the last of anything; it was a waste. He said he was going to send me to a place where I could be near my god, and listen to it, every day and every night, for the rest of my life, and know that I had come so close, and failed." Faro looked down at the floor. Under it, the sea had gone almost quiet. "And then he sent me here."

Edric stared at him. He looked at the floor, at the stone under their feet, at the dark beneath the stone. He thought about the governor, saying *lower east*. He thought about the guard, grinning: *down here you're under the sea, my lad.* He thought about the booming, day and night, like a great slow heart. Like something breathing. Like something *trying* to breathe.

"Faro," he said. "Faro, this place. The Keep. The King built it. In the first years. Over the place where—"

"Yes," said Master Faro. "Yes. Now you understand."

He reached out and laid one thin hand flat on the wet stone floor between them, the way a man lays his hand on the flank of a sick horse.

"It's down there," he said. "Under us. Not far. The Lock. The chains. The Deep. That's what you've been hearing all this time, Edric. That's what the men who go mad in here are hearing. It isn't the sea. It's *the* sea. It's the god. It's been lying chained under this rock for three hundred years, and every day the King drinks a little more of it, and every day the sea gets a little sicker, and every day it beats against the chains, and every night it cries." He took his hand away. "And I've been sitting on top of it for thirty years, close enough to touch, and there's not one single thing I can do."

---

Neither of them spoke for a long time.

At last, Edric said: "Show me."

Faro looked up. "Show you what?"

"Tidecraft. The gift. You said all water is one water. You said Tidewardens could touch it. Show me."

The old man looked at him for a long moment. Then he sighed, and smiled a little, and held out his right hand, palm up, above the wet stone floor.

"There's not much left," he said. "With the Deep chained, it's like trying to hear a whisper in a gale. When I was young, my mother could call a wave out of a flat calm. Me, on a good day, I can do this."

He closed his eyes. He breathed in, very slowly, and out, very slowly, and Edric saw his face change: saw it go still and smooth and far away, like the surface of a pool when the wind drops.

On the floor beneath his hand, the wet stone began to sweat.

Edric watched. The film of damp that always covered the floor of the cells began to gather. It crept together, slowly, from all directions, as if the stone were being tilted towards the centre: a thin sheen, then a skin, then a single bead of water, clear and round, the size of a pea, sitting on the stone under the old man's palm. It trembled. And then, very slowly, as if drawn up on an invisible thread, it rose off the stone, and hung in the air, a finger's breadth below Faro's hand, turning slowly, catching the faint grey light from the window like a tiny moon.

It hung there for the space of three breaths. Then the old man let out a long sigh, and the bead fell, and splashed on the stone, and was only water again.

Faro opened his eyes. He looked very tired, suddenly, and very old.

"That's all," he said. "That's all I have. Thirty years in here, and that's all that's left. A drop of water and a sore head."

Edric was staring at the place where the drop had fallen.

"Teach me," he said.

"Edric—"

"Teach me. You said the Weir-folk remember. You said my mother's blood. You said—" He stopped, and swallowed, and made himself say it calmly. "Old Ysolde. Mira's grandmother. At the feast, the night they took me. She looked at me for a long time and said I had the sea in me. She said it was quiet. But it was there. Teach me, Faro. Please. Whatever there is to learn. All of it. Everything you know."

The old man looked at him for a long, long moment, with those very blue eyes. Something moved in them: something old and sad and wary, and under it something else, the same thing Edric had seen on the first day. Stubborn, patient, nearly as old as the stones.

"It's not a gift," he said at last. "You understand that? Not with the Deep in chains. It's mostly pain, and very little to show for it. It's years of sitting in the dark learning to breathe, and listening to something scream. It may never come to anything. You may never so much as raise a drop."

"I've got years," said Edric. "I've got nothing but years. You said so yourself. What else have I got to do?"

And Master Faro laughed, the cracked, delighted laugh Edric had first heard through the wall, the laugh that ended in coughing, and wiped his eyes, and put out his hand.

"All right," he said. "All right, Edric Vane. Everything. We'll start tomorrow." He paused. "But not with Tidecraft. Tidecraft is the last thing. First, we'll start with what's in your head. Can you read?"

"A little. Enough for a manifest."

"A manifest," said Faro, in a voice of deep pain. "Gods below. Well, then. Tomorrow, we begin with the alphabet. And the day after, with arithmetic. And then history. And then languages. And then we'll see."

"How long will all that take?"

"With a good student?" Faro considered. "And nothing else to do, and no one to interrupt? Oh, ten years. Perhaps less."

Edric looked at him, and then, for the first time in more than a year, he laughed too.

Under the floor, in the dark beneath the rock, the sea had gone quiet. As if it were listening.
