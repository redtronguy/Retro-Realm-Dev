# Event 05: Faro's Medicine

*Branches from Chapter 11, "The Lock Beneath": the eighth year, after Faro's first stroke on the rope ("The first one's a warning. You get three"), in the days when a guard comes to Edric's door slot with an offer.*

*The canon path, in which Edric refuses and Faro recovers slowly and loses the use of his arm for good, continues in [Chapter 11](../../11-the-lock-beneath.md) and [Chapter 12](../../12-the-sack.md).*

## Give a false name

*Heart − · Tide 0*

He got the old man back to his own cell before dawn. He never knew how. Forty yards of tunnel on his belly in the dark, dragging Faro by the shoulders, stopping every few feet to listen to the breathing; and then the pallet, and the blanket, and the stone block pushed back into the wall behind it, and the joints smeared with grit. He lay in his own cell with his ear to the stone until the grey light came, and listened to Faro breathe on the other side of it, slow and rough, like a man sleeping off a blow.

At dawn the bowl came through Faro's slot and stayed there. At dusk the guard found it still full, and swore, and opened the door.

It was the squat, scarred guard with the ring of keys, the one who had grinned at Edric on his first day and told him he would hear the sea for as long as he lived. Edric heard him come in. He heard the lantern set down on the floor, and a long silence, and then the man's voice, oddly quiet.

"Well, grandfather. Look at you. Face slid off like butter in a pan." A grunt. "Thirty-odd years, and the old priest finally springs a leak."

Then another silence, longer. Edric held his breath.

"Where's your blanket, eh?"

There was no blanket. There had been none on Faro's pallet for four days, because both their blankets were hanging in a rotted rope from a spur of rock in the roof of the temple, and there had been no time, in the dark, with the old man dying in his arms, to think of it. Edric had put his own over Faro. It lay there now, on the pallet. One blanket, in a cell that had been issued one, with a number stitched into its hem that was not Faro's number.

He heard the guard pick it up. He heard him turn it over.

"Four-eleven," said the guard, slowly, reading. "Well, well."

The door shut. The key turned. The lantern went away along the corridor, and Edric lay on his pallet in the dark and waited for it to come to his own door, and it did not come.

---

It came the next night instead.

The bowl slid through the slot, and then, instead of footsteps going away, there was the creak of a man squatting down on the other side of the door, and the guard's voice, very low, close to the gap.

"You awake, four-eleven?"

Edric said nothing.

"Course you are. You've not slept since the old one went down. I've heard you through the door, knocking on that wall." A pause. "Here's a thing you won't know, being down here. Four nights ago, middle of the night, the whole Keep jumped. Like a ship hitting a rock. Woke the governor out of his bed. Cracked the big cistern on the lower deep clean across, and the sea came up through it a foot deep before it went down again. Governor's had men tapping walls for three days. He says it's digging. He says somebody on the low landings is digging, and he's damned if he'll be the governor who lost a prisoner in thirty years." The voice dropped further. "He's offering a reward for a name. Double rations for a month. Or something better, if a man asks for it."

"What do you want?" said Edric. His voice came out hoarse.

"What do *you* want? That's the question." The guard sniffed. "Your neighbour's dying. I've seen that face before; my old dad had it. Week, two weeks, he'll have another, and that'll be him. Unless." A small, careful silence. "Physician comes over from Saltmere Thursday, for the governor's chest. Fine fellow. Carries a draught for the palsy, a Radiant physic, gold-coloured, costs more than I earn in a year. Give it inside a week of a fresh stroke and they say a man gets up and walks. Face, arm, the lot. The governor'd give it for a name. He'd give a good deal more than that to be able to write *digger found* in his book."

Edric lay with his face against the wet stone.

"I've your blanket in my locker," said the guard, almost kindly. "I can lose it again, or I can show it to the governor. I don't much care which. I don't think the old priest's been digging; he couldn't dig his way out of a pudding. I think it's somebody else. And I think you know who. You give me a name by Wednesday night, and the old man gets his draught Thursday, and I forget all about the blanket. You don't, and I don't." The knees creaked as he stood. "Think on it."

---

He thought on it for two days.

He thought of the tunnel, and the temple, and seven chains of gold going down into black water, and the gate of fire across the sea-arch. He thought of the old man on the other side of the wall, who had stopped making sense on the second day and lay now muttering in High Sardhi to someone who was not there. He went through the wall twice a day, against all caution, and held the cup to the half of Faro's mouth that still worked, and watched the water run out of the other half and down into the white beard.

He thought of his own name. *Four hundred and eleven.* He thought of what would happen if he said it: the door open, the guards in his cell, the walls tapped, the block found behind the pallet. The tunnel. Eight years. Thirty-eight years. All of it, in a morning, for a gold-coloured draught.

And he thought of the voices that came down the stairwell sometimes, at the change of watch, when the guards talked.

*Two-oh-six is at it again. Scratching at his floor all night with his nails. Like a dog burying a bone.* That had been a year ago, or two. *Mad as a hatter, two-oh-six. Old Holm. Says the sea talks to him through the floor and he's digging down to answer.* Edric had never seen the man. He did not know his face, or his crime, or how long he had been there. He knew only a number and a name and a habit, heard through a door slot, and that the man was mad, and that the guards already thought he dug.

On Wednesday night, when the creak came on the other side of the door, Edric said, before the guard could speak:

"Two hundred and six."

A silence.

"Old Holm?" said the guard. "Upper north?"

"I've heard him. Through the stair. Every night. Scraping." Edric found that his voice was perfectly steady, and that frightened him more than anything else. "He's been at it for years. Ask anyone. The whole Keep hears him."

The guard was quiet for a long time.

"Well," he said at last. "Well, well. Two-oh-six. That'll please the governor. He's never liked Holm." The knees creaked. "Thursday, then."

---

On Thursday, a little before dusk, Edric heard the door of Faro's cell open, and a stranger's voice, fussy and educated and impatient, and the clink of glass. He heard it close again. And that night, when he went through the wall, the old man was asleep, really asleep, breathing slow and deep, and the dragged-down side of his face had already begun, very faintly, to lift.

Within a week Faro could speak. Within two he could sit up. Within a month he was walking about his cell with one hand on the wall, cursing his knees, and his left arm, which in the first days had hung at his side like a parcel, could lift a cup. By the end of the season he could hold the chisel again.

"I don't understand it," he said, flexing the fingers of his left hand in the grey light, and looking at them as if they belonged to someone else. "My mother had this. Her father had it. Nobody comes back from the first one whole. Nobody." He looked at Edric. "There was a man in here. I heard a stranger's voice. I remember a taste like honey and copper. What did you do, Edric?"

"The governor's physician was on the island," said Edric. "The guard took pity on you."

Faro looked at him for a long moment with his very blue eyes. Then he said, "Hm," and went back to his fingers, and did not ask again.

But a week later, sitting cross-legged on the floor of his cell, he began a lesson he had never begun before.

"Still Water," he said. "We'll start the third discipline early, since I've been given time I wasn't owed. The simplest thing in it, and the most useful. Truthsight. A lie makes ripples, Edric. In the voice, in the breath, in the water of the body. A man's blood moves differently when he lies, the way a pool moves when a stone goes in. A Still Water Warden learns to feel for it." He held out his left hand, the restored one, palm up. "Put your hand on mine. Breathe. Find the stillness. And then listen while I talk."

Edric put his hand on the old man's palm, and breathed, and found the stillness. And Faro talked: about the weather, about his mother, about the conjugation of irregular verbs. And once, in the middle of it, quite casually, he said, "The guard took pity on me."

Under Edric's hand, in the old man's palm, something moved. A faint, cold flutter, like a fish turning in deep water.

He snatched his hand away.

Faro did not look at him. He was looking at the wall, where the chalk poems were.

"Yes," he said quietly. "That's what it feels like. Good. You have it. I thought you would." He was silent a long time. "Two-oh-six went into the Well the Friday after the physician came. The guards talked about it on the stair. I heard them. I've been lying here a month, listening to you not tell me."

Edric could not speak.

"I'm not going to say I'd rather be dead," said Faro. "I'd be lying, and you'd feel it now." He flexed his left hand, slowly, open and shut. "I'm going to say that I'll use every day of this to teach you, because it was bought at a price I didn't agree to and can't give back. And you're going to learn all of it, every scrap, so that it was worth something." He turned his blue eyes on Edric at last, and they were very tired. "That's the only way I know to pay for a thing like this. Sit down. We'll go again."

### Later

The temple under the Keep was blazing when the longboat came out of the sea-tunnel, lit to every corner by seven chains burning like the noon sun, and Edric saw things in it he had never seen in the dark.

He saw the carvings, every one, the Tidewardens with their hands raised to the sea. He saw the rope he and Faro had plaited from their blankets, still hanging from the hole in the roof, rotted to a thread. And on a ledge high up the far wall, below a black opening in the rock that he had taken, twelve years ago, for a shadow, he saw something white.

A shaft. A dry shaft, cut straight down from somewhere far above, from the punishment cell the guards had called the Well, ending in the temple roof. And on the ledge below it, where whatever was thrown down the Well had come to rest at last, a heap of bones. Old ones, grey with centuries, crumbling. And on top of them, newer than the rest, still with rags of cloth clinging, a long thin skeleton lying on its side with its knees drawn up, and an iron collar round its neck.

He could read the number on the collar from the boat. The gold light made it easy. *206.*

The fingers of both hands were worn to the second joint. Down the rock face of the ledge behind the skeleton, in long uneven lines, there were scratches: hundreds of them, thousands, gouged into the stone with nothing but nails, going down towards the water.

*Says the sea talks to him through the floor,* a guard had said on a stairwell twelve years ago. *Says he's digging down to answer.*

He had dug. He had lain on that ledge in the dark above the black water and the chains, alone, with nobody to hear him, and dug with his fingers towards the sea until he died. And Faro had lived two years longer, and taught Edric the ripple in a lie, and the shape of a man's want, and a dozen things besides; and Edric had walked out of the Keep at thirty-five instead of thirty-three, knowing more than any listener since the Drying.

Across the dead water, the golden King rose from the edge of the pool and turned. Edric did not look at him. He was still looking at the ledge.

*I know that one,* said the Deep, faint as a pulse, from far below. *He talked to me every night for a year. He was the only one who ever asked how I was.*

## Give your own name

*Heart + · Tide 0*

He got the old man back to his own cell before dawn, and lay with his ear to the wall all day listening to him breathe, slow and rough, like a man sleeping off a blow. At dusk the guard found Faro's bowl still full in the slot and opened the door, and found the old man with half his face fallen, and found something else: the blanket on the pallet, with the number stitched in its hem. *Four-eleven.* Faro's own was hanging from a spur of rock in the roof of the temple, unpicked and plaited into rope.

The squat, scarred guard came to Edric's slot the next night and squatted outside it and told him how things stood. The whole Keep had jumped, four nights ago, like a ship striking a rock. The big cistern on the lower deep had cracked across and the sea had come up through it. The governor was sure someone was digging, and had promised a reward for a name. The physician was coming over from Saltmere on Thursday for the governor's chest, and carried a gold-coloured draught for the palsy that could make a man whole, if he had it within a week of the stroke.

"The governor'd give it for a name," said the guard. "And I've your blanket in my locker. I don't think the old priest dug anything. I think it's someone else. Give me a name by Wednesday, and he gets his draught, and I lose the blanket. Don't, and I don't."

---

He knew the name he could give. He had heard it on the stair at the change of watch, a hundred times: *two-oh-six, Old Holm, mad as a hatter, scratching at his floor all night with his nails.* The guards already believed it. It would cost Edric nothing. It would cost a stranger he had never seen everything.

He lay on his pallet and turned it over for a night and a day, the way Faro had taught him to turn over a puzzle. And every way he turned it, he came back to the same place: a man in a cell he could not see, on a landing he had never stood on, being dragged out into the lantern light by a name that had come out of Edric's mouth.

*Every man in this keep is innocent, if you ask him.* The guard had said that to him in his first week. Edric had hated him for it.

He thought, too, of what would happen if he gave no name at all. The blanket shown to the governor. The door of Faro's cell opened, and opened again, and the walls tapped, and the block found behind the pallet, and the old man dragged out half-dead to answer for a tunnel. Or no draught, and the old man lying as he lay now until the second stroke came.

There was a third way. It took him most of the second night to see it, and the rest of the night to make it.

He worked at the wall under his own window slit, at the far end of the cell from the pallet and the hidden block, with the edge of his spoon and his bare hands, as quietly as he had ever worked. He did not try to dig anything. He tried to make a place that looked as if a man had been digging for a year, badly, with nothing: mortar scraped out from round two stones, a hollow gouged behind them the size of a man's head, a little heap of grit pushed under the pallet. By dawn his fingers were bleeding and it looked exactly like what it was meant to look like. A clumsy, hopeless, dangerous hole.

On Wednesday night, when the creak came on the other side of the door, he said:

"It's me."

A long silence.

"You?"

"Four hundred and eleven. It's me. I've been digging. Under my window. I've been at it a year. The old man couldn't lift a spoon; he never knew. I took his blanket because mine was wet through from the stone." He kept his voice flat. "You can come and look. I'll show you."

The guard was quiet so long that Edric thought he had gone.

"Gods," he said finally. "You're either the honestest fool in the Keep or the cleverest liar." The knees creaked. "Either way, the governor gets his name. Get your things."

"The draught," said Edric. "The old man. You said."

"I said a name for the draught. I didn't say whose." The voice was almost amused. "Thursday. He'll have it Thursday. You'll not be here to see."

---

They came for him at dawn with three men and a lantern, and found the hole under the window, and looked at it, and laughed. They tapped every wall in the cell with an iron bar, and Edric stood against the far corner with his heart stopped and watched the bar ring off the stone a hand's breadth from the block behind the pallet, and ring off it again, and move on. Then they put irons on his wrists for the first time in eight years and took him down.

There was a landing below the lower deep that he had not known existed. The stair went on past it, narrower and wetter, and ended at three doors in a corridor where the walls ran with water and the floor was ankle-deep in it. The cell behind the middle door was four paces by three. There was no window. There was no pallet. There was a stone ledge above the waterline, a bucket, and the sea, so loud through the rock that it was not a sound any more but a weight.

"Governor says a year," said the guard, at the door. "Then we'll see." He hesitated. "The old man had his draught. Yesterday, at dusk. I saw the physician go in."

The door shut.

---

It was the worst year of his life, worse than the first. There was no Faro, no lessons, no tunnel, no chalk on the walls, no grey light. There was only the dark and the wet and the booming, and the ledge, and the bowl twice a day, and the memory of an old man's breathing on the other side of a wall he could no longer reach.

He did not stop eating. He had learned that much. He sat on the ledge with his feet out of the water and breathed. In for eight. Hold for eight. Out for eight. Hold for eight. He went over the poems in his head, every one Faro had written on the wall. He conjugated High Sardhi verbs aloud to the dark. He recited the histories of the noble houses of Valemont. And when he could not bear any of it any longer, he put his hand flat on the wet stone of the wall, the wall that had the whole weight of the Deep on the other side of it, and listened, and found that the sea, so close, so loud, did not sound angry down here. It sounded like a great, slow, patient breathing, keeping him company.

They brought him up in the spring.

The guard said nothing as he unlocked the irons outside the door of four hundred and eleven, except, gruffly, "Hole's been mortared. Don't start another." The door shut behind him. Edric stood in his old cell in the faint grey light from the slit, and wept at the light, and then went to the head of the pallet and knelt and laid his hand on the block in the wall.

From the other side of it, very close, came three knocks.

---

Faro was sitting on the floor of his cell when Edric came through, cross-legged like a heron on a post, the way he had sat on the first day. The draught had done what a draught could. His face had come mostly back to itself. His speech was slow but whole. His left arm lay in his lap, thin and useless, and would never move again.

He looked at Edric for a long time without saying anything. Then he held out his good hand, and Edric took it, and the old man gripped it so hard that it hurt.

"The guard told me," said Faro. "On the stair, the day they took you. He thought it was funny. *Your neighbour gave himself up for you, grandfather. Four-eleven. Says he dug a hole under his window for a year.*" His voice cracked. "I knew there was no hole. And I knew who they'd have taken if there hadn't been one. I've had a year to lie here and think about that."

"It was the only way I could see."

"It was the best way there was. There usually is one, and nobody takes it." Faro let go of his hand. "I've been a careful man all my life, Edric. Careful in the archives. Careful in here. I've kept things back, even from you. Things I wasn't sure of. Things I was afraid were only stories. I told myself it was because you weren't ready." He looked down at his dead arm. "It was because I was afraid that if I said them aloud I'd have to admit I'd never live to try them. I've had a year to get over that."

He was quiet a moment. The sea boomed under the floor.

"I told you," he said, "that nobody can break the chains. Not by strength. Not any Tidewarden who ever lived. That's true." He leaned forward. "But strength isn't the only thing that ever opened anything. My mother had a saying. Her mother had it from hers, back and back, from before the Drying, from before the chains. I thought it was a nursery rhyme. Perhaps it is." He said it in the oldest form of the Weir-tongue, slowly, the way you say a thing you have carried a long way. "*A chain is not always broken. Sometimes it is sung loose.*"

Edric stared at him.

"In the oldest days," said Faro, "before the cities, when the sea was sick, the Wardens didn't command it. They went down to the shore with the fisherfolk, a great many of them, and they sang. Not Wardens alone. Ordinary people. The ones who'd lived beside the water longest and remembered the old songs. My mother said it took many voices, and very little power, and that nobody since the Drying has known the songs." He smiled faintly. "Except perhaps the Weir. The Weir-folk were never quite brought to heel. They still sing to the sea at their weddings, don't they? And float lamps for the drowned."

Edric thought of a boat with no oars garlanded with sea-lavender, and an old woman with black bead eyes unwrapping a crown at midnight, and Mira saying, *the sea has to agree.*

"I don't know if it's true," said Faro. "I don't know if it can be done. I know it would take a man who doesn't want to be the strongest thing in the room. Very few men are like that." He looked at Edric. "You gave them your own name for a stranger. I think you might be."

### Later

On the ninth night of the voyage east, with the *Faro* running before a thin wind and the sea dying under her keel, Edric found Mira at the stern rail with the crown in her lap.

She had not let it out of her hands since Cristal. The dried sea-lavender had gone the colour of dust and two of the white shells were missing, and she turned it slowly in the lamplight, the way her grandmother had turned it once on the Night of Lamps.

"Do you remember the wedding song?" he said. "The one the Weir sings when the boat goes out. The one that asks the sea."

She looked up, surprised. "Of course. Every child in the Weir knows it. Why?"

He told her. An old man in a cell with one arm, the year after the deeper cell, saying a nursery rhyme in the oldest tongue as if it were the most precious thing he owned. *A chain is not always broken. Sometimes it is sung loose.* He had carried it for ten years. He had never told anyone. He had been afraid, as Faro had been afraid, that it was only a story, and that saying it aloud would make it smaller.

Mira was quiet for a long time.

"Gran used to say the wedding song wasn't for weddings," she said at last. "Not first. She said it was older than that, and the weddings came after, because people forgot what it was for and needed a reason to keep singing it." She looked down at the crown. "She said it took the whole Weir. Every voice. Even the ones who couldn't hold a note."

Then, very softly, under her breath, so that only he could hear it over the creak of the rigging, she began to hum: a slow tune, low and rising, like a tide coming up a channel among the reeds.

Under the keel, faint as a pulse, a long day's sail from the Lock, something at the bottom of the world went still, and listened.
