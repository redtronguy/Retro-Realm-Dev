# Event 04: The Other Prisoner

*Branches from Chapter 10, "The Schooling": the fourth year, after the chisel fails against the fused outer wall and "in the end, they went down", when the new tunnel passes behind the wall of a cell on the lowest landing and the man inside it hears them.*

## Bring him in

*Heart + · Tide 0*

The voice came out of the rock about a hand's breadth from his ear, so close and so unexpected that Edric dropped the chisel.

"Evening, gents. Going somewhere?"

He lay frozen on his belly in the dark at the end of the tunnel. They were deep now, under the foundations of the tower, following the soft grey seam Faro had chosen down and round the root of the fused wall. He had been digging alone for an hour while Faro rested above. He had heard nothing for an hour but his own breath and the sea.

"Don't stop on my account," said the voice. It was a young man's voice, quick and light, with the flat sharp vowels of the Solace streets. "I've been listening to you for four months. Scrape, scrape, scrape, and then the old one muttering about angles. It's been better than a play. I worked a stone loose in my wall to hear you better. And look, there's a crack behind it. And look, there's you."

Edric felt along the rock beside his face. There: a seam in the seam, a split no wider than two fingers, and a faint, foul breath of air coming through it. Air from a cell.

"Who are you?" he said.

"Joss." A pause. "Just Joss. Fathers are for people who can afford them. Number three hundred and ninety, lower deep, last landing before the fishes. Two years, about. You?"

Edric said nothing.

"Fair enough," said Joss. "I'd not tell me either. But here's the thing, friend." The voice dropped, and lost some of its lightness, and Edric heard under it what he had heard in his own voice through a door slot, years ago: the edge of something close to breaking. "You're digging out. I'm not a fool. You're going down and round and out, and you'll be gone one night, and I'll still be here, listening to the sea till I go mad like the old priest. So you dig me in. You widen this crack and you take me with you. Or the next time the guard comes down with my porridge, I tell him there's moles in the walls of lower east, and he can come and look for himself."

---

"No," said Faro.

He was sitting on his pallet with his bad knees drawn up and his beard in his fist, and he did not raise his voice, which was how Edric knew how angry he was.

"No. A thief who threatens is a thief who sells. He's sold us already, in his head. He's only waiting to see who pays better, us or the guard. You let him through that wall and you've given him the whole tunnel to sell. Every foot of it. Four years of your life. Thirty-four of mine."

"He'll sell it anyway if we don't."

"Then we stop digging there. We go round. We pack the crack and fill it and go round by another seam."

"And lose a year." Edric sat on the floor with his back to the wall. "And he'll still be on the other side of it, and he'll still know. All he has to do is say *lower east* to the guard, and they'll open your door for the fifth time in thirty-four years and find the hole behind your pallet."

Faro was silent.

"He's been in the dark two years," said Edric, more quietly. "Alone. Listening to us. Four months, he said, he's lain there with his ear to the stone, listening to two men talk. You know what that is, Faro. You heard me do it." He looked at his hands. "You dug faster because you heard a boy stop eating on the other side of a wall. You told me so."

"That was different."

"Was it? How?"

The old man looked at him for a long time with his very blue eyes. Then he let go of his beard and laughed, a short, sour, unwilling laugh.

"It was different because I chose you," he said. "And this one is choosing us. With a knife at our throats." He shook his head. "Gods below. I've taught you too much philosophy and not enough sense. All right. All right. Bring him through. But you'll teach him to be quiet, because I can't, and you'll share your bowl with him, because I won't, and when he sells us to the governor for a blanket and an extra spoon, you'll remember that I said so."

---

It took eleven days to widen the crack into a passage a man could crawl through, and another nine to make the hole on Joss's side into something a guard would not see. They did it the way Faro had done his first hole, years ago: a block low in the wall behind the pallet, the mortar replaced each time with damp grit. Joss did his share on his side with a spoon handle and a will. He was not good at it. He was impatient, and he scraped when he should have pried, and he sang under his breath while he worked, and twice Edric had to hiss at him to stop when the guard's feet came down the stairs.

And then, on the twentieth night, the block came out, and Joss came through.

He was smaller than his voice. A thin, quick, sharp-faced young man with a long nose that had been broken at least twice and a thatch of hair grown wild in the dark, and the darting eyes of someone who had spent his whole life watching for the hand that would grab him. He crawled out of the tunnel into Faro's cell in a shower of grit and stood up and looked around at the walls and the dead fireplace and the chalk writing on the stones, and at the old man on the pallet, and then at Edric, and Edric saw his face do something complicated.

"You're real," Joss said. "Gods. You're real. I half thought I'd made you up." And then, quickly, before anyone could see what was in his face: "You've a fireplace. A *fireplace*. And writing. What's all that say?"

"Poetry," said Faro. "Can you read?"

"Read?" Joss laughed. "That's why I'm here, grandfather."

He told them, sitting on the floor with his knees up and Edric's bowl of porridge between his hands, eating it in quick ferrety mouthfuls as if someone might take it away. He had been a housebreaker in Solace, on the Lantern Stairs under the cliffs, the best on the Stairs, he said, though he said it the way a boy says it. Two years ago he had gone over the garden wall of a fine house on the Street of Gold, a Radiant lord's house, and come out with a little ebony casket from a locked desk because it was heavy and had silver on the corners. It had been full of letters. He had not been able to read a word of them. He had been trying to sell the silver corners in a back-stair shop when the Radiants came.

"Didn't even ask me what was in them," said Joss. "Just looked at me, and looked at the letters, and the officer went white, and then I was in a boat. No judge, nothing. Sun-something warrant." He scraped the bowl. "Funny thing. If I'd known my letters, I might've known not to take it."

Faro and Edric looked at each other across the cell.

"It isn't funny," said Edric. "Not even a little."

---

So there were three of them.

It was slower. Faro had been right about that, as he was right about most things. Joss could not dig in silence and had to be taught, and Edric taught him, patiently, an hour at a time, the way Halloran had once taught a dockhand's son to coil a line without fouling it. Joss's cell had to be kept looking like a cell, and his hole had to be hidden, and his rubble went down behind the chimney with theirs, a handful at a time. Twice the guard on the lowest landing came by at the wrong hour, and twice they lost a week waiting to be sure he had not seen anything.

And the porridge did not stretch. A bowl twice a day was barely enough for one grown man; Edric had learned that in his first year. Now he split his bowl with Joss most nights, because Joss's guard was a sour man who skimped the lowest landing, and Joss came through the wall hungry.

He did not mind as much as he had expected to. He would lie on his pallet at night with his stomach growling and listen to the two of them through the wall, Faro and the thief, the old man reciting High Sardhi verbs and the young one mangling them, and Faro saying *no, no, you sound like a camel being sick, again*, and Joss laughing, and he would find that he was smiling in the dark.

"Another year," said Faro, one evening, measuring with his thumb on the chalk plan on the wall. "At least. Perhaps two. Because of him."

"I know."

The old man looked at him sidelong. "You don't sound sorry."

Edric thought about it.

"When I was alone in my cell," he said, "before you came through, I used to count. Marks on the wall. Bowls. Days. I thought if I counted carefully enough I'd know how long I had left." He looked at the chalk plan, and the thin line of the tunnel going down, and the new branch leading off it to the lowest landing like a twig off a branch. "I don't count any more. I don't think I'd want to know."

Faro grunted and went back to his plan. But later that night, through the wall, Edric heard him say to Joss, gruffly, *Again. The alphabet. From the beginning. If you're going to be a nuisance you can at least be a literate one.*

### Later

The *Slip* lay hove-to under the Teeth on a black night in the fifteenth year, with no lamp lit, a quarter of a mile off the foot of the cliff, where the guards of the Drowned Keep had thrown their dead into the sea for three hundred years.

It had cost him nine thousand marks and a long evening in the governor's parlour wearing the face of a foreign shipping agent called Master Wilde, who had a cousin, it seemed, among the prisoners on the lowest landing, and a great deal of money, and a kindly wish that the poor fellow's sufferings might be shortened. Ostric Pell had wheezed, and counted, and made a note in his ledger beside the number 390, and it was the first note in thirty-seven years that had not been a lie.

*Died of a fever. Buried at sea.*

The sack came off the cliff at the change of watch. Edric watched it fall, a small pale shape in the starlight, and felt it strike the water, and felt the iron ball drag it under.

He reached. The sea came to him at once, cold and eager; he felt the Mark on his chest flare and the salt rise in his mouth. He found the sack forty feet down and still going. He slit it with a thin edge of current, as he had once cut a rope from his own ankle, and he took hold of the thrashing, choking man inside, and brought him up.

Joss came out of the water beside the *Slip* like a cork out of a bottle, and Jac Teague's crew hauled him over the side by his collar, and he lay on the deck in a pool of seawater, retching, and looked up at the man standing over him, with his long hair and his foreign coat and the faint blue lines under his collar.

"You took your bloody time," said Joss, when he could speak.

"Two years," said Edric. "I had to find a fortune first."

Joss laughed until he was sick again.

He came to Cristal with them, in the first year of the rebuilding, when there were not yet forty people on the whole island. He could not dig. He could not build. He could not sail to save his life. But within a month he knew the name of every man who came into the hook-shaped harbour, and what he had in his hold, and whom he owed money to, and what he said in his cups. By the third year, there was not a smuggler in the Shattered Reach who could sneeze in a tavern without the Count hearing of it by morning. By the fifth, Joss was in Solace, on the Lantern Stairs again, in a fine coat, running a lodging-house where the servants of the great houses came to drink on their nights off.

He wrote his reports himself, in a clumsy, painstaking hand, with the High Sardhi verbs spelled wrong. Edric kept every one.

## Collapse his side of the tunnel

*Heart − · Tide +*

The voice came out of the rock about a hand's breadth from his ear, so close and so unexpected that Edric dropped the chisel.

"Evening, gents. Going somewhere?"

He lay frozen on his belly at the end of the tunnel, deep under the foundations, with the sea booming through the stone around him. There was a crack in the rock beside his face, a split no wider than two fingers, and a sour breath of cell air coming through it.

"Joss," said the voice. "Number three hundred and ninety, lower deep. Been listening to you four months. Scrape, scrape, and the old one muttering. Here's how it is, friend. You dig me in, or next time my porridge comes I tell the guard he's got moles in lower east."

Edric heard himself say, "Give us time."

"I've given you four months." The voice was light and quick and frightened underneath. "One week. You come back in a week with a way through, or I start shouting."

---

They argued for six days.

Faro would not have him. *A thief who threatens is a thief who sells.* Edric would not stop the digging. They sat in Faro's cell in the grey light and went round and round it, while the chalk plan of the tunnel on the wall stared down at them like an accusation: four years of Edric's life, thirty-four of Faro's, a thin white line going down into the rock towards the sea.

"We go round," Faro said, for the tenth time. "We pack the crack and go round by another seam."

"And he says *lower east* to the guard, and they open your door."

"Then we bring him in."

"You said yourself he'd sell us the moment it paid him."

"I did say that," said Faro wearily. "And I'll say it again. And I'll still say we bring him in, because the other choices are worse." He looked at Edric. "There are other choices, aren't there. You've been thinking them. I can see you thinking them."

Edric did not answer.

On the seventh day he went down the tunnel alone, to tell the thief they would widen the crack. He told himself that was what he was going to say. He had almost made himself believe it.

He was lying at the end of the tunnel, with his face to the crack, when Joss said, "Well?" And then, before Edric could speak, the man went on in a rush, as if he had been rehearsing it for a week: "And don't think you can cheat me, friend. I'm not stupid. I know how this goes. You'll dig me in and then you'll leave me behind on the night, or you'll knock me on the head down there in the dark where no one'll hear. So here's what. You bring the old one's chisel through to me first, as a pledge, and I keep it. And if I don't like how it's going, I give it to the guard. Proof, see? They'll hang you both for it."

And above them, faint, far, the ring of boots on the stair. The guard, early.

"There he is," said Joss. His voice had gone high and wild. "Hear him? That's him coming. You give me the chisel now, through the crack, or I start shouting now. I swear it. I'll shout. *I'll shout.*"

---

Afterwards, Edric could never put it in order.

He remembered the boots on the stair, coming down, ringing, and the thief's breath fast and ragged on the other side of the crack. He remembered the chisel under his hand, and thinking, with perfect clarity, *if I give him this, it's over.* He remembered the dark pressing on him from every side, the rock above and below and around, the sea booming, and Joss's voice saying *I'll shout*, and something rising up in him from a place he had not known was there, black and huge and cold, like a wave standing up out of a flat sea.

*Four years. Four years in the dark, and you'll give it to the guard for a spoon. You'll put me back. You'll put me back in the dark forever, like he did, like the magistrate did, with his fire and his kindness—*

He put both his hands flat on the wet rock of the seam, beside the crack, and pushed. Not with his arms. With the thing that was rising in him.

And the rock answered.

He felt it go. He felt the water in it, the damp that lived in every pore and crevice of the soft grey seam, the damp that Faro could coax into a single bead on a good day after an hour of breathing. He felt all of it at once, the way you feel the whole of a rope go taut. He felt it swell and shudder and *leap* towards him, as eager as a dog that has been waiting all its life for its master to call it, and the soft seam rock, soaked through, gave way.

It was not loud. That was the thing he would remember. A long, soft, grinding sigh, and a rush of wet stone, and a cough of foul air through the crack that was suddenly not a crack any more but a slumped mass of mud and broken rock. And behind it, beyond it, a crash, and a cry that was cut off.

Then nothing. Only the sea, booming, and the guard's boots, still on the stair, still a long way above, going about their business. They had not even reached the lowest landing.

Edric lay in the dark with his hands on the mud and could not move.

He tasted salt. His mouth was full of it, as if he had swallowed a wave. His hands were so cold that he could not feel the rock under them. And somewhere at the back of his eyes, for the space of a single heartbeat, he saw something that was not his: a city of white stone at the edge of a grey sea, and fire coming down on it from a clear sky, and a woman in a long robe standing on a quay with her hands raised to the water, screaming.

Then it was gone.

---

Faro was sitting bolt upright on his pallet when Edric came out of the tunnel. His face in the grey light was the colour of the chalk on the wall.

"I felt it," he said. "Gods of the deep. I felt it from here. The whole rock moved." He looked at Edric's hands. They were still shaking. They were wet to the wrist, though Edric had not touched any water. "What did you do?"

"The seam gave way," said Edric. "It was the damp. It was soft rock. It gave way."

"The seam gave way," Faro repeated. "With your hands on it."

"He was going to shout. The guard was on the stair. He was going to give them the chisel. He was going to—"

"And the thief?"

Edric said nothing.

The old man closed his eyes. He sat like that for a long time.

"You reached for it," he said at last, very quietly. "In anger. In the dark, with your back against a wall, in hate. And it came." He opened his eyes, and there was something in them that Edric had never seen there before and would see there, afterwards, every time the old man looked at him: something wary. "Three years I've sat with you in the stillness and you've raised nothing. Not a drop. And you hate one frightened boy for the space of a heartbeat and the whole seam comes down."

"I didn't mean to."

"No. I don't suppose you did." Faro looked down at his own thin hands. "That's what I'm afraid of."

---

The guard on the lowest landing found the rockfall at dusk, when number three hundred and ninety's morning bowl was still full in the slot. Edric lay with his ear to the wall and heard them talk about it, far below, their voices coming up through the stone.

"Old wall's gone. Whole back of the cell. Look at that."

"He under it?"

"Under something. Gods, the stink of the mud. Soaked right through. Must've been the sea getting in."

"Happens, down here. Governor'll want it bricked."

"Governor'll want a number crossed off and no fuss. Leave him. It's a grave as good as any."

They bricked up the door of three hundred and ninety. Edric heard the trowel, a long time, through the stone. And that was the end of it, as far as the Drowned Keep was concerned.

He did not sleep that night. He lay on his pallet with the salt still in his mouth, and his hands still cold, and he found that what frightened him most was not the cry that had been cut off, and not the thing he had seen behind his eyes, the white city and the fire and the woman screaming. What frightened him most was the memory of how it had felt, in the moment the water leapt towards him. How easy it had been.

How good.

### Later

The longboat came out of the sea-tunnel into the blazing temple, and the Hundred stood on the ledges in their ring of white and gold, and at the edge of the dead pool the King rose and turned and smiled.

And in the moment before Aurelian spoke, in the silence of the great cave, Edric heard someone whistling.

It came from high above, from the darkness of the roof, from the hole where an old rope still hung rotting from a spur of rock: a thin, cheerful, tuneless whistle, the kind a housebreaker makes under his breath while he works a lock. It drifted down through the gold light and the hot sharp air. He knew it. He had heard it through a crack in the rock for seven days, fifteen years ago.

*Evening, gent,* said a voice, quite close to his ear. Light and quick, with the flat vowels of the Lantern Stairs. *Going somewhere?*

He did not turn his head. Behind him, in the longboat, Mira was staring at the roof. Hayda had her hand on her knife. They had heard it too.

*He is in my waters, little one,* said the Deep, from very far away, faint as a pulse, the last whisper of a dying thing. *All the drowned are in my waters. Every one this rock has eaten. You gave him to me. Do you not remember? You called, and I came, and the stone fell, and I took him in.* A pause, slow as a tide. *He has been asking for you.*

*I'll shout,* said the voice in his ear, high and wild and frightened, exactly as it had been. *I swear it. I'll shout.*

And across the dead water, the golden King, who heard nothing, or heard everything, smiled more gently still, and said, "Edric. You came. I hoped you would."

## Report him to the guards for extra food

*Heart − · Tide 0*

The voice came out of the rock about a hand's breadth from his ear, so close and so unexpected that Edric dropped the chisel.

"Evening, gents. Going somewhere?"

There was a crack in the seam beside his face, no wider than two fingers. Through it came a sour breath of cell air and a young man's voice, quick and light, with the flat vowels of the Solace streets.

"Joss. Just Joss. Number three hundred and ninety, lower deep. Been listening four months. Here's how it is, friend. You dig me in with you, or next time my porridge comes, I tell the guard about the moles in lower east."

---

Faro wanted to bring him in. That surprised Edric more than anything.

"Of course I don't trust him," the old man said. "I'd not trust him with a spoon. But a thief in the tunnel is a thief we can watch. A thief on the other side of a wall is a thief we can't." He tugged his beard. "And he's a boy alone in the dark, Edric. I've heard that before, through a wall. I know what it sounds like."

"He's threatening us."

"So would you have, in his place. So would I." Faro shrugged. "We'll widen the crack. It'll cost us a season. Perhaps a year."

Edric said nothing. He went back to his own cell and lay on his pallet with his face to the wall, and did the sums.

A year. Another year, on top of the years already gone, on top of the fused wall that had cost them one already. And another mouth on the same thin porridge, because the thief would come through hungry, and Edric knew, the way he knew the tides, that Faro would give him half his bowl, and Faro was old and thin and could not spare it. And another voice in the tunnel, singing under its breath, scraping when it should pry, and one day, inevitably, scraping when the guard was on the stair.

And at the end of it, on the night, the thief would be in the tunnel with them. And if anything went wrong, if they were caught, if it came to a choice, Edric knew exactly which way a housebreaker from the Lantern Stairs would jump. He had learned that much from Faro's philosophy. *Men are moved by two levers only.*

He lay there a long time. The thing that made up his mind, in the end, was not any of the sums. It was a picture: the guard's lantern in the doorway of Faro's cell, for the fifth time in thirty-four years, and the block out of the wall behind the pallet, and the old man on his knees in the light with the chisel in his hand, looking up.

---

They packed the crack from their side that same night, he and Faro, with wet rubble and grit tamped hard, until it was only rock again. Faro did it because he thought they were buying time to widen it properly from a safer angle. Edric let him think so.

In the morning, when the bowl came through his slot, he caught the guard's sleeve through the gap.

"The lower deep," he said, low and quick. "Three hundred and ninety. He's digging. I can hear him through the floor at night. He's got a spike or a spoon-handle or something. He's been at it for weeks. I thought someone ought to know, before he brings the wall down on all of us."

There was a long pause on the other side of the door. Then the guard laughed.

"Well, well," he said. "Four hundred and eleven's found his tongue. Thought you'd gone the way of the mad priest." The sleeve pulled free. "We'll see."

They saw. Edric heard it that afternoon, through the stone: the door of three hundred and ninety banging open far below, and men shouting, and something heavy dragged across a floor, and Joss's voice, high and incredulous at first, *it wasn't me, there's others, there's moles, I can show you*, and then not incredulous, only high. Then the sound of the lash, which Edric had heard before, on ships, and had never once got used to. Then Joss's voice again, screaming words that were not words. Then nothing.

The guards found a loose stone and a hole behind it and a sharpened spoon-handle, and that was all they found, because the crack on the far side of the hole had been packed with rubble the night before, and looked like rock. They flogged Joss for the spoon-handle, and moved him to a cell on the north side, somewhere far above, where the walls were the old fused stone and nothing could be heard through them at all.

That night he lay on his pallet and listened for the thief, out of habit, the way a man listens for a clock that has stopped. There was nothing. No scrape through the floor, no whistling, no singing under the breath. Only the sea, booming, and the drip of the walls, and his own heart, which seemed louder than it had been. He told himself it was relief.

The next morning, there were two bowls in Edric's slot instead of one. And a heel of bread, grey and hard, laid across the top of them like a payment.

---

He brought half of it through the tunnel to Faro, as a gift. He had not told the old man yet. He had meant to tell him gently, explaining, with all the sums laid out. He had the explanation ready.

He did not need it. Faro was sitting on his pallet when Edric came out of the wall, and he looked at the bread in Edric's hand, and then at Edric's face, and he knew. Faro always knew.

"I heard them," he said. "This morning. Through the floor."

"He'd have sold us," said Edric. "You said so yourself, the first day. A thief who threatens is a thief who sells. I only sold him first. We'd have lost a year, and he'd have—"

"Yes," said Faro. "Yes, he might. And now you've a heel of bread." He did not take it. "I thought I'd taught you that every man wants something, and what he wants tells you what he'll do. I didn't think you'd take it as a recipe."

"He was going to put us back in the dark."

"We're in the dark," said Faro. "We were always in the dark. That boy was in it too. That was the only thing about him that mattered." He looked away, at the chalk on the wall, where yesterday he had begun to write out, in his small neat letters, the first lines of the high hand of the old Tidewarden masters, the script the oldest books were written in, the one he had been saving, he said, until Edric was ready. "I was going to begin that today."

"Then begin it."

The old man was quiet for a long time.

"Not today," he said. "Perhaps not this year. There are things a man teaches because they're useful, Edric, and things he teaches because he trusts the hands they're going into. That's the second kind." He picked up the wet rag and wiped the chalk off the wall in three long strokes, and the old letters were gone. "Eat your bread. You've paid for it."

### Later

The book lay open in his lap on the ledge in the sea-cave under Cristal, in the light of Jac's lantern, with eleven more stone chests of books behind it in the oldest alcoves, and the water singing softly below.

*On the Three Disciplines, and the Ways of Their Teaching.* That much he could read. The title page was in the oldest form of the Weir-tongue, the one Faro had taught him, the one the Deep had spoken in the dark. He turned the page, and then the next, and then sat very still.

The rest of it was in the high hand.

He knew it when he saw it. He had seen the first lines of it once, ten years ago, in chalk on the wall of a cell, for the space of a single morning, before an old man wiped them away with a wet rag. Narrow, looping, interwoven letters, like weed in a current, each one leaning into the next. The script of the masters of the order. He could pick out a word here and there, where the high hand borrowed from the common one. *Stillness*. *Blood*. *Return*. The rest slid off his eyes like a fish in murky water.

*There are things a man teaches because he trusts the hands they're going into.*

"Faro?" said Jac, behind him. "You've gone a funny colour. What's it say?"

"I don't know," said Edric. "I was never taught."

It took him three years. Three years of evenings in the ruined library of the citadel, with the oldest books spread out on a table under a lamp, comparing letter with letter, word with word, the way a man works out a cipher, alone, with no one to tell him when he was wrong. Three years before he could read a page of the high hand without a headache, and longer before he could read it well. There were nights he sat over a single line until dawn, knowing that an old man in a cell could have read it to him in a breath.

And sometimes, late, with the lamp guttering, he would find himself thinking of a heel of grey bread laid across two bowls, and of a voice through a crack in the rock saying *you're real, I half thought I'd made you up*; and he would wonder whether there was a cell on the north side of the Drowned Keep where a man was still lying in the dark with stripes on his back, and whether anyone had ever taught him his letters, and he would find that he could not read the page in front of him at all.
