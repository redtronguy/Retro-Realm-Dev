# Event 06: The First Drop

*Branches from Chapter 10, "The Schooling": the fifth year, when Faro names the man who signed the Sun Warrant and, as the roaring rises in Edric, "the water in the cup began to move".*

*The canon path, in which Edric first raises water in blind anger and then spends three years learning to raise it without hate, continues in [Chapter 10](../../10-the-schooling.md) and [Chapter 11](../../11-the-lock-beneath.md).*

## Calm

*Heart 0 · Tide −*

He did not remember standing up. He remembered the cell, very small, and the walls pressing in, and a roaring in his ears that was not the sea. He remembered a young, still, careful face behind a desk, holding a letter in the fire until the flame reached its fingertips. *I've done you a kindness.*

And he remembered his cup, on the floor beside Faro's pallet, half full of water, beginning to shiver.

He saw it. That was the strange thing, afterwards. In the middle of the roaring, with his fists clenched and his whole body shaking, some small, cold, patient part of him that Faro had been building for five years, stone by stone, like a man building a wall against the sea, saw the water in the cup tremble, and ripple in tight rings from the edges in, and knew what it meant.

*It's there,* that part of him thought. *It's there, and it's coming. All I have to do is let it.*

And then another thought, in Faro's voice, worn smooth by a thousand repetitions in the dark: *In for eight.*

He breathed in.

It was the hardest breath he had ever drawn. It went in against the roaring like a man wading against a rip, and the roaring fought it, and the rings in the cup grew tighter and the water began to slop against the sides. *Valtor. Valtor. Valtor.* The name beat in him like a second heart. He held the breath. *Hold for eight.* He made himself count. One. Two. The water climbed the sides of the cup. Three. Four. He could feel it now, not in the cup but in himself, a great cold eager pressure at the back of his skull, like a dog straining at a leash, like a wave standing up out of a flat sea and waiting only for him to say the word.

Five. Six.

*Out for eight.*

He let the breath go. Slowly. All of it. And with it, a little at a time, as if he were paying out a line to a ship that was pulling too hard, he let go of the roaring. Not the anger. He could not let go of the anger; it was in him like a bone. But the roaring, the reaching, the leap. He let that go, the way you let go of a rope you know will drag you overboard if you hold it a heartbeat longer.

*Hold for eight.*

The rings in the cup grew slack. The water slopped once more, and settled, and lay still.

Edric sat down on the stone floor, very suddenly, because his legs would not hold him. He was shaking from head to foot. His face was wet. He did not know when he had started crying.

---

Faro had not moved. He was sitting on his pallet with his good hands gripping his knees, and he was looking at the cup, and his face was quite white.

"Do you know," he said, very quietly, "what you just did?"

"Nothing," said Edric. His voice came out in pieces. "I did nothing. I was going to, I could feel it, I was going to make it—and I didn't. I didn't do anything."

"Yes," said Faro. "Exactly. You did nothing." He let out a breath that seemed to go on for a long time. "Five years I've sat with you in the stillness, and you've raised nothing. And just now you were a hair's breadth from raising more than I've raised in thirty-five. I felt it. I felt it go up through the rock under us like a bell ringing. It was *there*, Edric. Reaching for you. Begging. And you didn't let it."

"It would have been easy."

"It would have been the easiest thing you ever did." The old man leaned forward. "Listen to me. I'll tell you what I'd have told you if you'd let it come. The Deep is chained and starved and three hundred years angry. A calm mind can barely touch it. An angry one touches it at once, because they're the same, like calls to like. Hate is a tide, Edric. It'll carry you anywhere, very fast. And it will never bring you home." He reached out and took Edric's hands, which were still shaking, and held them. "You just stood on the shore and watched that tide go out without you. I don't know a Tidewarden in all my mother's books who did that the first time it called. Not one."

Edric looked at their joined hands.

"I want to kill him," he said. "Faro, I want to kill him. I want to kill all of them. I can see his face. I can see his hand in the fire."

"I know."

"Then what's the point? What's the point of not reaching, if I still want it?"

"The point," said Faro, "is that wanting is a thing you feel, and reaching is a thing you do. You can't help the first. You can always help the second. That's the whole of it. That's the whole of the discipline, in the end, every book my mother ever read. The rest is breathing." He let go of Edric's hands. "Go back to your cell. Sleep, if you can. Tomorrow we'll sit in the stillness again, and you'll raise nothing again, and the day after, the same. And I'm sorry, because you could have had it tonight."

---

He raised nothing for two more years.

There were nights when he lay on his pallet with the names going round in him, *Dorran Quell, Fenrick Morrow, Cade Rusk, Corvan Valtor*, and felt the pressure at the back of his skull, eager, waiting, and knew that one word would bring it. There were nights when he wept with the wanting of it. He would sit up in the dark and breathe. In for eight. Hold for eight. Out for eight. Hold for eight. Until the names were only names again, and the pressure ebbed, and he could sleep.

Faro never asked. But in the mornings, sometimes, the old man would look at him across the cell for a long moment, and nod, very slightly, as if Edric had done something.

And in the spring of the seventh year, on a day like any other, sitting cross-legged on the wet stone floor of Faro's cell with his eyes closed and his hands open, Edric breathed himself into stillness, and listened, and heard it.

Not the booming. Something under the booming. Very faint, and very far, and very large, like a second voice under the first in a choir. And what it felt like, when he let himself feel it, was not rage. He had expected rage. He had felt the edge of the rage, two years ago, straining at its leash at the back of his skull. This was not that. This was pain. A grief so old and so huge that it had no shape at all, the way the sea has no shape, and under the grief, a terrible, terrible patience.

He did not open his eyes. He let the tears run down his face, and they were not his tears, and he did not wipe them away. He reached, gently, with nothing in him at all but a kind of grave attention, like putting a hand into a pool to touch a fish without frightening it.

On the stone beneath his palm, a film of damp crept together into a bead. It trembled. It rose. It hung in the air below his hand, turning slowly, catching the grey light from the window like a tiny moon.

He held it for a count of eight, and set it down. It did not fall. He set it down, on the stone, whole, and let it go, and it lay there, round and bright, for a long moment before it spread.

---

Faro was weeping into his beard.

"My mother did that," he said, "the first time. Set it down instead of dropping it. She was six." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "Nobody else I ever heard of. Nobody."

"I'm twenty-six," said Edric.

"And she had a sea that wasn't screaming." The old man looked at the damp spot spreading on the stone. "What did you hear? Under the booming. Tell me true."

Edric thought about it.

"It hurts," he said. "That's all. It hurts, and it's been hurting so long it doesn't remember anything else. It isn't angry, Faro. Or it is, but the anger's on top. Underneath, it's only hurt."

Faro looked at him for a long time with his very blue eyes.

"That's what my mother used to say," he said at last. "That the anger's on top. Nobody reaches the bottom of it any more. Nobody's calm enough." He pushed himself up on his bad knees, wincing. "Well. That changes the lessons. I was going to begin with Riptide, because it's the simplest. Force. Moving water. But you'll be slow at Riptide, I think. You'll be slow at everything that pushes. Give me your hand."

He took Edric's hand and laid it, flat, on his own left knee, over the swollen, knotted joint that had kept him off his feet for thirty years.

"There's water in there," he said. "Too much of it, in the wrong places. That's what an old man's knee is. A tide that came in and never went out. Breathe. Find the stillness. Then feel for it. Don't push. Never push. Only ask it, very politely, to go where it belongs."

Edric breathed. In for eight. He found the stillness, which came more easily now, as if a door had been oiled. He felt for the knee under his palm and found it: not as flesh, not as bone, but as water, slow and thick and sullen, pooled where it should not be, aching. He asked it, very politely, to go.

It went. Not much. A little.

Faro let out a long breath through his nose, and closed his eyes, and for the first time in all the years Edric had known him, the lines of pain around his mouth went slack.

"Mend," said the old man softly. "That's what they called it, in Undertow. The Wardens who drew sickness out instead of pulling ships down. There haven't been any for three hundred years." He opened his eyes. "You'll never be fast, Edric. You'll never be terrible. But gods below, you might be the first healer the sea has had since the Drying."

### Later

The blighted things came ashore at Cristal on a moonless night in the third year of the rebuilding, out of the black water below the citadel stair: grey, slick, many-jointed shapes the size of dogs, with eyes like drowned lanterns and a smell of rot, crawling up the shingle towards the new houses.

There were eleven men on the watch that night. Edric had seen the Radiants' reports of what happened on the mainland when the things came ashore: a village gone in a night, the houses empty in the morning and the doors hanging open. He stood on the shingle at the head of the watch with a borrowed boat-hook in his hands, because he had nothing better, and reached for the sea.

He could not drown them. He knew it before he tried. Faro had been right, in the cell, a lifetime ago: he was slow at everything that pushed. He could feel the water around the things, the black sick water they had crawled out of, and the water in them, thick and wrong and full of pain, but when he tried to make it strike, to rise up and smash them back into the surf, it came to him like a wave coming up a long, shallow beach, and spent itself, and slid back.

So he did the other thing.

He breathed. In for eight. He let the boat-hook fall on the shingle. He walked forward alone, down the beach, towards the nearest of them, with his hands open, and found the stillness, and listened under the rot and the hunger to what was at the bottom of the thing. It was pain. It was always pain. A creature of the sea that had been made sick by a sick sea, crawling up out of the water towards warmth and light because it hurt and did not know why.

He reached for it, gently, as if into a pool, and asked the pain to be still.

It stopped. It sank down on the shingle like a dog lying down. Behind it, one by one, the others sank down too, eleven, twenty, thirty of them, until the whole beach was covered with grey shapes lying quiet in the dark, and the only sound was the surf, and the hiss of thirty creatures breathing.

It took him until dawn to send them back into the sea. By then he could not feel his hands, and his own chest ached with a sick, borrowed weight, as if he had breathed in the blight himself. One of the watch, a Weir lad named Daveth, had been bitten on the leg before Edric reached the beach, and lay feverish for a week, and walked with a limp ever after.

But the next new moon, when the watch went down to the shingle with their lanterns and their boat-hooks, fewer things came. And the one after that, fewer still. And the Weir-folk on Cristal began to say, among themselves, in the old tongue, that the Count did not fight the sea. He talked to it.

## Anger

*Heart 0 · Tide +*

He lay on his pallet that night and did not sleep.

He lay with his eyes open in the dark and said the names over to himself, one by one, as if learning them for an examination. *Dorran Quell. Fenrick Morrow. Cade Rusk. Corvan Valtor.* And under him, under the stone, the sea boomed, slow and deep, and for the first time in five years it seemed to beat in time with his own heart.

*Hate is a tide. It will carry you a very long way, very fast. And it will never bring you home.*

He thought about Faro's face, white, looking at the spilled water on the stone. He thought about the old man's thin hands holding his. He thought about the years and years of slow, calm, patient practice that Faro had promised him, at the end of which, perhaps, he might raise a single drop without hating anything. He was twenty-four. He had been in this place five years. He had sat in the stillness every day of the last three and raised nothing, not a ripple, not a bead, while the sea screamed under him and the men who had put him here grew older and richer in their beds.

And tonight, in one moment, with one name, he had made the water jump out of the cup.

*Home,* he thought. *What home? There's no home. There's a cell, and a tunnel that ends in black glass, and an old man who's going to die.*

He reached out in the dark and found the bucket in the corner, the one they used for washing, a quarter full of stale water. He put his hand on its rim. And he did not breathe, or count, or seek the stillness. He thought of a young, still, careful face behind a desk, and a letter curling black in the fire, and a voice saying *I've done you a kindness*. He let the roaring come.

The water in the bucket stood up.

---

He practised every night after that, in his own cell, with the stone block in the wall and the tunnel dark between him and Faro.

He never needed long. That was what astonished him. Five years of sitting in the stillness had given him nothing. Now he only had to lie on his pallet and say a name, and let himself see the face that went with it, and the pressure came at once to the back of his skull, eager and enormous, like a dog that has waited all its life for its master to whistle. The water came to it. The water came the way iron filings come to a lodestone.

Within a month he could lift the whole quarter-bucket in a single shivering column and hold it at the height of his chest. Within two he could make the sweating walls of his cell run with water from floor to ceiling, in sheets, like rain on a window. Within three, lying at the end of the tunnel in the dark with the soft grey seam in front of him, he discovered that he could take the damp that lived in the rock and make it into an edge, a thin hard sliver of water no wider than a hair, and drive it into the seam along the grain, and the rock would split away in a slab as large as his hand.

He dug more in that month than he and Faro had dug in a year.

He told Faro it was a better seam. The old man, measuring on the chalk plan with his thumb, said nothing. He only looked at the plan for a long time, and then at Edric, and then went back to his poetry.

---

There was a price. He noticed it, the way a man notices a draught.

He was cold. Not the cold of the cell, which he had long since stopped feeling, but a deeper cold that lived in his hands and the soles of his feet and the hollow of his chest, and did not go away even when he wrapped himself in the blanket. He tasted salt, sometimes, for hours after, as if he had swallowed seawater. And he dreamed. He had never dreamed much in the Keep; there had been nothing to dream of. Now he dreamed every night, and the dreams were not his. A city of white stone at the edge of a grey sea, and fire falling on it from a clear sky. Ships burning in a harbour. A woman in a long robe on a quay, with her hands raised to the water, screaming a name he did not know. And under it all, every night, the pressure at the back of his skull, patient and enormous, waiting for him to wake up and call it again.

And one morning, crawling through the tunnel with the porridge, he came out behind Faro's pallet and found the old man doubled over in a fit of coughing, the kind that left him grey and shaking for an hour afterwards. And Edric stood there with the bowl in his hand and watched him, and waited for it to stop, and thought, quite calmly, that the lesson would be late today.

It was only afterwards, back in his own cell, that he understood what he had felt. Nothing. He had watched the only friend he had in the world choke, and felt nothing at all except impatience.

He sat on his pallet with his cold hands between his knees for a long time.

---

Faro came through the wall that night.

He had not crawled the tunnel in years. His knees would not bear it. But Edric woke in the dark to the grind of the stone block, and a shower of grit, and the old man's breath, ragged and painful, and sat up to find Faro kneeling on the floor of his cell with one hand pressed flat to the wet stone.

The stone was not wet. It was running. A thin sheet of water was sliding down every wall of the cell from ceiling to floor and pooling, in a ring, around the pallet where Edric had lain: around and around, slowly, the way water goes round a drain. He had been dreaming. He had not known.

"So," said Faro. He did not get up. "A better seam."

"Faro—"

"I felt it the first night. You think I didn't? I've lived on top of the Deep for thirty-five years, boy. I feel it when a gull lands on the roof." The old man's voice was quite even, and that was worse than if he had shouted. "I waited for you to tell me. I thought, he'll come to it on his own. He'll get frightened, and he'll stop, and he'll come and tell me. You didn't."

"It works." Edric heard his own voice, cold and flat, and did not like it, and could not change it. "Calm doesn't. Three years I sat in the stillness and got nothing. This works. I can split the seam with it. We'll be under the fused wall by next spring. We'll be *out*, Faro. Isn't that what you wanted?"

"I wanted you out," said Faro. "I didn't want *it* out, wearing your face." He looked at the water running down the walls. "Do you know what's on the other end of that rope you're pulling? I told you. Three hundred years of rage. You think you're drawing water. You're drawing *it*. A little at a time, into you, every time you say a name. You feel the cold? You taste the salt? Those aren't the price, Edric. Those are the *change*. That's what it feels like to become something else, one drop at a time." His voice cracked. "I watched you this morning. While I coughed. I saw your face."

Edric said nothing.

"I can't stop you," said Faro. "I'm old and I'm weak and I can't even crawl through a wall without it costing me a week. I can't stop you reaching. I can only ask you, as the only friend you have left in the world, not to. And if you won't stop, then at least let me teach you what you're doing, so you don't do it blind." He put out his hand, and Edric saw that it was shaking. "Will you give me that much?"

Edric looked at the hand for a long time. Then at the water, running down the walls, going round and round the pallet, patient as a tide.

"Teach me," he said. "But I'm not stopping."

The old man closed his eyes.

"No," he said. "No. I didn't think you would."

### Later

The child was seven, a fisherman's daughter from the new houses below the citadel, and she had the blight-cough. She lay in the hospital on Cristal under a blanket with her lips gone blue and her chest going in and out like a bird's, and her mother knelt by the cot and looked up at the Count with a face that had nothing left in it but asking.

He had done it before, at Grey Point, on the coast. He knew how it went. You found the stillness, and listened for the sickness in the water of the body, and drew it out, gently, into yourself.

He knelt by the cot and put his hand on the girl's chest and breathed. In for eight. Hold for eight.

Nothing came.

He tried again. He found the edge of the stillness, a thin, brittle, unfamiliar place he had visited perhaps a dozen times since the Keep, and reached from it, gently, as Faro had tried for three years to teach him, as if into a pool. There was nothing there. Only the girl's small hot body under his hand and the rattle in her chest, getting slower.

The mother made a sound.

So he did what he always did. He thought of a young, still, careful face behind a desk, holding a letter in the fire. He said the name in his head. *Valtor.*

The sea came like a fist.

It came up through him so fast and so hard that the lamp on the wall guttered and the water in the jug by the cot leapt out onto the floor. He felt the sickness in the girl's chest, thick and grey and clinging, and he did not draw it; there was no drawing, with this; he tore it out, the way you tear a weed out of wet ground, roots and all. The child arched off the cot and screamed. The blight came into him in a black cold rush, and he swallowed it down, and the Mark under his collar burned like ice, and for the space of a heartbeat, at the back of his eyes, he saw the white city again and the fire falling and the woman on the quay.

Then it was over. The girl lay on the cot, breathing, pink-lipped, alive, staring up at him with eyes as round as coins.

"Thank you," said her mother. "Thank you, my lord. Thank you."

The girl did not say anything. She shrank back against the wall, away from his hand, and would not look at him; and when he went out, he heard her begin to cry, softly, not from pain.

He walked back up the citadel stair in the dark with the blight cold in his chest and the salt in his mouth, and he made himself look at the thing he had felt in the moment the sea came, the thing he had felt every time since a bucket of stale water stood up in a cell in the Drowned Keep. Not pity. Not the girl at all. Only the rush of it, the great cold glorious rush, and the name in his mouth, and how sweet it had been.
