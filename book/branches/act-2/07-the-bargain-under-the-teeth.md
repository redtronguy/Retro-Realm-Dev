# Event 07: The Bargain Under the Teeth

## "Give me everything"

*Heart 0 · Tide ++*

He was quiet for a long time, in the dark. Or perhaps it was no time at all.

He thought of Faro. He tried to. He tried to hold the old man's face in front of him, the blue eyes and the white beard and the useless arm in his lap, and hear him say *hate is a tide*. But the face would not stay. It kept sliding away, the way Mira's had slid away in the fifth year, and in its place came the other face, the one he had said over in the dark ten thousand times: young, still, careful, behind a desk, holding a letter in the fire.

And then the others. Dorran at the rail, smiling with only his teeth. Fenrick by the door, white and wet. Cade with his face in his hands. And behind them, behind all of them, in a place he had never let himself look, an old man at a window on Ropewalk Lane, looking out at the harbour every day for a ship that did not come, getting lighter and lighter in his good coat until there was nothing left in it at all.

Faro had died with half his face fallen, in the dark, under the sea, on a pallet of mouldy straw, after thirty-eight years. *Wait,* he had said. *And hope.*

Edric had waited. He had waited fourteen years.

*How much can you give me?* he said.

The eye considered him. He felt the vast dark around him go very still, the way the sea goes still in the moment before a squall.

*You ask the question,* said the Deep slowly, *that the young always ask. The old one taught you the other one. I heard you ask it. And now you ask this.*

*How much?*

*All of it,* said the Deep. *All that I am that is not chained. Three hundred years of tide, held back. No listener has asked it since the oldest days, and of those who asked, none lived long. They burned. They drowned standing up. They became me, and walked into the sea, and did not come back out.* The eye drew closer. *But you are not as they were. You have my waters in you already. You lifted my sea when the old one fell. You might hold it. For a while.*

*Then give me everything,* said Edric. *Everything you can spare. I'll carry it. I'll carry all of it, to the men who did this, and then to the one who did it to you. And I won't stop until there's nothing left of them.*

The Deep did not laugh. He had half expected it to. Instead there was a long, slow pressure, like a tide turning, and in it something he would only understand much later, when it was much too late to matter. It was not approval. It was hunger.

*Yes,* said the Deep. *Oh, yes, little one. Yes.*

---

It came in over his heart, and it did not stop there.

He had braced for a burning. It was worse than a burning, and colder. It was the whole of the sea at the bottom of the world coming through a door the width of a man, and the door was him. It went out from his heart in lines, branching like rivers, like the veins of a leaf, across his chest and down his arms to the wrists and into the palms. It went up his throat. It went over the line of his jaw and up across his cheek, and he felt it there, an ice-cold brand laid along the bone, and up over the temple and into the roots of his hair. It went into his eyes. He felt them fill with it, the way a cup fills.

And with it came the rest.

Memories. Not his. A thousand of them, a hundred thousand, pouring in so fast he could not hold any one long enough to see it. The white city and the fire falling. A girl of nine screaming in a fishing village in the north. Ships, ten thousand ships, going down in ten thousand storms, and the sea not caring, and then caring. A golden man kneeling at the edge of a black pool with his hands in the light. Three hundred years of being drunk from, every hour, every day. And under all of it, not grief, not pain, though those were there, but rage. Rage so old and so deep that it had no edges, the way the sea has no edges. Rage that did not ask whether the cliff deserved to fall.

He opened his eyes.

He was in the water again, falling past the black flank of the mountain under the Teeth, with the iron ball dragging at his ankle and his lungs full of sea. He did not think *let go*. He did not need to think anything. The rope did not part; it exploded, in a burst of white water that drove the iron ball down towards the chains like a stone from a sling.

He did not think *up*. He simply went.

---

He came out of the sea in a column of water forty feet high.

It held him at the top of it, in the howling dark, in the rain, with the storm screaming round him, and he stood on it as a man stands on a hill, and looked at the Drowned Keep.

It was so small. That was what he thought. The great black rock, the walls, the tower, the four hundred stairs he had counted inside a sack. The red lantern burning on the tower top, that had burned there for three hundred years. It was a child's toy on a rock, and he was standing on the sea.

He reached. He did not have to breathe, or count, or find any stillness. The sea was in him now, and it came the way his own arm came when he lifted it. A swell rose out of the dark beyond the Teeth, and then another behind it, and another, a long black hill of water as high as the walls, walking in towards the rock.

He could feel the cells inside. He could feel the water in them, in the sweating stone, in the slop buckets, in the bodies of the men lying on their pallets in the dark, and in the guards on the walls, and in the fat grey governor asleep in his bed. He could feel it all, every drop, the way you feel your own fingers. He could close his hand.

*Yes,* said the Deep, very softly, somewhere behind his eyes.

The black hill of water reached the foot of the Teeth and went up the cliff.

It went up, and up, white at the crest, and broke against the outer wall a dozen yards below the battlements with a sound like the end of the world, and the whole rock shook, and the red lantern on the tower swung wildly on its chain and went out.

Edric stood on his column of water and watched the spray fall back, slowly, in the dark, and felt a joy so huge and so cold that it was like being struck.

And somewhere, at the back of it, very small, he heard an old man say: *There are other men in there. Men like you. Men who did nothing.*

He let the next wave go. It sank back into the sea without breaking. He let the column under him go, and fell, and lay in the heaving black water with the rain on his face, and the joy went slowly out of him and left him shaking.

*Not yet,* he told the thing behind his eyes. *Not them.*

*Not yet,* the Deep agreed, quite gently. *There is time, little one. We have all the time in the world.*

He turned his back on the Teeth and asked the sea to take him away, and it took him so fast that the wind tore the breath from his mouth.

---

He woke to a boot in his ribs, and then to the click of a pistol being cocked.

"*Oi.* Don't you move. Don't you so much as blink."

He opened his eyes. It was full day, bright and blue and blinding. A short, stocky woman with a mass of curly red hair tied back with fishing line was standing over him with a cutlass in one hand and a pistol in the other, and the pistol was pointed at his face.

"Who are you?" said Jac Teague. "*What* are you? This is Gull Reef. There's nothing for thirty miles. There was a storm last night that'd sink a warship, and I saw a light out on the water in the middle of it, a blue light, going past like a comet. And now here you are, without a mark on you." She swallowed. "Without a mark on you except *that*."

He put his hand to his face. The skin of his cheek was cold under his fingers, colder than the morning, and he could feel the lines in it, raised very slightly, like veins.

"Shipwreck," he said. His voice was a croak.

"Shipwreck." Jac did not lower the pistol. On the deck of the black cutter in the cove behind her, half a dozen men were not leaning on the rail. They were standing very still, and two of them had crossbows. "My old gran used to tell stories," said Jac, "about men who came up out of the sea with the sea written on their faces. You know what she said to do if you ever met one?"

"No."

"Nor do I. I never listened." She looked at him for a long, long moment. And then something in her face changed, the same resigned shrewdness that comes to anyone who has spent their life finding strange things on beaches. "Can you work a sail?"

"Yes."

"You'll work it below decks, then, or in a hood, or at night. I'll not have my crew looking at that all day." She lowered the pistol, a little. "I'm a man short since Benno went off the bowsprit. I'm not fussy. What do I call you?"

He stood up on the sand, swaying. "Faro," he said.

He did not ask to see his reflection. He did not need to. When he leaned over the stern rail of the *Slip* that evening, alone, and looked down into the still water of the cove, he saw a gaunt bearded stranger with grey eyes gone dark as the bottom of the sea, and over the whole left side of his face, from the jaw to the temple, a branching pattern of pale shimmering blue, like light on water, moving very slightly when he moved.

He could not button any shirt high enough to hide it.

### Later

The Countess Morrow saw him across the ballroom of the Marshal's palace in Solace on the night the Count of Cristal was first received, and the glass in her hand stopped halfway to her mouth.

He had been holding the stillness for six hours. He had held it in the carriage, and on the steps, and through the endless line of bows. It was like holding a breath at the bottom of the sea; it cost him something every minute, a thin cold trickle of the Deep into his blood, like a tap left running, and the Mark on his face burned under it like frost on a window. To the hundred people in the ballroom, the Count of Cristal was a handsome foreign nobleman with a pale, grave face. To himself, in every mirror he passed, he was a man with the sea written across his cheek, holding a pane of still water in front of it with both hands.

Mira did not see the Count. He knew it the moment their eyes met. She saw through the stillness the way she had once heard his step on the walkway a hundred yards off: completely, and without trying. She looked at him, and her face went white, and then her eyes went to his cheek, to the place where the Mark lay under the mask, and stayed there.

She found him on the terrace an hour later, alone, in the dark, where the stillness could slip a little.

"Your face," she said. It was the first thing she said to him in nineteen years. Not his name. "Edric. Your face. What did you let it do to you?"

"It's nothing. It's a mark. It doesn't hurt."

"Don't lie to me." She was shaking. "I've seen that. I've seen it before. Gran had a drawing, in the box under her bed, from her gran's gran. A man who came up out of the sea at Saltmere in the old days with lines on his face like that, and walked into the town, and the town wasn't there in the morning." She reached up, slowly, as if towards a dog that might bite, and touched his cheek with her fingertips, and snatched her hand back. "You're *cold*. You're cold like the bottom of the Weir in winter."

From somewhere behind his eyes, slow and fond and patient, the Deep said: *She is afraid of us, little one. They will all be afraid of us, in the end.*

Mira was looking at his eyes now. Whatever she saw there made her step back, one step, and then another, until her back was against the balustrade.

"Who's in there?" she whispered. "Edric. Who's in there with you?"

## "Only enough to live"

*Heart 0 · Tide −−*

He was quiet for a long time, in the dark. Or perhaps it was no time at all.

He thought of Faro. The old man was simply there, suddenly, as clear as if he were sitting on the pallet in his ragged robe. *It's not kind, Edric. It's not cruel either. It's hungry.* And: *Something that was mostly sea. Mostly anger. Mostly the Deep, looking out through a human face.*

He thought of the bead of water rising off the stone under Faro's palm, the first time, in the fourth month: so small, so hard won, trembling, the size of a pea. *That's all I have. A drop of water and a sore head.* And of the old man weeping into his beard because Edric, at twenty-seven, had done the same.

He thought of the four names. He let himself think them, all four, one after another, and felt the pressure at the back of his skull, eager, enormous, waiting to hear what he would ask for.

And he thought: *If I take its strength to break them, I'll be its strength. And then whose hands will they be, when they close?*

*I don't want your strength,* he said, slowly, in the old tongue, pushing each word out into the dark like a stone into water. *I don't want to be stronger than any of my kind. I don't want your waters to move at my asking. I want to live. That's all. Enough to reach the air. Enough to see the sky again, once, and stand on land. Only enough to live.*

The dark went utterly still.

It was not the stillness before a squall. It was a different kind, one he had never felt from the sea before, in all the years of listening through the stone: the stillness of something very large that has stopped, in the middle of what it was doing, and turned its whole attention towards a sound it did not expect.

*Say it again,* said the Deep.

*Only enough to live.*

The eye looked at him. It looked at him for a long time, the way Faro had looked at a puzzle, turning it to the light, examining every side; and he felt it go through him again, through all his years, as if it were looking for something it had missed the first time.

*Interesting,* said the Deep.

---

*No listener has asked me for so little,* it said, *since before the golden one. Since before the chains. In the oldest days, before the cities, there were some who came down to the water and asked me for nothing at all but to be heard. Those I listened to.* A slow pressure, like a tide easing. *Since then they have all asked for more. Strength. Storms. Fish. Vengeance. The golden one asked for everything, and took it, and is taking it still. And now you. With your death in your lungs and my waters already in you. You ask for a breath.*

*Is it enough?*

*It is enough to live,* said the Deep. *It is not enough to win. You understand that? You will be weak, little one. You will not break the men who put you in the dark. Not with my strength. You will have to break them with your own, if you break them at all. And the golden one has three hundred years of me in him.*

*I understand.*

*Then here is your breath,* said the Deep. And then, more slowly, as the eye began to close: *There is another way to break a chain than by strength. The old one knew it, once, and forgot. His mother knew it. Perhaps you will remember. I find that I would like to see.*

The eye closed.

The sea came into him: not in a flood, not in a brand of cold across his chest, but in a single thread. He felt it go in over his heart, a line of cold no longer than his finger, as fine as a hair, and stop there.

That was all.

---

He opened his eyes in black water, falling, with the iron ball dragging at his ankle.

He was not drowning. Not quite. The sea in his lungs had stopped hurting, the way a wound stops hurting when you are past the worst of it, and he understood, dimly, that he had been given time. Not much. A few long moments, borrowed.

He could not cut the rope with the water. He felt for the sea, around him, and it was there, but faintly, the way Faro's drop had been there: a whisper in a gale. So he did it with his hands. He doubled over and found the rope by feel, the half-cut place where the chisel had sawed before it slipped away, and he took the frayed fibres in his fingers and pulled, and twisted, and tore. Thirteen years of the chisel had made his hands as hard as horn. He tore until his nails broke. The black flank of the mountain went by beside him, and he reached out and caught a spur of rock and dragged the rope across its edge, back and forth, back and forth, and felt the last fibres part.

The iron ball fell away towards the chains.

He swam.

There was no column of water to carry him. There was only a current, very faint, at his back, like a hand laid between his shoulder blades: not pushing, only there. He swam up through the black water with his lungs full of sea and the borrowed moments running out, and the surface was so far above that he stopped believing in it, and swam anyway, because there was nothing else to do.

He came up into the storm with no breath left to take.

The sea came out of him in a great choking gout, and the air went in, and he went under again, and came up, and a wave broke over his head and drove him under, and he came up. He did not laugh. He did not have the strength. The Teeth were a black shape behind him in the rain. A spar of wood went past him on the face of a wave, some wreck's broken yardarm, and he caught at it, and missed, and caught it, and held on.

He did not know how long he held on. All night. The storm threw him about like a cork. He lost the spar twice and found it twice. And all night, faint, patient, at his back, the current held him: never carrying him, never lifting him, only turning him, a little, now and then, away from the rocks and towards the west.

At dawn it let him go in shallow water over pale sand, and he crawled up onto Gull Reef on his hands and knees, and did not get as far as the high-water mark before he fell.

---

"He's dead," said a man's voice, a long way off.

"He's not dead. Look, he's breathing. Just." A boot nudged him, quite gently. "*Oi.* You. Dead or alive? Make your mind up."

He could not open his eyes. He tried to say *alive* and nothing came out.

"Gods, look at him," said the woman's voice. "Thin as a rake. Pale as a fish. Hands torn to ribbons. He's been in the water all night, in that." A pause. "Pike, Olaf. Get him aboard. Carefully, you great ox. And get the brandy."

He was ill for nine days in a corner of the *Slip*'s hold, with a fever that came and went like a tide, and in his fever he heard the sea talking: not words, only a slow, curious murmur, like a voice in another room. On the tenth day he woke clear-headed, and asked the nearest man what the date was, and when he heard it, he went still for a long time.

That evening he went up on deck alone and opened his borrowed shirt and looked down at his chest.

There was a single line over his heart, pale blue, no longer than his finger, as fine as a hair. When he moved, it seemed to move too, very slightly, like light on the sea. He touched it. It was cold.

He buttoned the shirt. He did not need to button it to the throat.

### Later

The customs cutter came round the point off the Sorrows at noon, with all sail set and the King's sun on her ensign, and Jac Teague swore at her for a full minute without repeating herself and then turned to the man at her side.

"Well, Warden? Now's when you do the thing."

He had tried, in the months since they found the books. He had stood at the rail on a calm night and breathed, and found the stillness, and reached for the sea the way the old book from Cristal told him the Riptide Wardens had reached for it, and asked for a wave. What came was a ripple. A long, low swell that lifted the *Slip* a hand's breadth and set her down again, and Big Olaf had said, kindly, that it was a very nice ripple.

He tried now. He got a ripple.

They outran the cutter honestly, in the end, by Jac's seamanship and a fair wind and by heaving half the brandy over the side to lighten her. Jac did not speak to him for two days. On the third she came and sat beside him on the hatch and said, "Never mind. I've had worse crew. I had a cook once who couldn't boil water."

He was not good at Riptide. He was not good at anything that pushed. But in the hospital on Cristal, when the fishermen's children came in with the blight-cough, he could sit by a cot and breathe and draw the sickness out of a small chest so gently that the child slept through it. It went into him, the sickness, cold and grey, as it always did. But only a little of the Deep came in with it. The line over his heart barely grew.

And in the oldest alcoves of the sea-cave, in the twelfth stone chest, he found a book bound in sealskin with no title, written in a hand he did not know, and on its first page a single line in the oldest form of the Weir-tongue:

*Those who ask little of the One Below are those it listens to. A chain is not always broken. Sometimes it is sung loose.*

He read it by lamplight, sitting on the ledge above the singing water, three times. And from somewhere very far away and very deep, slow and curious, like a voice in another room, he felt the sea turn over in its sleep and murmur, as it had murmured in his fever, the one word it had said to him at the bottom of the world.

*Interesting.*
