# Event 21: The Courant Correspondent

*Branches from Chapter 21, "Currents of Gold": the morning after the false signal and the note at Dorran's gate, after Edric has caught himself smiling over the bowl and "stopped smiling".*

## The visitor

Bastien brought the card up at ten in the morning, holding it by one corner, the way he held anything he would rather have dropped in the sea.

*Mr Ansel Tarrow. The Solace Courant. The Exchange and the Town.*

"He's in the courtyard," said Bastien. "He says he'll wait. He says he's very good at waiting." He paused. "He's been at the gate since seven. He was at Grey Point yesterday. One of Rook's lads saw him at the inn, asking about a carriage with silver on the harness."

Edric looked at the card for a long moment. Then he took the bowl from the corner of the desk and found the stillness in it, and went down.

Ansel Tarrow was sitting on the edge of the black marble pool with his hat beside him, watching the fountain dance. He was perhaps thirty: a thin, untidy, quick-eyed man in a coat that had been good two winters ago, with ink on his cuffs and on the side of his middle finger where the pen rested, and a notebook on his knee. He did not get up when the Count came down the stair. He finished writing a line, and closed the notebook on his thumb, and then he got up.

"My lord Count. Thank you for seeing me. I'll be quick, because I've a column to write, and I'd like to know before I write it whether I'm going to be sued or shot." He smiled, a crooked, tired, likeable smile. "I write the exchange column. Prices, rumours, who's lending to whom. Dull work. But you notice things, doing it. You notice, for instance, when a steward with a scar across his throat takes a cart of gold out of the house of Quell every week for a month, under armed guard. Three million eight hundred thousand marks, by my count. You notice when the Lord Treasurer stops coming to the exchange in person and starts sending his chief clerk. You notice when a mirror fault at Grey Point costs the house of Quell a million marks in an afternoon, and nobody else in Solace loses a copper." He opened the notebook. "And when you go down to Grey Point yourself, because you're curious, you find the keeper gone and his rooms swept, and an innkeeper who remembers a tall gentleman in black who stayed two nights, a week apart, and had a carriage with silver on the harness, and paid in Port Jasmine gold." He looked up. "Tampering with the King's mirrors is treason, my lord. They hang you for it. On the Sunspire steps, at noon."

The fountain played. Edric said nothing.

"I don't know why," said Tarrow. "That's what I can't make add up. You don't need money. You've not made any on this; you've lost it, if anything, drawing on a bank that's going to fail. So it's not greed. It's something personal, between you and Quell. I'd very much like to know what. But I don't need to know, to print what I've got." He tapped the notebook. "Thursday's paper. Front page. Unless you'd like to give me a reason not to."

He said it quite lightly. But Edric felt the water in him: the quick pulse in his wrists, the dampness of his palms, the pull of him. Ambition, bright and hungry and a little desperate. And under it, fear: real fear, because he knew exactly what kind of man he might be talking to, and he had come anyway.

He was brave, then. Or poor. Or both.

## Bribe him

"You have a family, Mr Tarrow," said Edric.

Tarrow went still.

"I don't threaten families," said Edric. "I only notice them. You have ink on your cuffs and your coat is two winters old, and you've been at my gate since seven without breakfast. You work for a paper that pays you, I'd guess, four hundred marks a year to print what the Throne allows. And you've found the best story of your life, and you know perfectly well that if you print it, the editor will have it read by a man from the Sunspire first, and the man from the Sunspire will ask how a hack from the exchange column came to know so much, and you'll be lucky to keep your post." He went to the small table by the pool, where Bastien had left wine, and poured two glasses. "So you didn't come here to print it. You came here to sell it. I'm only saving us both an hour of pretending otherwise."

Tarrow looked at the glass in his hand. He did not drink.

"Ten thousand marks," said Edric.

The water in the man leapt. He felt it the way you feel a fish take a line.

"For what?" said Tarrow, after a moment. His voice was quite steady. "My silence? My notebook?"

"Your notebook, which you'll leave with me. Your silence, which you'll keep. And your column, which you'll go on writing, exactly as before, about prices and rumours and who's lending to whom." Edric sat down on the rim of the pool. "And once a month, a letter to this house, telling me what the Street of Gold is saying that it doesn't print. For which I'll pay you another thousand. Every month. For as long as I'm in Solace."

"Twenty thousand," said Tarrow.

"Fifteen."

"Done." Tarrow drank the wine in one swallow, as if he needed it, and set the glass down, and for a moment his thin face did something complicated: relief, and shame, and a sort of bitter amusement at himself. "Do you know, my lord, I had a speech ready. About the freedom of the press. About the duty of the *Courant* to the people of Solace. I'd practised it at the gate." He laughed. "You didn't give me the chance."

"You'd have made it very well," said Edric. "And then you'd have taken the money."

"Yes," said Tarrow. "I expect I would." He put the notebook down on the rim of the pool, between them, and stood up, and put on his hat. At the foot of the stair he turned. "May I ask one thing? Not for print."

"You may ask."

"Is he worth it? Quell. Whatever he did to you. Is it worth all this?"

Edric looked at him without expression, and after a moment the journalist touched his hat, and went.

When he had gone, Edric opened the notebook. It was very good. Dates, sums, names, the innkeeper's description of the tall man in black, a sketch of the carriage's harness. A careful, clever, honest piece of work, by a man who would have made a good magistrate, or a good spy.

He fed it to the brazier in the study, page by page, and watched it burn, and felt nothing much at all. It had cost him less than one of his dinners. The money did not matter. It never did.

What stayed with him, afterwards, was how easy it had been. How quickly the man had folded. How little Edric had needed to reach. He had seen the price in him the moment he walked in, the way you see a fish's shadow under clear water, and he had named it, and that was all. Fifteen thousand marks and a thousand a month, and Ansel Tarrow, who had come to the house with a speech about the freedom of the press, had gone away with a pension and a master.

*Everyone has a price,* he thought, and did not like the voice he thought it in. It was not his. It was the voice of a man in a warm study in Saltmere, holding a letter to a fire.

### Later

The letters came every month, as agreed, in a neat, slanting, intelligent hand, and they were worth every mark. Tarrow had a nose for the Street of Gold that Bastien's agents never matched; he heard things in coffee houses that nobody else heard, and he wrote them down drily and precisely, with now and then a line of sour wit that made Edric, against his will, look forward to them. When the bank of Quell fell, Tarrow's letter had told him the day before the queue began.

The last letter came in the week the King's fleet sailed.

It was not in the slanting hand. It was a copy of the *Courant*, folded, with no note. On the front page, under a woodcut of the Sunspire with its rays blazing, was a long, eloquent, beautifully written article, unsigned, about the sorcerer who had come to Solace under a false name and a false title. How he had drained the house of Quell by witchcraft. How he had broken the King's own mirror at Grey Point to rob honest men on the exchange, and spirited away its keeper. How the storm at noon on the Street of Lanterns had been his doing, and the deaths in the High Justicar's house, and the Lord Marshal's ruin. How he kept a nest of heretics and smugglers on an island in the west. It named the island. It named the date of the false signal, and the inn at Grey Point, and the harness with silver on it. It was the story in the notebook, every word of it, rebuilt from memory by the man who had written it, and sold again, to a higher bidder.

Edric read it on the deck of the *Faro*, with the sea pouring through him and Cristal ten days away.

He did not feel angry. He found, examining it, that he felt something closer to recognition. He had taught the man what he was worth. He could hardly complain that someone else had learned the lesson too, and offered more.

## Wipe his memory with Still Water

"I see," said Edric. "May I look at that?"

He held out his hand for the notebook. Tarrow hesitated, and then, because he was clever and knew that a notebook could be copied and a story could be rewritten, and because the Count's hand was so steady and his voice so mild, he gave it to him.

Edric did not open it. He laid it on the rim of the pool, and looked at the journalist, and let the stillness go out of his face, a little. Not all of it. Only enough.

"Sit down, Mr Tarrow," he said. "Look at the water."

Tarrow sat. He did not quite know why. He looked at the black marble pool, at the fountain dancing on it, at the light breaking and re-forming on its surface, and the light was very bright, and the sound of the water was very loud, and then very soft.

*On the Discipline of Still Water. Of the Clouding.* It was in the twelfth chest, in a book bound in grey sharkskin, in a chapter that some Warden long dead had marked, in red ink, in the margin, with a single word: *Do not.* Edric had read it anyway, on the long nights at Cristal. He had told himself it was so that he would know what to guard against.

*Every memory is a current in the waters of the mind. And as a still pool, when a stone falls into it, is troubled, and then grows still again and shows no mark of the stone, so may the Warden, holding his own stillness over another's, make a current in them still, until it is as if it had never run.*

He found the stillness in himself, and went down into it, and reached.

It was not like healing. It was not like the child at Grey Point, drawing sick water out of lungs. It was like putting his hand into a stream in the dark and feeling for one particular stone. The man's mind was full of water, quick and cold and bright, running in a thousand channels. Prices. A woman's voice, scolding, fond. The smell of the press-room at four in the morning. An unpaid tailor. A daughter, very small, asleep with her fist in her mouth. And there, running through it all for six weeks, a bright, hard, eager current: carts of gold on the Street of Gold, a steward with a scar, the exchange in panic, an innkeeper at Grey Point, a carriage with silver on the harness, *this is it, this is the one, this is the story that gets me out*.

He held his stillness over it.

It fought. Not much; Tarrow was not a strong man. But it was his best thing, this current, the thing he was proudest of, and something in him knew it was being taken and clung to it. Edric pressed. He pressed the way you press a hand over a man's mouth. And slowly the bright current slowed, and dulled, and grew still, and went dark, like a stream freezing.

And as it froze, it took the things around it. He felt them go and could not stop them, the way you cannot stop the ice spreading once it starts. A week of the man's life, and then two. Not only the story. A dinner with his wife, on her name day; the joke he had made that had made her laugh. The afternoon his daughter had first said his name. Gone, all of it, into the same dark, silent ice.

Edric pulled his hand back out of the water.

Tarrow was sitting on the rim of the pool, blinking, with his hat in his lap. He looked up at the Count of Cristal, and his face was entirely, pleasantly blank: the face of a man who has walked into a room and forgotten why.

"I'm so sorry, my lord," he said. "I don't. I'm sorry. Have we been introduced? I seem to." He laughed, uncertainly, and put his hand to his forehead. "I'm with the *Courant*. I write the exchange column. I must have come about. About." He stopped. "I'm afraid I've no idea."

"You came to ask whether I'd be banking with the house of Quell," said Edric. "I told you I would. It's a dull story. I doubt it'll make a paragraph."

"No," said Tarrow vaguely. "No, I suppose not." He stood up. He looked at the notebook lying on the rim of the pool, and did not seem to know it was his. "Thank you, my lord. Forgive me. I don't know what's the matter with me today. I've had no breakfast."

Bastien showed him out. Edric stood by the fountain until the gate shut.

Then he went up to the study, and shut the door, and sat down on the floor with his back against it, because his legs would not hold him.

He was cold. He was colder than Grey Point. And there was something in his head that had not been there an hour ago. He could feel it, like a stone in his shoe. A kitchen, low and warm, in a house he had never been in, in the lower town. A woman at a table, shelling peas into a bowl, singing. A song he did not know, about a girl who waited by a mill. He could hear every word. He could smell the peas, and the woman's apron, and the smoke from the range. He could feel, with a small boy's whole body, how much he loved her.

It was not his. It had come back with him, up out of the man's waters, the way the sea's sickness had come up out of the child. *What is sent is not always all returned,* the books said. They did not say that what came back might be someone else's.

He sat on the floor with his arms around his knees and listened to Ansel Tarrow's mother sing, and could not make her stop.

### Later

The *Courant* let Tarrow go before the winter. He had lost his nose for it, the editor said; he forgot things, sources, appointments, which bank had lent to which. He kept coming in at four in the morning, as if for a deadline, and sitting at his desk, and not writing anything. And there were days, the clerks said, when he walked twenty miles down the coast road to Grey Point and back, and could never say why. He stood at the foot of the empty mirror tower, they said, and looked up at it, like a man trying to remember a name.

Edric saw him only once more, months later, on the night before he sailed for Cristal for the last time. He was in a gin-shop near the docks, in a coat that had been good three winters ago, with a small girl asleep against his side. He looked up as the tall man in black passed the window, and his face did a strange thing: went white, and frightened, the way a man's face goes when he hears footsteps on the stair in a dream. He did not know why. He would never know why.

And on the Long Day, on the walls of Cristal, with the mirrors burning and the Tide rising in him like a flood, Edric heard the song again. A girl who waited by a mill. It came up out of the sea in the Deep's own vast, slow voice, every word, as if the god had learned it from him.

*What you take, little one,* said the Deep, *you keep. Did no one tell you? Everything you take, you keep. And so do I.*

## Tell him the truth about the Drying

Edric looked at the journalist for a long moment. At the ink on his cuffs. At the fear under the ambition, and the ambition under the fear, and under both of them, very small and very stubborn, something else, which he had not expected to find and which made him think, unaccountably, of Faro: the itch of a man who wants to know.

"You're right," he said. "It's personal. And you're right that you could print what you have. It would make a stir for a week. The Throne would close the story, and they'd hang me if they could catch me, and you'd have a name on the Street of Gold for a season." He sat down on the rim of the pool. "But it's the wrong story, Mr Tarrow. It's a small story about a rich man and a banker. Would you like the right one?"

Tarrow looked at him warily. "What's the right one?"

"Come upstairs," said Edric.

---

He took him to the study, and locked the door, and took down from the shelf behind the desk a book that he had brought from Cristal in the bottom of a sea-chest, wrapped in oilcloth. It was bound in grey sharkskin, and its pages were vellum, and the writing on them was in a script that no scholar in Solace could read, because the last people who could read it had been killed three hundred years ago.

He read it to him. He translated as he went. And then he told him the rest.

He told him about the Deep. About the Tidewardens, who had kept the balance between the land and the sea for a thousand years. About a prince of the Radiant Order, three hundred years ago, who had bound a god in chains of sunfire and made it the foundation of his immortality, and drank its life through the chains, and had been drinking it ever since. About the Drying: the burning of the Warden houses, the drowning of their children in the harbours of their own cities, the burning of their books, the rewriting of every history in Valemont, until there was nothing left but a story about a Drowned Heresy and a King blessed by the Sun. About the sea, dying slowly, for three hundred years, in chains. Thinner fish runs. Storms at the wrong time. The blighted things that came ashore at night. The cough that took the children first.

And then he told him about the Blight Fund. Six million marks, raised every year from the fishing towns to pay for the beacons and the medicine. Four million of it gone, into Dorran Quell's grain ships and southern loans and Sardh bonds. He showed him the figures. He showed him the shape of the hole, in the Treasury's own numbers, which Bastien's people had copied.

He did not tell him his own name. He did not tell him about Saltmere, or the Keep, or the letter. That was his. But he told him everything else.

It took three hours. When he had finished, Ansel Tarrow sat in the chair across the desk with his notebook closed on his knee, and had not written a word in it for an hour, and his face was grey.

"You can't prove it," he said at last. His voice was hoarse. "The Drying. The King. You can't prove any of it. It's a book no one can read, and the word of a foreigner who rigs the mirrors."

"No," said Edric. "I can prove the Blight Fund. To the mark. The rest you'll have to take on trust. Or go down to the fishing towns and look at the nets, and the beacons that don't light, and the children's graves. And ask yourself why, in a kingdom ruled for three hundred years by a King who never ages and never errs, the sea is dying."

Tarrow was silent for a long time.

"If I print this," he said, "they'll smash the press. They'll hang me. They might hang the editor. They'll certainly come for you."

"Yes."

"And you want me to print it anyway."

"I don't want anything," said Edric. "You came here with a story to sell. I've given you a different one, for nothing. It's yours. Print it or burn it. Or print the one about the mirror, if you'd rather; it's safer, and it's true." He leaned back. "But you asked me why. That's why. It's not between me and Dorran Quell, in the end. It's between all of us and the light at the top of that tower."

Tarrow looked out of the window, at the Sunspire, white against the sky, burning.

"I came here to get rich," he said slowly. "Do you know that? I came here to make you pay me." He laughed, a strange, shaky sound. "I had a speech ready, in case you wouldn't. About the freedom of the press." He looked down at his closed notebook. Then he opened it, and took the pencil out of the spine, and turned to a clean page. "Tell me about the beacons again. Slowly. Which towns."

---

It ran a fortnight later. Not in the *Courant*; the editor read the first page of it and went white and put it in the stove. It ran as a broadsheet, printed in a single night on a little hand-press in a cellar in the fish-market quarter below the harbour, by Tarrow and two apprentices and a Sardhi typesetter who could not read the common tongue and did not care to. Five thousand copies. By six in the morning they were on every corner of the lower town, pushed under doors, pasted on walls, passed from hand to hand in the fish market and the rope-walks and the queues at the bakers.

By eight, the Radiant Order had found the cellar and smashed the press. By nine, they were tearing the broadsheets off the walls. By noon, it was a crime to own one, and everyone in the lower town of Solace had read it, or had it read to them, and nobody had handed one in.

Tarrow was not in the cellar when the Radiants came. He was on a fishing boat with a grey and brown pennant, going west, with his wife and his small daughter and his notebook, because a black-browed man with a scar across his throat had come for them at four in the morning and told them there was a schoolmaster on an island who would very much like to meet a man who could run a press.

Edric heard the tramp of armoured feet on the Street of Lanterns at noon, going past the blue door towards the Sunspire. He stood at the window of the study and watched them go. He knew what he had done. He had put a light on himself. He had told the city, in all but name, that the man who had come with a credit without limit had come with something else too. The Order would remember it. The King would remember it.

But that afternoon, when he walked down through the lower town, as he sometimes did, without a carriage, a fishwife at a stall in the market looked up at him as he passed, at his black coat and his still face, and put two fingers to her lips, and touched them to her forehead. The old Weir sign, from Saltmere. *The tide.* And looked down again at her fish, as if she had done nothing at all.

### Later

The Radiants came for him in the autumn, a month before they had any right to, and a month before he was ready.

There was no proof. There did not need to be. The broadsheet had never named him, but the Order had a long memory and the King a longer one, and a foreign count who appeared from nowhere with a fortune and a ward and an island in the west, in the same season that someone began telling the lower town about the Drying, was a sum that even a novice could add. They watched the house with the blue door. They followed Bastien. They came to the gate with questions, politely, and then less politely, and there were weeks that autumn when Edric could not cross the city without a white and gold shadow two streets behind him, and the plan he had laid for five years had to be bent, and hurried, and in places broken.

But when the King's fleet gathered in the harbour below the cliffs to sail for Cristal, the bakers of the lower town found that they had no bread to sell to the Order's quartermasters. None at all. The coopers had no casks. The rope-walks had no rope. The water-carriers' guild discovered that every cistern on the harbour front was fouled. And on the morning the twelve white ships sailed, the people of the lower town of Solace came down to the harbour in their thousands, and stood along the quays and on the steps and the sea walls, men and women and children, and watched them go.

They did not cheer. They did not throw anything. They stood in perfect silence, every one of them, the way the Weir-folk stand when they will not weep in front of their enemies. And as the flagship passed the end of the harbour mole, someone in the crowd began to sing, very low, an old fishing song from the south coast about the tide coming in. And then someone else. And then all of them.

On the steps of the Order's chapterhouse above the harbour, where she was to take ship two days later, High Radiant Cassia Vell stood and listened to it, and did not give the order to stop them. She had read the broadsheet. She had read it in her cell in the Sunspire, at night, by candlelight, three times, and burned it, and had not slept.
