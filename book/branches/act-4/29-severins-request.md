# Event 29: Severin's Request

*Branches from Chapter 24, "Still Water": the day after old Noll dies of the poisoned glass, when Edric sits back from the bowl thinking "I did this. I gave her the name", and goes to the grey house to see the old man who now has no one to speak for him.*

*The canon path, refusing him and protecting Lyse instead, continues in [Chapter 24](../../24-still-water.md).*

## Grant it

*Heart ± · Tide 0*

There was no one to hold the board.

He found the old man where he always was, in the chair by the window at the top of the grey house, in the afternoon sun, with the rug over his knees. The High Justicar was at the Hall of Justice. His wife was out paying calls, in grey, speaking very feelingly about poor old Noll's seizure. Lyse had gone to the Temple of the Noon to sit with the old servant's body, because nobody else would.

The board was on the table beside the chair, where Noll had left it. Nobody had touched it since.

Edric sat down across from Severin Valtor, and picked it up, and held it the way he had watched the old servant hold it, a hundred times: up, at the height of the old man's eyes, with his finger at the first row.

The dark eyes had been on him since the door opened. They did not leave his face.

"I'm sorry," said Edric. "About Noll."

The old man blinked. Once. Then, before Edric's finger could move, again, fast, impatient, the way a man taps a table. *Go on. Get on with it.*

Edric ran his finger along the rows.

*N-O-L-L.*

*D-R-A-N-K.*

*M-Y.*

*G-L-A-S-S.*

"I know," said Edric.

*F-I-F-T-Y. Y-E-A-R-S.*

The tears came up in the old man's eyes and spilled over, and he could not lift a hand to wipe them, and Edric put down the board and did it for him with his handkerchief, and picked it up again.

---

It took a long time. It always did. The sun moved across the floor while they worked, and Edric spoke each word aloud as it came, the way he had the first time, so the old man would know it had been understood.

*S-H-E. W-I-L-L. T-R-Y. A-G-A-I-N.*

"Yes."

*L-Y-S-E. C-A-R-R-I-E-S. M-Y. T-R-A-Y.*

Edric's finger stopped on the board.

He had seen it, on the stairs, a month ago: the girl coming up with broth and a glass of wine for her grandfather. He had felt the wine. Clean. *For now.*

*E-V-E-R-Y. D-A-Y.*

*E-V-E-R-Y. C-U-P. F-O-R. M-E.*

*T-H-R-O-U-G-H. H-E-R. H-A-N-D-S.*

"I'll tell her," said Edric. "I'll tell her not to carry it. Not to touch anything. I'll find a way to—"

The old man blinked, hard, twice. *No.*

Then he looked at Edric for a long moment, and Edric, who could feel the water in everyone, felt the water in him go very still and very deep, like a man gathering himself at the edge of a cliff. And the eyes moved again, slowly, deliberately, one letter at a time, so that Edric had to wait between each.

*E-N-D.*

*I-T.*

*M-Y. L-I-F-E.*

*Y-O-U.*

He put the board down on his knee.

"No," he said.

The old man looked at him.

"No," said Edric again. "My lord. I can't. You don't know what you're asking." He was surprised to find that his voice was shaking. "You don't know what I've already—" He stopped. He looked at the floor. "Three people are dead in this house because of me. Your son's wife learned the name of that poison from me. In her stillroom, the first night I dined here. I told her what it was and what it did, because I wanted to see what she'd do with it." He made himself look up. "Noll drank your glass because of me. And now you want me to finish the work."

The old man's eyes did not leave his face. They did not change. If anything, Edric thought, there was something in them like a terrible patience, the patience of a man who has had twelve years in a chair to think about every possible thing, and has already thought about this.

He lifted the board again. He could not think what else to do.

*I. K-N-O-W.*

Edric stared at him.

*S-H-E. A-S-K-E-D.*

*Y-O-U. A-N-S-W-E-R-E-D.*

*I. H-E-A-R. E-V-E-R-Y-T-H-I-N-G.*

"Then you know why I can't."

*N-O.*

*T-H-A-T. I-S. W-H-Y. Y-O-U. M-U-S-T.*

---

He argued. He told the old man that if Severin Valtor died, his fortune went to Lyse, and Lyse became the richest young woman in Valemont, and the only thing standing between Heloise Valtor and everything she wanted. That his death would not save the girl. It would paint a target on her.

The old man had thought of it.

*W-I-L-L.*

*B-R-I-N-G. A. N-O-T-A-R-Y.*

*T-O-D-A-Y.*

*A-L-L. T-O. L-Y-S-E. I-N. T-R-U-S-T.*

*Y-O-U. T-R-U-S-T-E-E.*

*I-F. S-H-E. D-I-E-S. T-O. T-H-E. P-O-O-R.*

*T-H-E-N. H-E-R. D-E-A-T-H. P-A-Y-S. N-O-T-H-I-N-G.*

Edric sat with it. It was very like Faro: every move foreseen. With the old man alive, every cup that came up these stairs was a weapon, and the girl carried it. With the old man dead and his fortune locked away where Heloise could never reach it, half the reason for killing Lyse was gone. Not all of it. The Aubrays had left her everything too. But half.

"And the other half?" he said.

*Y-O-U.*

*P-R-O-T-E-C-T. H-E-R.*

*A-S. Y-O-U. P-R-O-M-I-S-E-D.*

"I can protect her with you alive."

The old man's eyes went past him, to the window. To the sea beyond the roofs, blue and far and glittering in the afternoon, the sea he had looked at from this chair for twelve years and not been able to turn his head towards.

*T-W-E-L-V-E. Y-E-A-R-S.*

*I-N. T-H-I-S. C-H-A-I-R.*

*C-H-A-I-N-E-D.*

Edric did not say anything. He did not have to. He had felt the chains under the Keep for fourteen years, shuddering through the floor of his cell. *Boom.* He knew what the word meant to a man who had once crept into the archives under the Sunspire to free the sea.

*I. W-I-L-L. N-O-T. D-I-E.*

*B-Y. H-E-R. C-U-P.*

*I. W-I-L-L. N-O-T. D-I-E.*

*I-N. T-H-I-S. H-O-U-S-E.*

*T-A-K-E. M-E. T-O. T-H-E. S-E-A.*

---

The notary came at five, a dry little man from the Street of Gold, with Bastien to witness and a young priest from the Temple of the Noon who was too kind to ask why it had to be today. The law of Valemont allowed a testament by sign, for those the palsy had silenced, before a notary and a priest of the Sun. It took two hours. Every word, letter by letter. When it was done, and sealed, Severin Valtor's fortune belonged to his granddaughter, held in trust by the Count of Cristal until her twenty-fifth birthday, and to the hospital of Saltmere if she did not live to see it.

Then the old man asked for one more thing.

*R-I-N-G.*

It was on his right hand, the useless one, lying on the rug: a heavy old signet of dark silver, too big to slide off a swollen finger. Edric worked it off, gently, with soap from the washstand. On its face, very worn, there was a wave.

*T-H-E-Y. W-I-L-L. K-N-O-W. I-T.*

*S-H-O-W. T-H-E-M.*

*S-A-Y.*

The old man's eyes held his.

*T-H-E. T-I-D-E. I-S. T-U-R-N-I-N-G.*

---

They took him out at two in the morning, through the garden gate into the alley behind the wall, where a young Guard captain had stood so many nights in the shadows calling up to a window. Bastien carried him. He was a big man still, in the frame, but there was nothing left on the frame, and Bastien carried him like a child, wrapped in his rug, down the alley to the closed carriage at the end, and the old man's eyes were open the whole way, looking at the stars.

They drove down through the sleeping city to the foot of the cliffs, north of the harbour, where an old flight of steps went down to a strip of shingle that nobody used. Bastien carried him down the steps. Edric went ahead with a shaded lantern.

At the bottom, on the shingle, Edric took him from Bastien's arms. He had not been sure he could. He found that he could, easily; there was enough of the sea in him for that.

"Wait here," he said.

He set the lantern on a rock, and held up the board for the last time, by its light, with one hand, the old man propped against his shoulder.

*L-Y-S-E.*

"Yes."

*T-E-L-L. H-E-R.*

*N-O-T. A-F-R-A-I-D.*

*F-R-E-E.*

"I'll tell her."

Then he put the board down on the shingle and walked into the sea, with Severin Valtor in his arms.

It was cold, the way it was always cold. He walked out until it was at his waist, and then his chest, and the old man's body grew light in the water, lighter and lighter, until Edric was holding him the way you hold someone you are teaching to float, with one hand under his shoulders and one under his knees, and his ruined face turned up to the stars, and the swell lifting them both and setting them down.

Edric felt the water in him. Old, and slow, and worn very thin, like a stream at the end of a long summer. And something else in it, that Edric had not expected: a lightness. Something rising in the old man the way the sea was rising under him, a thing that had been held down for twelve years, for thirty, for a lifetime, beginning to lift.

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight.

*On the Discipline of Still Water. The Stilling.* He had read it, a dozen times, in the library at Cristal and in the study in Solace. He had never done it. He had thought, if he ever did, it would be to save someone.

He found the stillness in himself, and went down into it, and laid it over the old man the way you lay a hand on a sleeping child's head. And the old man's waters, which were so few, and so tired, slowed under it without any resistance at all. His breath slowed. His heart slowed. And Edric did not keep the thread. He let it go, out into the black water around them, out of his hands and into the sea, the way you let go of a boat you have pushed off from the shore.

The old man's eyes were on the sky, and then they were on nothing.

And far below, very faint, very deep, Edric felt something turn in the dark water. Not the cold eager hunger that rose in him on the beach at Cristal when he killed. Something slower. Something that noticed.

*This one,* said the Deep, as if remembering. *I know this one. He was young. He sang to me once, from a boat, when no one was allowed to sing to me.*

Edric stood in the sea up to his chest, holding a dead man, and wept, and the salt of it went into the salt of the water and was lost.

---

They had him back in his chair before dawn, with the rug over his knees and his face to the window, and his eyes closed. The maid found him there in the morning. The physician, the young Sardhi, came and looked, and laid his hand on the old man's forehead, and paused, and looked for a long time at the faint white line of dried salt at the hairline, and in the creases of the neck. Then he wrote *apoplexy, in sleep, at a great age,* and signed it, and the High Justicar countersigned it without reading it, and Heloise Valtor stood in the doorway in her grey morning gown with her hand at her throat and her pale eyes very bright.

Her eyes were less bright a week later, when the notary from the Street of Gold read the will.

---

Lyse came to him on the evening of the funeral.

She came alone, on foot, in black, through the gate of the house with the blue door, and Bastien brought her up to the study, and she stood in the doorway and looked at Edric with her grandfather's grave dark eyes.

"There was salt in his hair," she said.

He did not hold the stillness. He did not even try.

"Yes," he said.

She came into the room and shut the door and stood against it. "Tell me."

He told her. All of it, from the board to the shingle. The tray, and the cups, and the will. The ring. The sea. He did not tell her what he had told the old man, about the stillroom and the name of the poison; not yet. He did not have the courage, that night. He would find it later, on the fifth night, in the dark, through a hole in a wall.

When he had finished, she did not weep. She stood with her back against the door and her hands pressed flat against the wood behind her, and looked at him, and he felt the water in her: still, and deep, and churning at the bottom, like a pool where a stone has gone in.

"You had no right," she said.

"No."

"He was going to die. Soon. Anyway. I knew that. I'd made myself know it." Her voice cracked, and steadied. "But I wasn't ready. I was going to read to him tonight. I was halfway through a book. I was going to read him the end." She pressed her lips together. "You didn't even let me say goodbye."

"He didn't want you to have to choose," said Edric. "Whether to let him. He said you'd say no. He said you'd sit by his chair and carry his cups until one of them killed you, because you loved him." He looked at her. "He asked me to tell you something. At the end. On the shore."

She waited.

"*Not afraid,*" said Edric. "*Free.*"

Lyse Valtor slid down the door until she was sitting on the floor in her black dress, and put her face on her knees, and wept, at last, without a sound. He sat at the desk and waited until she lifted her head.

"What do I do now?" she said.

---

There had been strangers at the funeral. Edric had noticed them, standing at the back of the crowd in the gardens of the Temple of the Noon, while the priests sang and the bronze door of the Valtor tomb closed: old men and old women, a dozen of them, perhaps more, in plain unfashionable clothes, who did not look as if they belonged at the funeral of a High Justicar's father. A retired clerk. A harbour pilot. A woman who kept a bookshop below the Temple steps. A very old Radiant priest in a threadbare white robe.

As the crowd went home, one by one, they passed him. And one by one, they looked at his hand, where the dark silver signet with the worn wave sat on his finger. And one by one, they touched his sleeve, very lightly, as they went by, and said, so quietly that no one else could hear it, the same four words.

*The tide is turning.*

### Later

There was no old man in a wheeled chair on the quay below the cliffs, on the day the King gave Edric until dawn to leave Solace. There were seven.

They were standing at the foot of the *Faro*'s gangway when he came down the cliff stair at a run with the noon sun on his back: the retired clerk from the Ministry of War, the harbour pilot, the woman from the bookshop, the old Radiant priest in his threadbare white, and three others he did not know. The youngest of them was sixty. Bastien stood beside them with his arms folded and a look on his face that Edric had never seen there before, which on another man might have been bewilderment.

"They say they're the Ebb," said Bastien. "What's left of it."

The clerk stepped forward. He was a small, dry, bald man with ink on his fingers, and he held out a packet wrapped in oilcloth.

"Severin made a copy," he said. "Thirty-five years ago, the night he took the Tidewarden into the archives. The plans of the Lock under the Drowned Keep. The sea-gate. The temple. Where the joins are, in the King's workings." He pressed it into Edric's hands. "We've kept it in a bookshop for thirty-five years, waiting for someone to give it to. He'd have wanted you to have it. He trusted you with his death. We can trust you with this."

The harbour pilot spoke next: a big, slow, white-whiskered man who looked as if he had been carved out of a quay piling.

"Second squadron of the Order's still in harbour," he said. "Four ships, with the mirrors on. They were to sail after the fleet tonight, to cut you off." He scratched his whiskers. "Only the harbour chain's jammed, it seems. Winch has gone. Can't think how. And the keys to the boom towers have gone missing. They'll have them in two days, maybe three." He did not smile. "Severin got me out of a Sun Warrant in the year of the Drying Jubilee. I was twenty. Nobody else would have lifted a finger."

Edric looked at them, the seven old people at the foot of the gangway, in the bright noon, with the Sunspire burning above them.

"Come with us," he said. "It's not safe for you here. Not now."

"It's never been safe for us here," said the woman from the bookshop. "That's rather the point." She looked at the ring on his finger. "Go and free it, Warden. We'll keep the house warm."

He went. And the second squadron of the Radiant Order sat in Solace harbour behind a jammed chain for three days, with its mirrors turning uselessly at the sun, and never reached Cristal at all.
