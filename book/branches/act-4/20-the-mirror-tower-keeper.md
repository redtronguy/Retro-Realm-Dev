# Event 20: The Mirror-Tower Keeper

*Branches from Chapter 21, "Currents of Gold": the night Edric knocks at the foot of the tower at Grey Point and says, "Master Fennick, I'm told you have a sick child."*

## Make the healing conditional on the false signal

*Heart − · Tide 0*

Hob Fennick took him in, because a man with silver on his harness does not stand in the road, and because the old man had stopped, some months ago, being able to tell the difference between hope and fear.

The two rooms at the foot of the tower smelled of tallow and boiled linen and the sea. The child lay in a box-bed against the inner wall, under a heap of blankets that had been a coat once, and two shawls, and a sail. She was asleep, or nearly. Her face was the grey-white of a fish's belly, and there were dark hollows under her eyes like thumbprints in wax, and every time she breathed there was a sound in her chest, a small wet labouring sound, like water moving in the bilge of a boat that is slowly settling.

Edric stood by the bed and listened to it. He did not need to touch her. He could feel the water in her from the doorway: the sick water, grey and cold and heavy, filling her from the bottom up, a little more every day. Another month, perhaps. Perhaps less.

"The physicians say there's nothing," said Hob. He was standing by the hearth with his cap twisted in his hands. He was a small, bent, weathered man, brown as a walnut from thirty-one years of sitting in the sun on top of a tower, and his eyes were red from not sleeping. "I've had three. The last one came all the way from Harnmouth. He took my savings and said to pray."

"The physicians are wrong," said Edric. "I can draw it out of her."

The old man stared at him.

"Not tonight," said Edric.

He had not known he was going to say it until it was said. He had come out from Solace in the carriage with the paper folded in his coat and the book's words in his head, *find the current that is wrong, and draw it out, gently, as thou wouldst draw a splinter*, and he had meant to do it at once. He had been sure of it, on the road. But he had stood in the doorway and felt the sick water in her, and felt how much of it there was, and how it clung, and he had understood, all at once and very clearly, what it would cost him to take it. A day. Two days, perhaps, flat on his back in the study on the Street of Lanterns, with the sea's sickness in his blood. And in those two days the old man would have his granddaughter back and nothing left to want.

He thought of Faro, at the wall, at the beginning: *Never spend a coin you can make a man give you.*

Faro had been talking about information. Faro had been talking about guards.

He took the paper out of his coat.

"There's a message on this," he said. "A pattern of flashes, long and short. I want you to send it north to the tower below Solace, tomorrow, at noon, as if it had come to you from the south. Just once." He held it out. "Tomorrow night I'll come back. And I'll sit by that bed until she breathes like you and me."

Hob Fennick looked at the paper. He did not take it.

"And if I don't?"

Edric said nothing. The fire ticked in the grate. In the box-bed, the child drew a breath, and the water moved in her, and she coughed in her sleep, a small, dragging, hopeless cough, and was still.

He watched the old man understand. He watched it go into him the way cold goes into a man who has fallen through ice: slowly, from the outside in, until it reaches the heart. Hob's face did not change much. Faces like his did not have much room left in them for change. But his hands stopped twisting the cap. They stopped completely, as if someone had taken hold of them.

"It's not a true message," said Hob. "Is it."

"No."

"Will anyone die of it?"

"No one," said Edric. "One man will lose a great deal of money that was never his. Money stolen from towns like this. From children like her." He heard his own voice, steady and reasonable, the voice of the Count of Cristal explaining a rate of interest. He heard how true every word was. It did not help as much as he had expected.

Hob took the paper. His fingers were shaking so badly he nearly dropped it. He did not look at it. He looked at the bed.

"Tomorrow at noon," he said. And then, very low, not to Edric, not to anyone: "Sun forgive me."

---

Edric did not sleep that night, at the inn at Grey Point where the carriage put up. He sat at the window of a room that smelled of old beer and watched the tower on the headland, black against the stars, and the single light at its foot, which did not go out.

At noon the next day, from the inn yard, he saw the great mirror on the summit swing on its brass frame, catch the sun, and throw it north. Long, short, short, long. He counted. Every flash right. The old man had not missed one.

He went back that night.

Hob opened the door before he could knock. He had been standing behind it. He did not say anything. He stood aside, and Edric went in past him, and sat down on the stool by the box-bed, and laid his hand flat on the child's chest.

It was as bad as he had known it would be. Worse, perhaps, because he had waited a day, and the sick water had had a day longer to settle. It clung to her like weed to a rock. He drew, and drew, and felt it come up through her, out of her lungs, out of her blood, into his hand and up his arm, grey and cold and stinking of the dying sea. The Tidemark on his chest flared and burned. He tasted salt and rot. Behind his eyes, for a moment, he saw the sea from very high above, grey and still, with dead fish floating on it as far as he could see, and a chain of gold light running down into it, and something at the bottom of the chain, turning in its sleep.

He held on until there was nothing left to draw.

When he took his hand away, the child was breathing quietly, slowly, clean. And he was on his knees on the old man's floor, being sick: grey seawater, more of it than a man could hold, spreading across the boards and into the cracks.

Hob Fennick did not come to help him up. That was the thing Edric would remember. In the account of small kindnesses that he had learned, in the Keep, to keep, it was the thing that was not there. The old man stood by the hearth with his back against the wall and watched him retch, and watched the grey water creep across his floor, and watched, when Edric's collar fell open, the pale blue lines on his throat glowing in the firelight like something seen at the bottom of a well. He watched all of it with his red, sleepless eyes. And then he looked at his granddaughter, breathing, with colour in her face. And then back at the man on his floor.

His face was not grateful. It was not even relieved. It was the face of a man who has made a bargain at a crossroads in the dark and only now, in the lamplight, sees properly who he made it with.

Edric got up. He wiped his mouth. He put the purse on the table, and the letter beside it, and said the name of the ship that would be waiting at Harnmouth harbour, and the name of the island in the west where no one would ever find them.

"You'll want to be gone before the Ministry sends a rider," he said. "They'll send one by the day after tomorrow."

Hob looked at the purse, and did not touch it.

"I'll go," he said. "I'll take her and go. You needn't fear that." He was quiet a moment. "I'd have done it, you know. Your signal. If you'd healed her first. I'd have done anything you asked. I'd have gone up that tower and thrown myself off the top of it if you'd asked." He looked at Edric with his red eyes. "But you didn't trust me to. So now I'll never know, will I. Whether I'd have done it for love."

Edric had no answer to that. He went out to the carriage, and the door shut behind him, and he heard the bar drop across it on the inside.

All the way back to Solace, in the dark, with the taste of rot in his mouth, he told himself that it had been the sensible thing. That the old man had been frightened anyway. That fear was more reliable than gratitude, and he had needed the signal sent, and it had been sent. That Nell was alive, which was all that mattered.

All of that was true. He had learned, in the Keep, that a thing can be entirely true and still not be the whole account.

### Later

The Radiant chapterhouse at Harnmouth was a squat, white, sun-bleached building at the top of the harbour steps, and its audience room was very bright, because every room the Order built was very bright. High Radiant Cassia Vell sat behind a plain table in the light from the long windows with her gauntlets off and her square, plain face giving nothing away, and listened to the old man.

He had not gone to Cristal. He had taken the purse, in the end, because a man with a child does not refuse gold, and he had taken Nell, and they had gone down the coast to Harnmouth as the letter said. But when he had stood on the quay and looked at the black cutter waiting for him, with its grey and brown sails and its crew who did not ask questions, he had found that he could not make his feet go up the gangway. He had turned round and walked back up the steps into the town, and found a room above a chandler's, and stayed. He had lived on the purse for half a year. He had lived on it quietly, and watched Nell grow fat and loud, and waited for a knock at the door.

When the King's proclamation came down the coast, nailed to every post, naming the Count of Cristal a sorcerer and a heretic and an enemy of the Sun, the old man had read it standing in the street, and gone home, and put on his good coat, and come up the steps to the chapterhouse.

"He held her breath," Hob said, "like a purse. That's what he did. He stood by her bed and listened to her drowning and told me the price." His voice was steady; he had rehearsed it. "A man who'll do that will do anything. That's all I came to say. He has lines on him, blue lines, like the old stories. He brings up seawater. And he sends folk west, to an island beyond the Reach. He has a ship that waits for them at this harbour. A black cutter, grey and brown sails. I can tell you the name."

Cassia Vell wrote it down. She wrote down the name of the ship, and the bearing of the island, and the description of the man, and the date of the false signal at Grey Point, and when she had finished, she looked for a long time at the nine-year-old girl sitting on the bench by the door, swinging her legs, round-cheeked, loud, alive.

"He healed her," she said. "Whatever he is. He did heal her."

"Yes," said Hob Fennick. "He did. I've thanked the Sun for it every day." He turned his cap in his hands. "I've never once thanked him."

## Find another way to ruin Dorran

*Heart + · Tide +*

He sat by the little girl's bed for three hours, that night, with his hand on her chest, and drew the sick water out of her until there was nothing left to draw. He was sick on Hob Fennick's floor, grey seawater, more of it than could possibly have been in him. And when he could sit up again, the old man was kneeling by the bed with his granddaughter's hand in his, weeping, and the child was asleep with colour in her cheeks for the first time in a year.

He went back to Grey Point a week later, as he had always meant to. Nell was up and running about the yard at the foot of the tower, chasing chickens round the well. Hob Fennick met him at the door and fell on his knees in the dust.

"Anything," said the old man. "My lord, anything. Ask me anything. My life."

Edric helped him up. He had the paper in his coat, folded small, with the pattern of flashes on it, long and short. He had copied it out himself, from the code-books Bastien's people had bought from a drunk clerk in the Ministry of War. He could feel the edge of it against his chest, just over the place where the Tidemark began.

He looked over the old man's shoulder at the child, laughing, with her hair in her eyes, and the hens scattering in front of her.

He thought about what would come after. The rider from the Ministry. The empty rooms. An old man who had sat on top of one tower for thirty-one years, who had never been further than Harnmouth in his life, packed onto a smuggler's cutter with his granddaughter and a purse of gold and sent to the far end of the world because a stranger had needed a lie told at noon. He thought about the look on the old man's face, the night of the healing: not a man who owed a debt, but a man who would pay any debt you named, because he could not imagine a world in which he had the right to refuse.

*Anything. My life.*

"I don't want anything," said Edric. "I came to see that she was well."

Hob stared at him.

"She's well," said Edric. "That's all." He took a purse out of his coat, a small one, and put it in the old man's hands and closed his fingers over it. "For her. For physicians, if she ever needs one again. And if anyone ever asks you who came here, tell them a priest. Brother Faro. A travelling priest who knew a little medicine." He turned back to the carriage. Then he stopped. "There's an island in the west called Cristal. If ever you want to leave this coast, any ship out of Harnmouth that flies a grey and brown pennant will take you there, and ask nothing. That's not a price. It's a door. You don't have to go through it."

"I'll stay," said Hob slowly. "If it's all the same. It's the only work I know." He looked up at the great mirror on its brass frame at the top of the tower, flashing as the sun moved. "And somebody has to pass the messages."

"Yes," said Edric. "Somebody does."

---

He did it the slow way.

He had known it would be slow. He had not known how slow, or what it would ask of him.

The false signal would have done the work in a day: one flash of panic, one afternoon on the exchange, and a frightened man selling everything at once. Now there would be no signal. There would only be the currents. He would have to make the panic himself, out of nothing, out of the water in the city, a drop at a time.

He sat in the study at the back of the house with the blue door, at night, with the lamp turned low and the bowl of still water in front of him, and breathed, and reached.

The currents of Solace opened round him like a map. The great slow drain of the bank of Quell at the centre of it; the Treasury, with its hollow foundations; the Street of Gold, where the money moved fastest, in short bright pulses, like a heart beating too quickly. And threaded through all of it, so fine he had never troubled to look at it before, the other current. Not gold. The thing under the gold. Want, and fear, and the pull between people: a merchant's quiet dread of the Sardh, where his brother had died in the war; a banker's habit of reaching for his purse when he heard a rumour; the clerks of the Vossens, whispering over their ledgers. It was all there, in the water in them, in the blood, in the sweat on a man's palm as he signed a bill. And it could be pulled.

Not hard. Never hard. If he pulled hard, they would feel it, and it would look like what it was: a hand on the scale. He pulled the way the moon pulls. A little. Every night. A Sardh bond-holder in a house on the great terrace woke at three in the morning with his heart racing, from a dream of fire in a desert city, and lay awake till dawn, and at the exchange that day sold a tenth of his holding, without quite knowing why. A clerk at the Vossens, adding a column, felt a sudden cold certainty that the Sardh peace would not last the winter, and told his master so. A rumour that no one had started went round the coffee houses on the Street of Gold, about unrest in the hill provinces beyond Mazrah, and was denied, and went round again.

Every night, a little. Every morning, the Sardh bonds were a little lower.

And every morning, in his white marble bedroom, a pale, neat man sat on the edge of his bed and looked at the numbers, and watched his two million marks of Sardh paper lose a quarter of a point, and a half, and a point, and told himself it was nothing. The Count's drafts kept coming. The reserves kept thinning. And on the fourth week, when the bonds had fallen nine points, and the Count's steward had come for another seven hundred thousand in gold, Dorran Quell began to sell. Not all at once. A little, every day, at a loss, to cover the drafts. A little more. A little more. The way a man with a leaking boat throws over his cargo, piece by piece, and each piece makes the next one easier.

By the end of the sixth week, the house of Quell had sold a million and a half marks of Sardh paper for a little under five hundred thousand. Nobody had lied to anybody. No tower had flashed a false word. There was no keeper to find, no rider sent to an empty room at Grey Point. There was only a frightened man, and a frightened market, and the tide.

On the last night, a boy left a letter at the gate of the white marble house, marked *urgent, for the Lord Treasurer only*. It was a single sheet of cream paper, with one line on it.

*Every captain needs to keep good accounts.*

---

Edric took his hand from the bowl, and the image of a pale man at a window trembled on the water, and was gone.

He could not get warm.

He had not been warm, he realised, for some days. He had stopped noticing. Six weeks of it, every night: six weeks of sitting over the bowl with the sea pouring through him, out into the city, a thread at a time, and coming back. And it did not all come back the same. Some nights it came back with the taste of salt so strong he could not eat. Some nights it came back with things in it that were not his. He had dreamed, three nights ago, of a city with towers of green stone standing in the sea, and a bell ringing under the water, and had woken knowing the name of the city, a name that no one had spoken in three hundred years, and lain in the dark saying it over until it went away.

He went up to the roof, in the cold, and took off his coat and shirt, and looked at himself by the lamp.

The Tidemark had spread. Across his chest, over both shoulders. Down his right arm almost to the elbow, where a month ago it had stopped above it. Up his throat, and onto his jaw, where it had never been, a single fine branching line, like a crack in glass, creeping towards his ear.

He stood there for a long time in the wind off the sea.

He had saved an old man his tower and a child her home. He had not made anyone lie. He had brought down Dorran Quell's fortune without a single false word, and it had taken him six weeks instead of a day, and it had cost him this.

*It's always the same price,* Faro had said once, through the wall, about something else entirely. *You only get to choose who pays it.*

He put his shirt back on, and went down, and did not reach for the bowl again that night. But he knew, as he lay down, that the dream of the green city would come back. And that some part of him, small and cold and very deep, was looking forward to it.

### Later

On the morning the King's fleet sailed for Cristal, the mirror on the tower at Grey Point was the first on the coast to see it.

Hob Fennick was at his post, as he was every morning from sunrise to sunset, in his old coat on his old stool, with his eye to the brass sighting-tube. He saw the white sails come round the headland from the north, one after another, twelve of them, white hulls and golden suns, with things on their decks that flashed in the early light like mirrors. He had never seen ships like them. He watched them pass below his tower, turning west, and he did not know what they were, and he did not need to.

He knew what the mirror on the Sunspire said, a moment later, because it came south through the tower below Solace and onto his own glass: a long message, heavy with the sign that meant *the Throne*, which he copied, flash for flash, as he had copied every message for thirty-one years. He could not read it. No keeper could. But in the long middle of it, again and again, there was one group of flashes he did know, because he had seen it once before, on a folded paper in a black-coated stranger's hand, a paper that had never been sent. The group that meant *Cristal*.

He sent the King's message on. He was a keeper; it was what keepers did.

And then, because there was a minute of sun before the next tower would expect him, he swung the great mirror on its brass frame, not north, not south, but west. Out to sea. To where a Cristal fishing boat with a grey and brown pennant had lain at anchor off Harnmouth all summer, doing nothing in particular, because someone had told her skipper to watch the coast.

He did not know the code. But he knew a simple thing that every child on that coast knew, the warning the fishing fleets used when a storm was coming. Three long. Three long. Three long.

On the fishing boat, someone saw. Someone ran.

Her skipper brought the news into the harbour at Cristal four days ahead of the fleet, and those four days were the difference between a harbour with its people up on the terraces and a harbour full of boats. Edric heard who had sent the warning, much later, when it was all over. He went down to the shore by himself and stood for a long time looking east, towards a coast he could not see, and a tower on a headland, and an old man on a stool who owed him nothing at all.
