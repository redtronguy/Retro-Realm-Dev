# Event 27: Fenrick's Soldiers

*Branches from Chapter 23, "The Desert Witness": three days after the Lord Marshal falls on his sword, after Edric and Hayda have "watched the sea together until the stars came out", when Bastien brings word that Fenrick's unpaid veterans have risen in the Lancer Yards.*

## Pay them out of your fortune

*Heart + · Tide 0*

"Three months," said Bastien. "That's what they're owed. Some of them more."

He stood in the door of the study with the morning *Courant* in his hand and the smell of smoke on his coat. He had been down to the lower town before dawn, to see for himself.

The Lancer Yards lay on the second terrace above the harbour, a long grey sprawl of barracks and stables and drill-squares where the Marshal's regiments had been quartered since the Sardh war: the men who had marched east with Fenrick Morrow and come home with him, and been his, for eleven years, the way a sword is a man's. Their pay had come through the Marshal's own accounts. The Treasury granted the money; the Marshal's paymasters drew it and paid it out. It was how Fenrick had kept them loyal. It was how every Marshal had, for a hundred years.

And the Marshal's accounts were at the house of Quell.

"The bank froze them," said Bastien. "The day of the verdict. Before the Chamber had even risen. The Lord Treasurer didn't want a dead traitor's debts on his books." His voice did not change. "Then the Crown seized the estate. Forfeit, for felony. And the Treasury says the arrears were the Marshal's debt, and the Crown says a forfeit pays nobody's debts. So nobody pays." He laid the paper on the desk. "They broke into the Marshal's palace last night. Took the silver. Set fire to the stables in the Yards. Four thousand men, my lord. Old ones, most of them. Cripples. Widows drawing their husbands' pensions. They're saying they'll march on the Street of Gold tonight."

Edric looked at the paper without reading it.

He could feel them. He had felt them since he woke, without knowing what it was: a hot, sour, churning current at the bottom of the city, like a pot coming to the boil. And he could feel, if he let himself, where it had come from. The bank, bleeding. The reserves he had drained. The draft after draft after draft that had left the house of Quell so hollow that its master had frozen a dead man's accounts out of fear, rather than pay four thousand soldiers what they were owed.

He had pulled one thread. This was the cloth coming apart at the other end.

"What do they need?" he said.

Bastien looked at him. "Their pay."

"How much?"

Bastien told him. Edric sat with it for a moment. It was a great deal of money. Not a great deal for the Count of Cristal. More than the Count of Cristal could afford to be seen spending on the men of the man he had just destroyed.

"Have it in gold by noon," he said. "Drawn on Quell." He almost smiled. "Let the Lord Treasurer pay the Marshal's men after all."

---

Hayda found him in the courtyard as the carts were being loaded.

She did not ask where he was going. She had read the *Courant*. She stood by the black marble pool in a plain grey dress, with her hair tied back, and watched the chests go up onto the carts, one after another, and the four armed men climb up beside them.

"They were at Qasr," she said.

"Some of them."

"Most of them. The Marshal's regiments. The ones he took east." She was very still. "Some of them opened the gate. Some of them went through the palace with torches. Some of them stood in the market at Qadir and watched."

"Yes," said Edric.

"And you're going to pay them."

He turned and looked at her. At the slight straight figure by the pool, with her chin up and her desert-well eyes, and something in them he had not seen since the day in the Chamber of Lords.

"I'm going to pay four thousand men what they're owed," he said, "so they don't burn the lower town tonight, and so the King doesn't send the Order down to burn it for them tomorrow. Some of them were at Qasr. Most of them were sixteen when they marched east, and did what a captain told them, and have been paying for it in their sleep ever since. I know. I can feel it in them." He was quiet. "You don't have to come."

Hayda looked at the carts for a long time.

"Yes," she said. "I do."

---

The Lancer Yards smelled of wet ash and horse and old beer. The stables at the north end were still smoking, a black roofless shell, and the drill-square in front of them was full of men: sitting on the cobbles, leaning on the barracks walls, standing in knots with their arms folded. Old men in faded blue coats with the facings torn off. Men on crutches. Men with one sleeve pinned up. Women in black with children on their hips. Somebody had chalked on the wall of the paymaster's office, in letters a yard high: *WE BLED FOR HIM. WHO PAYS?*

They went silent when the carts came in. Four thousand people, going silent all at once. It was a sound in itself.

Edric got down from the first cart and walked out into the middle of the square, in black, with the silver wave at his collar, and stood there, and let them look at him. He felt them know him. The Count of Cristal. The foreigner whose ward had stood up in the Chamber. A low sound went through the square, like wind getting up.

A man came forward out of the crowd: big, grey, square, sixty perhaps, with a sergeant's stripes on a coat that had been mended so often it was more patch than cloth, and a face like a slammed door.

"Garrow," he said. "Holt Garrow. Sergeant, Third Lancers. I speak for this lot, for my sins." He looked at the carts, and back at Edric. "You'll be the man who killed our Marshal."

"Your Marshal killed himself, Sergeant."

"Aye. After you'd finished with him." Garrow's eyes did not move. "What's in the chests?"

"Your pay," said the Count of Cristal. "Three months' arrears, for every man and every pension on the muster rolls. In gold. Today." He lifted his voice, just enough. "Bring me the rolls, and a table, and a clerk who can read them, and form a line."

Nobody moved.

"Why?" said Garrow.

Edric thought about how to answer. He thought about the truth, which was that the bank had frozen their pay because he had bled it white, and that he did not want to add four thousand more to the list of people who had paid for his revenge without owing anything. He did not say that.

"Your Marshal owed you," he said. "I took his name. I'll take his debts with it."

---

They paid all afternoon.

Bastien found a table, and the paymaster's clerk was dragged out of the gin-shop across the road, and the muster rolls were brought up from the cellar, and a line formed. It went round the drill-square three times and out through the gate into the street. Bastien counted the gold. The clerk read the names. And at the end of the table, after the first hour, there was a second chair, and Hayda bint Tariq sat in it.

She had not said anything. She had simply got down from the cart and walked to the table and sat, and when the clerk called the next name she put out her hand for the purse and gave it herself. Coin by coin. Counting each one aloud, in the common tongue, in her clear cool voice, into each man's palm.

The men did not know what to do. Some of them would not take it from her. Some took it and could not look at her. Some knew her face from the *Courant*, and stood there with their hands out and their mouths working, and went away without a word. One old man went down on his knees on the cobbles in front of her, and wept, and she waited until he had finished and put the coins into his hand anyway, and called the next name.

Holt Garrow came last. He had put himself at the end of the line, behind every man in it.

He stood in front of the table for a long time, looking at her.

"I was on the gate," he said at last. "At Qasr. That night. Corporal, then. Captain Morrow told us to open it, and we opened it. I didn't go into the palace. I stayed on the gate. But I saw them bring the women out." His face did not change. "I saw you. Tied to your mother. You weren't crying. I remember that. All the noise, all the fires, and you weren't crying." He looked at the gold on the table. "I don't want it. Not from you."

Hayda looked up at him.

"My father," she said, "paid his soldiers on the day they were owed. Every month. In person. With his own hand. It was the first thing he taught me about ruling. He said a lord who doesn't pay his men has no right to their swords." She counted the coins out, one by one, onto the table in front of him. "He can't do it any more. You saw to that. So I'm doing it for him." She pushed the coins across. "Take it, Sergeant. It's the only thing of his I have left to give."

Garrow stood very still. Then he picked up the coins, slowly, one at a time, and closed his big hand around them, and bowed: a stiff, old-fashioned, soldier's bow, all the way from the waist.

He turned to Edric, standing behind her.

"What do you want for this?" he said. "Nobody pays four thousand men for nothing. Not even you."

"Nothing," said Edric. "Not now." He looked out over the drill-square, at the smoke still drifting from the burnt stables, at the old men in their torn blue coats going home in twos and threes with their purses inside their shirts. "One day, perhaps, a wall to stand on."

"Name the wall," said Holt Garrow, "and we'll be there."

---

The Lancer Yards did not march on the Street of Gold that night. The *Courant* said, the next morning, that the Marshal's veterans had been paid their arrears by an anonymous benefactor and dispersed peaceably to their homes, and that the Chamber of Lords would be inquiring into the conduct of the house of Quell in the matter of the frozen accounts. Edric read it at breakfast and folded it and laid it beside his plate.

Across the table, Hayda drank her coffee and did not look at him.

"Did it help?" he said, after a while. "Paying them."

"No," said Hayda. She set down her cup. "But I think it might, one day. I think it might be the kind of thing that helps later." She looked at him. "My father would have done it. He'd have hated you for making him. But he'd have done it."

### Later

The King disbanded the Marshal's regiments a month later, by decree, without pensions: a traitor's soldiers were no soldiers of his. Edric's ships were in Solace harbour that autumn, coming and going. Some three hundred of the old men took passage west on them, with their wives and their children and their torn blue coats, to an island they had never heard of, where the Warden needed a watch.

On the Long Day, they held the first terrace.

They did not hold it for long; nobody could have, against sunfire. But Jac's watch had lost it twice by noon, and was falling back up the processional stair with its wounded, when a line of grey-haired men in faded blue came down the stair the other way, at a walk, in step, with pikes and old cavalry sabres and the lance-pennons of the Third and Fifth stitched onto broom-handles, and formed across the top of the first terrace as if they were on the drill-square at the Lancer Yards. Holt Garrow walked in front of them with a sergeant's half-pike on his shoulder.

The Radiants coming up the stair stopped. They knew those coats. Half of them had fathers who had worn them.

"Third Lancers!" roared Garrow, in a voice that carried to the citadel. "You'll remember this wall, lads! We were asked to stand on it!"

They held it for an hour. Long enough for the people on the lower terraces to get up the stair. Long enough for the wounded to be carried. Edric, on the roof of the citadel with the noon sun pressing him into the stone, could feel every one of them: old water, slow and stubborn and unafraid, like a river that has decided not to move.

Garrow came up the stair at sunset with his arm in a sling and forty fewer men than he had taken down. He found Hayda at the top, on the third terrace, with Bastien's long knife in her hand, and stood in front of her, and saluted.

"Paid on the day, Princess," he said. "In person."

Hayda looked at him for a long moment, in the smoke and the red light. Then she put out her small hand, and he took it, and they stood like that, the corporal from the gate at Qasr and the six-year-old who had not cried, while the sun went down into the western sea.

## Let the riots burn

*Heart 0 · Tide 0*

"Three months," said Bastien. "That's what they're owed."

He told it all: the Marshal's accounts at the house of Quell, frozen on the day of the verdict; the estate seized by the Crown; the Treasury saying the arrears were the Marshal's debt, and the Crown saying forfeits paid nobody's. Four thousand men in the Lancer Yards on the second terrace above the harbour, old soldiers and cripples and widows. The Marshal's palace broken into, the stables burned. The march on the Street of Gold, promised for tonight.

"Shall I have gold drawn?" he said.

Edric did not answer for a long time.

He could feel them: the hot sour churning current at the bottom of the city. He could feel, too, where it had come from. The bank he had bled. The frightened man in the white marble house who had frozen a dead man's accounts rather than pay. He could follow the thread all the way back to his own desk.

And he could follow it the other way. To a gate in the Sardh, opened in the dark. To a palace full of torches. To a market at Qadir, where men in blue coats had stood and watched a slaver count gold into their captain's hand.

"No," he said.

Bastien waited.

"They're the Marshal's men," said Edric. "The Marshal's debts. The Throne's army. If the Throne won't pay its own soldiers, let the Throne answer for it." He looked at the window. "And if the Count of Cristal walks into the Lancer Yards with four thousand men's wages the week after the Lord Marshal dies, everyone in Solace will ask why. I didn't come here to be asked why."

"And if they burn the lower town?"

"Then the city will see what the King's order is worth when nobody pays for it."

Bastien looked at him for a moment longer, with his black eyes. Then he nodded, once, and went out.

Hayda had been standing in the corridor. She came in after he had gone, and stood by the desk, and Edric waited for her to argue. She did not.

"They were at Qasr," she said. "Most of them." She was quite calm. "Let them burn."

He had wanted her to argue. He found that out only when she did not.

---

They marched at dusk.

He watched from the roof. He told himself he would not, and then he was on the roof with his collar open in the cold wind, looking down over the terraces of Solace to the lower town, where the light was wrong.

It started as torches: a long ragged river of them, coming out of the Lancer Yards and up the Street of Carts towards the stair that climbed to the Street of Gold. He could hear it even from here, faintly, under the wind: not shouting, exactly, but a low, steady, rolling roar, like surf. Then the first fire. A warehouse at the foot of the Street of Carts, a corn-chandler's, he thought, going up like a bonfire. Then another. Then a whole row of them, the flames running along the rooftops from house to house in the old wooden quarter behind the Yards, where the carters and the porters and the soldiers' widows lived, and the sky over the lower town turned orange.

He felt the people in the houses. He did not want to, and he did. Small frightened currents, running, scattering, like fish when a stone falls in the pool.

He stood with his hands on the parapet and did not move.

At the second hour, the Sunspire woke.

He felt it before he saw it: a pressure, a heat, coming down off the white tower behind him like the noon sun on the back of his neck, though it was full dark. He turned. Down the long stair from the great terrace, from the gates of the Radiant Order's chapter-house below the Sunspire, a column of light was moving. Two hundred knights, perhaps more, in white and gold, marching four abreast, and every one of them carrying fire in his hands. Not torches. Sunfire. Small blazing suns, cupped in their palms, so bright they threw the shadows of the houses up the cliff.

At their head walked a tall, broad-shouldered figure with short iron-grey hair, without a helmet, with the sun of Aurelian blazing on her breastplate.

They went down into the lower town like a blade going into a wound.

He did not see what happened in the Street of Carts. He was too far. He saw the light: gold and white, flaring, flaring again, in the narrow streets between the burning houses, and the roar of the crowd changing its note and breaking up into something higher and thinner. He felt it. Every flash of sunfire, at that distance, was like a needle in the Tidemark. And under the needles, the currents in the lower town breaking apart and running, running, and some of them stopping.

It took less than an hour.

When it was over, the fires were still burning in the wooden quarter, and nobody was putting them out, and the column of light was standing in the drill-square of the Lancer Yards, quite still, in rows, like a field of burning wheat.

Hayda came up onto the roof and stood beside him. She did not say anything for a long time.

"I thought," she said at last, very low, "that I would feel something."

"Yes," said Edric.

"I keep thinking that." She was looking at the fires. "It keeps not being true."

---

By the next noon, there were gibbets in the Lancer Yards. The *Courant* said nine ringleaders had been hanged, by order of the High Radiant, under the King's emergency law; that some sixty rioters had been killed in the suppression and many more injured; that the fire in the carters' quarter had destroyed two hundred houses before it burned itself out against the cliff. It did not say how many of the dead had been rioters, and how many had been asleep.

It said that, for the present, the city watch of Solace was placed under the command of the Radiant Order, and that a curfew would be kept from sunset to dawn.

Edric went out that evening, as he went out most evenings, in a closed carriage, to a reception at the palace of a lord on the great terrace. He was stopped four times between the Street of Lanterns and the palace gate. Each time a Radiant knight in white and gold came to the carriage window with fire in his hand and looked in at him, at his still foreign face, for a long moment, before he waved the carriage on. Each time, the Tidemark under his collar burned as if someone had laid a hot iron on it.

When he came home, Bastien was waiting in the courtyard.

"They've put a post at the end of the street," he said. "Two knights. Day and night. And the harbour's closed after dark. A chain across the mouth, and a galley on either side." He looked at Edric. "The King's taken the city, my lord. Not the watch. The city."

Edric stood by the black pool and looked at the fountain, dancing, and found that it was hard to breathe.

### Later

The chain was still across the harbour mouth on the day the King sent him away.

He came down the cliff stair at a run, from the foot of the Sunspire, with the noon sun on his back and Cassia Vell's words in his ears, and found the *Faro* lying at the quay with her sails loosed and her crew aboard, and Rashid at the gangway grey-faced.

"They've closed it," said Rashid. "The minute the fleet cleared. The chain's up. Four galleys, not two, and a cutter with a mirror on her, lying off the breakwater. The harbourmaster says no ship leaves Solace today without the High Radiant's seal." He spread his hands. "My lord, we can't even get out of the harbour."

Edric looked down the length of the harbour to its mouth: the great iron chain stretched across it on its floats, a yard above the water, from tower to tower, and the four long Radiant galleys at its ends, white and gold, with their oars run out. Since the night of the riot, the Order had held the harbour as it held every street in Solace. They had had a season to practise.

"Cast off," he said.

He went to the bow. He did not have the strength for it. He knew that. He had given half of what he had to the sea under the Pale Cliffs, and gone up the shaft of the Sunspire, and he could feel the noon sun on him like a hand. He reached for the harbour anyway, and it came: slow, faint, cold, eager.

He lifted the *Faro* on it. Not over the chain; he could not have done that. Into it. He brought her down the harbour on a swell that rose under her keel like a hand, faster and faster, with the galleys' oars thrashing as they tried to close, and the cutter's mirror swinging round to find her, and at the last moment he reached down past the ship, past the swell, into the water under the chain, and *pulled*, and the floats went under, and the chain went with them, and the *Faro* went over it with a yard to spare and a sound under her keel like a bell.

The mirror found her as she cleared the breakwater. It took her mizzen topsail off in a sheet of flame.

They put it out. They ran west. But he had spent, getting out of Solace, what he had meant to spend on the first day's wind, and Rashid's eleven days became eleven in earnest, and he stood at the bow with the sea pouring through him and counted the thread beside his heart, and knew that the riot he had let burn had just cost him a day he did not have.

## Crush the riot with Riptide

"Three months," said Bastien. "That's what they're owed."

He told it all, and Edric listened: the frozen accounts, the forfeit estate, the Treasury and the Crown each pointing at the other. The Lancer Yards on the second terrace above the harbour. The stables burned, the Marshal's palace looted. The march on the Street of Gold tonight.

"What will the King do?" said Edric.

"Send the Order," said Bastien. "What else? Sunfire in the Street of Carts. Gibbets in the Yards by morning. And the Order on every corner after." He looked at the Count. "That's bad for us, my lord. The Radiants in the streets. Posts on the Street of Lanterns. Knights at the harbour. We've the Valtor house to watch. Quell's not finished."

Edric thought about it. He thought about the Order in the streets, and the curfew, and every night's work made twice as hard. He thought about four thousand old soldiers who had stood at a gate at Qasr and watched. He thought, too, if he was honest, about the sea, which he had held so carefully, for so long, and which had been waiting.

"Then the Order mustn't be needed," he said.

---

They marched at dusk, and he was waiting for them.

He stood at the top of the Street of Carts in a plain dark coat, with his collar up, where the street narrowed between high houses before it climbed to the stair. Below him the torches came up the hill out of the Lancer Yards in a long, ragged, roaring river: old men in torn blue coats, men on crutches, men with cudgels and pitchforks and the sabres they had carried in the Sardh, and behind them the fires. The corn-chandler's warehouse at the foot of the street was already burning. The flames were spreading along the wooden roofs of the carters' quarter, house to house.

He felt the people in the houses, running. He felt the people in the street, coming. He felt, below them, behind them, under the whole lower town, the harbour, black and full on the evening flood.

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight.

Then he reached down the hill, past the torches, past the fires, past the Lancer Yards and the quays and the breakwater, into the harbour, and took hold of it.

It came so fast it frightened him.

It came up out of the harbour the way it had come to his hands on the beach at Cristal, on the night of the first raid: eager, glad, as if it had been waiting for him to ask. It came over the quays in a long black hump of water, and up into the lower town, filling the lanes, running uphill, which water does not do. It came up the Street of Carts behind the rioters faster than a horse could gallop, and he felt it go over the burning houses on either side and put them out, every one, with a hiss that went up into the night like a thousand snakes, and then it reached the crowd.

He did not drown them. He told himself that, afterwards, many times. He did not drown them. He only knocked them down. The wave went through the crowd at waist height and took their legs from under them, all of them, at once, four thousand people, and the torches went out, and the roar of the march became a single long cry and then a confused gasping and splashing and coughing in the dark, as the water carried them back down the hill, tumbling, the way a wave carries pebbles down a beach, and left them in heaps in the drill-square of the Lancer Yards, soaked and bruised and terrified, and drew back into the harbour, hissing.

He held it a moment longer than he needed to.

He knew it, even as he did it. The street was empty. The fires were out. It was done. And he held the sea there in the Street of Carts, black and humming and alive, filling the lanes to the first-floor windows, because it felt so good. Because it was the first time in a month that he had let it come all the way up, and it was like breathing after holding your breath for a year. He held it, and felt it, and the cold went through him and the salt filled his mouth and something in him, very deep, laughed.

*Yes,* said the Deep. *Yes, little one. Like this.*

He let it go.

---

They found the old man in the morning: a one-armed sergeant of the Fifth, sixty-eight years old, who had been carried by the wave into a doorway on the Street of Carts and pinned there by a cart that came down the hill with the water. He had drowned in four feet of water in the dark, on dry land, a hundred feet above the sea.

He was the only one. The physicians said it was a mercy it was not hundreds. The *Courant* said that a freak tide, of a kind not seen in Solace in living memory, had come up out of the harbour at the height of the riot and broken it up, and extinguished the fires in the carters' quarter, and that the Marshal's veterans, half drowned and wholly cowed, had gone home. Some of the old fishermen in the harbour said it was no tide. They said they had seen the sea stand up and walk. The priests of the Radiant Order said nothing at all, which was worse.

Edric sat in the study at the back of the house with the blue door, with the shutters closed, and did not look in the mirror.

He did not have to. He could feel it: the Mark on his face, climbing. Past the cheekbone. To the corner of his eye, and over it, into the brow, cold as frost on glass. When he closed his eyes he could see the drowned sergeant in the doorway, and behind him, in the dark, his own face smiling.

Hayda came in at noon and stood in front of the desk. She looked at him for a long time.

"There's a man dead," she said.

"I know."

"One man. Instead of a hundred. Instead of gibbets in the Yards." She was quite still. "I'm not saying you were wrong."

"No," said Edric. "You're not saying it."

She did not answer. She looked at the Mark over his eye, and something went across her face, very quick, like a cloud over water. Then she went to the window and opened the shutters, and the light came in, and he flinched from it, and she saw that too.

Far above the house, at the top of the Sunspire, the gold light had burned all night a little brighter than usual. Nobody in Solace noticed. Only one man, sitting in a dark study with his eyes closed, who had felt it, at the moment he let the sea go, turn very slightly in his direction, the way a sleeper turns at a sound in the next room, and settle again, and go on burning.

### Later

The King had tea waiting.

"Sit down, do," said Aurelian, in the great round room of glass at the top of the Sunspire, in the light of the chains. "No? Never mind." He poured anyway, and held out the cup, and set it down when it was not taken. "You'll forgive me if I don't pretend to be surprised to see you. I've been looking forward to this since the night the harbour came up the Street of Carts." He smiled, gently. "Did you think I wouldn't notice? A tide that runs uphill? In my city? It was the most beautiful thing I'd seen in a hundred and forty years. I sat here and watched it. I nearly applauded."

Edric stood by the shaft of light, scalded and shaking, and said nothing.

"I've had the High Radiant watching your house ever since," said the King. "Quietly. She's very good. I know where you go at night. I know about the wall between your empty house and the Valtor girl's room." He dipped his white cup in the light from the well, and drank, and far away at the bottom of the world something flinched. "I know what two men carried out of the Valtor tomb at three in the morning, wrapped in a cloak. And I know where the black cutter took it." His golden eyes rested on Edric's face with grave, gentle interest. "Still Water. The Stilling. Forty days, isn't it? I read the books too, you know. Before I burned them."

The thread beside Edric's heart lay very faint, very far, very still.

"Leave her alone," he said.

"I sent the fleet a week ago," said Aurelian. "You were busy. Sixteen ships, not twelve; I had time to be thorough. And the High Radiant has given every captain a letter, under my seal." He set the cup down. "The library first. Then the rest."
