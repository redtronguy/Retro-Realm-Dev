## Forgive him but keep the money for Cristal's walls

*Heart + · Tide 0 · City +*

"So I'm not going to do anything to you," said Edric Vane.

He said it the way you set down something heavy that you have carried a very long way: carefully, because your arms have forgotten how to be empty. He took the rest of the bread out of his coat and put it into Dorran's thin hands, and closed the man's fingers round it.

"I came here to watch you starve. I wrote the price myself. I smiled, writing it." He looked down at his own hands, bandaged, on his knees. "And I'm not going to. I forgive you, Dorran. Not because you deserve it. Because I need to be forgiven too, and I don't know how else to start."

Dorran stared at him over the bread. Then he began, very quietly, to weep again, and to eat at the same time, which is a thing only the very hungry can do, and Edric sat on the straw beside him in the grey light from the cave mouth and let him.

---

Rook brought the strongbox down into the cloister on the third morning, when Dorran could stand.

It was an iron-bound chest of black oak, the kind the house of Quell used for moving gold between the Street of Gold and the Treasury, and Rook set it on the broken flags of the cloister and opened it and stood back. Inside, in neat bundles tied with tape, were the letters of credit Dorran had signed in the cave, one for every meal: a hundred thousand marks apiece, drawn on banks in Corrow and Port Jasmine and the southern empires, and under them, the gold that had been in the saddlebags, and the deeds to a house in the far south. Every mark of it. Rook had touched none of it, as he had been told.

"Two million, near enough," said Rook. "Less what he spent on the horse." He scratched his black beard. "Where's it to go, my lord?"

Edric looked down into the chest.

He knew where it was supposed to go. He had known since the night at the bowl in Solace, five months ago, when he first felt the hole in the Blight Fund: back to the towns. Back to the beacons and the fishing villages and the hospitals, the places it had been taken from. He had planned it in the spring, in the library at Cristal, before he ever came north. A list, in his own hand. An anonymous letter. *For the purpose for which it was raised.*

And then he thought of a gold light at the top of a white tower, burning brighter since the storm.

*One more,* he had told himself, lying awake in the house with the blue door. He had not let himself think much past it. He thought past it now, standing in the cloister with the chest at his feet, and what he saw was Cristal.

Four thousand people. A harbour behind a Tidewarden breakwater that had been patched with rubble. Walls along the shore that had been built, five years ago, to keep out crabs the size of horses: low walls, a man's height, of dry stone and lime, with oil lamps on them. A school with no roof. A hospital of twelve beds. A citadel on the hill with a broken dome, and in it, the last Tidewarden books in the world. And a library room with a bed in it, where a girl lay sleeping with her hands folded on her breast, and would lie for twelve more days.

When the King looked for him, he would find Cristal. Edric was as sure of it as of the tides. And Cristal had nothing to meet him with but forty men and women with spears and nets.

Two million marks would build a sea-wall of dressed stone round the whole of the harbour. Gates. A boom. Granaries cut into the rock, out of reach of fire. Cisterns. A hospital that could take five hundred. It would put the *Slip* and the Corrow ships to sea with a year's provisions, and bring every family on the island something to stand behind.

And if he sent it to the Treasury in Solace, with his anonymous letter and his careful list, it would go back into the hands of the King who had built the beacons in the first place. Who lit them for his own fishing towns and left the rest of the coast in the dark. Who was sitting at the top of his tower at this moment, drinking the sea.

"Load it on the mules," he said. "It's going west."

Rook looked at him for a long moment, with his head on one side.

"West," he said. "Not back to the towns."

"Cristal's a town."

"Aye," said Rook slowly. "So it is." He did not say anything else. He closed the chest, and called two of his men, and they lifted it between them and carried it away across the cloister, and Edric watched it go and felt, under the Tidemark, nothing he could put a name to. Not the sweetness. Not the cold. Something smaller and more ordinary than either, like a stone in a shoe.

---

He sent Dorran south that afternoon, as he had meant to, with a horse and a cloak and a purse of fifty thousand marks.

Dorran sat on the horse in the cloister of the ruined monastery, thin as a rail, in a borrowed cloak, with the purse in his lap. He had been watching from the cave mouth, that morning, when the chest went by on the mules. He had always been good at watching where money went. It was the one thing he had ever been good at.

"Fifty thousand," he said. "It's more than I had when I came to Solace."

"It's what Cade got for my diamond," said Edric. "It seemed right."

Dorran looked at the purse, and then up, towards the west, where the mules had gone down the ravine.

"And the rest?" he said. "The two million. The Fund." He said it quite gently. There was no accusation in it. Only a clerk's curiosity, the curiosity of a man who has kept the books on that particular sum for eleven years and would like to know how the column closes. "Not to the Treasury, I think."

"To Cristal," said Edric. "For walls."

Dorran was silent for a moment.

"I told myself that, too," he said. "Not walls. Grain ships, and bonds, and loans to the Empress. Something that needed it more, for a little while. Something I'd pay back." He looked down at Edric from the horse, with his sunk, bright, tired eyes. "The beacons on the coast failed in the storm. Did you know? Half of them between Harnmouth and Saltmere. That's why they ordered the audit. The priests were asking where the money had gone for new ones." He almost smiled. "It's in your mules."

"I know," said Edric.

"I'm not saying you're wrong," said Dorran Quell. "I'm the last man in Valemont who could say that. I'm saying," and he gathered the reins in his thin hands, "welcome to the account, Edric. It's very hard to close. I never managed it."

He turned his horse and rode out of the cloister, south, down the ravine, towards the passes and the border and a city where nobody knew his name, and did not look back.

---

The letter went to Cristal by Rook's fastest rider and the *Slip*'s fastest crossing, and Tam's answer came back within the month, though Edric was not in Solace to receive it by then; it found him later, in a stack of letters on the library table, after everything.

*The council has voted the stone, eleven to one. Jac has the masons on the harbour wall already. Olaf asked where the money came from. I said it came from the Warden. He said that wasn't what he asked. I didn't have an answer. — T.*

### Later

They put in at Saltmere on the seventh day, because the wind failed off the Hound's Teeth, and because Edric found that he could not sail past.

The light tower on the harbour wall was lit, as it always was. But on the headland south of the town, where the King's beacon had stood for as long as anyone could remember, with a Radiant priest tending a little piece of the noon sun at its top every night, there was nothing. A stump of white stone, black with soot. It had failed in the storm, a month ago, like half the beacons on the coast, and no one had come to rebuild it.

He went to the Weir, and found it broken.

Not half empty, as it had been for five years. Broken. A whole row of stilt-houses at the seaward end lay tilted into the channel, their piles snapped, their walkways hanging in the water like broken fingers. There were scorch marks on the planks where somebody had fought with fire. There were new graves on the hill above the town, the Weir-folk's way, with shells on them.

Mira was sitting at the end of the walkway with her feet in the grey water, as he had known she would be. She had a bandage round her left forearm, and her shawl was torn.

"They came three nights running," she said, without looking up. "In the dark of the moon. Crabs. Eels. Something that had been a seal. With the beacon gone, there was nothing to stop them. Aren killed one with a boathook. He's very proud." She did not smile. "Old Bettan's gone. And the Callis boy. And four more." She looked out at the channel. "They said in the town the money for a new beacon was coming. From the Blight Fund. Then the Lord Treasurer ran off with the Blight Fund, and nobody knows where it went."

Edric sat down beside her on the planks and did not say anything.

A week before, on the Long Day, he had seen what the money bought. It had come too late for dressed stone; three weeks will not build a sea-wall. But it had bought everything that money can buy in three weeks. A boom of chained timber across the harbour mouth, that held the Radiant boats off the shore until noon, five hours longer than they would have been held without it. A second wall of timber and sand behind the old shore wall. Oil and grain and bandages, carried up to the citadel by the shipload, so that the stores did not burn with the warehouses. Two hundred Corrow sailors on the terraces, hired at three times their wages. In the hospital on the second terrace, new-roofed and fitted out with every bed the Reach could sell, there had been room for everyone they carried up the stair. Tam had written it all down that night, in his ledger, very carefully, and shown him the page. Edric had read it twice, and been glad.

He sat on the end of the walkway with his feet in the grey water beside the woman he had loved, and thought of that page, and did not know which column to put the Callis boy in.

## Let him starve

*Heart −− · Tide 0*

"I'm so tired of being afraid," Dorran had said. And then Edric had let the stillness go, and Dorran had looked up at Edric Vane, and his face had opened like a ledger at a broken spine.

"I thought you were dead," whispered Dorran. "Valtor said. At a dinner. I was glad. I'm sorry. I went home and sat on the edge of my bed and thought: there, that's balanced. And then I looked at the numbers. And my hands still shook."

Edric knelt on the straw and looked at him.

He had come up the ravine at a gallop, through the night, with the old man's eyes in front of him the whole way. *Enough.* He had come to stop this. He had meant to stop it. He had walked into the cave with the bread in his coat and the water at his belt, and filled the empty jug, and held it to the man's lips, and watched him drink like an animal.

And then he had looked at the man's hands, shaking on the blanket. And thought of another pair of hands.

"My father starved," he said.

Dorran's eyes went very still.

"In Saltmere. In Ropewalk Lane. While I was in the Keep. Cade told me. Five years ago, at an inn, by the fire, thinking I was a priest." His voice was quite level. "He spent my wedding money on letters to Solace that nobody answered. Then he stopped writing. Then he stopped eating. Moren offered him a pension, and he wouldn't take it. He said his son would provide for him when he came home." He looked down at Dorran on the straw. "He sat at the window every day and watched the harbour. A year, and the winter after. Mira was holding his hand at the end. The physician said it was hunger."

"Edric—"

"You did that," said Edric Vane. "You and your left hand. You took his son, and he sat at a window waiting for a ship that was never coming, until there was nothing left in him. And you went to Solace and bought grain ships with the money for the fishing towns." He stood up. "You've had eight days, Dorran. He had a winter."

---

He went out into the cloister. Rook was there, with his arms folded, and two of his men.

"Water," said Edric. "As much as he wants. No bread."

Rook did not move.

"My lord," he said, very carefully, the way a man speaks to a horse that might kick. "Your rider never came. I know. But you rode all night to get here, and you came in through that gate with a face like a man coming to pull a child out of a well. I saw it." He looked at the cave mouth, and back. "He's paid. He's paid every mark. There's nothing left in him."

"There's something left in him," said Edric. "There's his life. It's what he took from my father. He can pay that too."

Rook looked at him for a long time. Then he looked away, at the broken arches of the cloister, at the hills.

"You saved my neck once," he said. "On a gallows in the south, with the rope round it. I said then I'd do anything you asked." He unfolded his arms. "I'll do this. Water, no bread. But I'll not stand at the door and watch it. You can do that yourself."

"I will," said Edric.

---

He did.

He sat on the broken step outside the cave mouth for three days, in the cold, with his coat round him and his back against the rock, and the thread of Lyse Valtor's life lying faint and far beside his heart, counting down. *Fourteen. Thirteen.* He told himself he stayed to make sure it was done. He did not let himself ask what else he was staying for.

Dorran did not beg. That surprised him. On the first day he called out, once or twice, in a voice like dry paper, Edric's name; and when Edric did not answer, he stopped. After that, there was only the sound of him breathing, very shallow, in the dark at the back of the cave, and now and then the scrape of the water-jug on the stone.

On the second night, he asked for a pen.

Edric did not have one. He took a stick of charcoal from the dead fire in the cloister and went into the cave and put it in the man's hand, and went out again. He did not ask what it was for. He heard it, all that night and into the morning, very slowly: a faint, steady, scratching sound, stopping and starting, like a mouse in a wall.

Like Faro, digging.

He put his hands over his ears. After a while he took them away again, because it did not help.

On the third evening, the scratching stopped. Dorran's breathing went on a while longer, slower and slower, and then it stopped too, and Edric sat on the step outside in the dark and felt the last of the water in the man go still, like a pool at the end of a drought, and found that he could not get up.

---

He went in at dawn, with a lantern.

Dorran was lying on the straw on his side, with the blanket over him and his face to the wall, the way he had been on the eighth day. He looked very small. He looked like a child. Edric did not look at him for long.

He looked at the wall.

It was covered in figures. From the floor to as high as a man lying down could reach, in charcoal, in a neat, small, careful hand that grew less neat as it went down: columns. Dates and sums. Every withdrawal from the Blight Fund, for eleven years, from memory, with what it had gone to. *Grain ships, northern harvest. Loan, the Empress. Sardh bonds.* Every repayment, which were few. Every loss. The mirror-tower day. The Count of Cristal's drafts. The saddlebags. The meals: a hundred thousand marks, a hundred thousand marks, a hundred thousand marks, all the way down.

And at the bottom, where the hand had almost given out, a line drawn across, and under it, two words.

*Balance: Vane.*

Under that, so faint he had to hold the lantern close to read it, a last line, the letters wandering:

*Send it to the towns. Please.*

---

He sent it to the towns.

Rook brought the strongbox down out of the hills, every letter of credit untouched, and Edric sent it to the Ministry of the Treasury in Solace with an anonymous letter, and a list, in his own hand, of every beacon on the coast that had failed in the storm and every fishing town where the nets came up empty and every hospital where children were dying of the blight-cough. *For the purpose for which it was raised.*

He did not sign it *The Tide remembers.* He found, when he came to write the words, that he could not.

They buried Dorran Quell in the cloister of the ruined monastery, under a cairn of stones, Rook's men, without a priest. Rook did not come. Edric stood by the cairn alone, in the wind, and waited to feel it. The sweetness. Nineteen years.

It did not come. What came instead was very slow and very cold and very patient, rising from far down, and it was not his.

*Was it sweet, little one?* said the Deep.

He did not answer. He got on his horse and rode down out of the Greyhills towards the coast, and it was three nights before he could bring himself to go down to the sea.

### Later

She came to Cristal in the second summer after the Teeth fell, on a merchant ship from Port Jasmine, with a harp case and one small bag, and asked on the quay for the Warden.

Edric knew her at once, though he had seen her only once, across the great hall of the house of white marble, in white, with her chin up and her face like stone. Elsabet Quell was twenty-one now. She had cut her hair short, in the southern fashion, and her hands had a musician's calluses, and she looked at him on the quay at the inner end of the harbour with Dorran's clever, measuring, pale eyes, which was strange, because they were not Dorran's eyes at all.

"A boy came to Port Jasmine," she said. "With a squint, and a tooth missing. He said he'd been in the Greyhills with Vasco Rook, and he'd carried my father's meals. He said I ought to know where the grave was." She did not move. "He said you sat outside the door."

"Yes," said Edric.

"For three days."

"Yes."

She looked out at the harbour for a long time.

"He wasn't my father," said Elsabet Quell. "Did you know? My mother told me, the year I was twelve, in a temper. Old Haverel's. Born just before he died. Dorran married her for the bank and got me in the bargain." Her voice was quite steady. "He raised me anyway. He never said a word about it. He was a cold, frightened, greedy little man, and he wanted to sell me to a murderer for a fortune in Port Jasmine, and he was the only person in that house who ever came to hear me play." She picked up her harp case. "I wanted you to know that. That's all. I didn't come for anything else."

She went back up the gangway, and the ship sailed on the evening tide.

In Saltmere, the hospital used the Blight Fund money well, that year, and the next. But no other money ever came. No small monthly sum by a ship from the south, no line in a neat careful hand. There was no one left to send it.

## Take the money

*Heart − · Tide 0*

"I'm so tired of being afraid," Dorran had said. And Edric had let the stillness go, and Dorran had whispered his name.

Edric sat back on his heels on the straw, and looked at the man for a long time.

He had ridden up the ravine through the night to stop this. He had meant to. He had the bread in his coat. And he had felt, kneeling here with the jug to Dorran's lips, the thing he had felt since the grey house: the old man's eyes. *Enough.* He had been ready to say it. He had the words. *I forgive you.*

And then he had looked past Dorran, at the saddlebags Rook's men had piled by the cave wall when they emptied them. Two million marks. And he had thought of a gold light at the top of a white tower, and twelve ships he had not yet seen but already knew would come, and an island in the west with walls built to keep out crabs.

He thought: *Forgiving him is one thing. What do I do with the money?*

He thought: *And what do I do with him?*

If he forgave him and sent him south, Dorran would go to a city where nobody knew his name, and live. If he sent the money back to the Treasury, the Treasury would hand it to the King. And the King would build beacons for the towns he cared about and leave the rest of the coast in the dark, as he always had, and spend whatever was left on mirrors for his ships.

And the auditors had been in the Treasury for nine days now, with the ledgers open, finding the hole.

"You'll want to know what happens now," he said.

Dorran looked up at him from the straw.

"You're going back to Solace," said Edric. "Rook's men will take you. They'll leave you on the steps of the Treasury, at dawn, with your black ledger round your neck on a string. The one you kept by your bed. I know where it is. The auditors will find you there when they come in to work." He stood. "And when they ask you where the two million marks have gone, you'll tell them the truth, which is that you don't know. Because you won't."

Dorran stared at him. His lips moved.

"They'll hang me," he said.

"No," said Edric. "I don't think so. The King doesn't hang Lord Treasurers. It's bad for confidence." He looked down at the man on the straw. "I think there'll be a Sun Warrant. Treason, the details of which are a danger to the Throne. Natural life." He was quiet a moment. "There's a black rock half a day's sail from Saltmere. You'll have heard of it. I'm told there are two empty cells."

He watched it go into Dorran. He watched it reach the bottom.

"Fourteen years, Dorran," said Edric Vane. "I counted every day of them. Let's see how you count."

Dorran did not beg. Edric had thought he would. He lay on the straw for a long time with the bread forgotten in his hand, looking up at the rock ceiling, and his lips moved, very slightly, the way they must have moved every morning for eleven years on the edge of his bed. Doing a sum.

"Two million," he said at last, in his dry paper voice. "And the fifty thousand I'd put by for the house in the south. And the deeds. You'll want the deeds. There's a vineyard with it." He turned his head on the straw. "It's a good sum, Edric. Clean. No one will ever trace it, if you move it through Corrow. I'd move it through Corrow." He closed his eyes. "I'm sorry. I can't help it. It's the only thing I know how to do."

Edric went out into the cloister. Rook was waiting by the broken fountain with his arms folded, and he had heard; the cave mouth carried sound like a shell.

"The Keep," said Rook. He said it the way a man names a disease. "My lord, I've had men go to the Keep. None of them came back. I'd sooner you'd let me cut his throat."

"You were told not to touch a hair of his head," said Edric. "You still are. Take him down tonight. Take the gold west, to the *Slip*, at Harnmouth. Every mark."

Rook looked at him for a long moment, and did not argue, and did not look at him again for the rest of the day.

---

The auditors of the Ministry of the Treasury found the Lord Treasurer of Valemont at six o'clock the next morning but one, sitting on the top step of the Treasury in the rain, in a brown cloak, barefoot, as thin as a rail, with a small black leather ledger hanging round his neck on a piece of tarred string. He did not speak when they spoke to him. He only held out the ledger.

The *Courant* had the rest within the week. The Blight Fund was gone: four million marks, over eleven years. The ledger accounted for every withdrawal. The last two million had been carried out of Solace in the Lord Treasurer's saddlebags, and he said he had been robbed of them in the Greyhills by bandits, and nobody believed him. A Sun Warrant was signed, by the new High Justicar's deputy, three days later. No trial. The details being a danger to the Throne.

Edric watched the prison carriage go down to the harbour below the cliffs. He watched it from a closed carriage of his own, at the top of the quay, with the curtain open a finger's width. A black-hulled ship was waiting at the end of the quay, the same kind of ship that had once waited at Saltmere, with its sails furled and its oars run out.

They brought Dorran out of the carriage in chains. He stood on the quay in the grey morning, very small, between two guards, and looked once, slowly, all around him, the way a man looks at a city he will not see again. His eyes passed over the closed carriage at the top of the quay. They stopped. They stayed on it for a long moment, on the finger's width of open curtain.

Then the guards took his arms, and he went up the gangway, and did not look back.

---

The two million went west. Every mark. The *Slip* carried it, in iron-bound chests, and Jac signed for it on the quay at Cristal, and Tam wrote it into the island's ledger in his careful hand, under a heading he had invented himself and did not explain: *Received, of the Warden.*

Hayda found Edric that night on the roof of the house with the blue door.

"I read the *Courant*," she said.

"Yes."

"You did to him what they did to you." She stood beside him at the parapet, with her small hands folded. "The steps. The warrant. The ship. The cell."

"Yes."

"I keep waiting for you to say you had a reason."

"I had several," said Edric. "They're all good. The King's coming. Cristal needs walls. The money would only have gone to him." He looked down at the dark harbour. "They're all true. I'm not sure they're why."

Hayda was quiet for a long time.

"Then what was it all for?" she said. "The fourteen years. If at the end of it you're the one sending a man to the Keep." She did not wait for an answer. She went back down the stairs, and left him with the wind and the city and the light at his back, burning a little brighter than it used to.

### Later

He felt it from the bottom of the longboat, as the Teeth came apart.

He was lying on his back with Mira's shawl over him and his head in Hayda's lap, emptier than he had ever been in his life, the Tidemark gone, the sea gone out of him, given back, all of it. He could barely feel the water under the boat. He could barely feel his own hands. But as the great black mountain cracked down the middle behind them, from the summit to the waterline, and the walls began to fall, he felt one thing more, very faint, very far, the last thread of Undertow he would ever have.

A man. High up in the rock, in the old part of the Keep, where the cells were cut. Thin water, slow and frightened, running in tight small circles like a fish in a bucket. He knew it. He had felt it in a white marble house, and a counting-house, and a cave in the Greyhills. Counting. Even now. Counting the cracks in the wall.

Eight paces long, he thought. Five paces wide. A slit in the wall, too high to reach.

He tried to lift his head. He tried to reach for the sea, for anything, a wave, a hand of water, the way he could have once, without thinking, the way he had driven a ship ten days across an ocean. There was nothing there. He had given it back. There was only a thin, grey, tired man lying in the bottom of a boat, and the rock falling.

The tower leaned, and leaned, and fell. The sea took the walls, and the stairs, and the corridors, and the cells.

The thin frightened current stopped counting.

"Edric," said Hayda, above him. "Edric, what is it?"

He did not tell her. He lay in the bottom of the boat and watched the sun come up over the place where the Teeth had been, and it did not hurt him any more. It was only warm. And he thought that the sea had taken it all, gently, the way it took everything in the end, and that it had not asked, as a wave never asks, whether the cliff deserved to fall. And that this time, the wave had been him.
