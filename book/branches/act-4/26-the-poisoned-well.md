# Event 26: The Poisoned Well

*Branches from Chapter 21, "Currents of Gold": the morning after the false mirror signal, when Edric, who "did not reach for the bowl again that night", is offered a quicker way to finish Dorran Quell, through the grain in his warehouses.*

## Do it

*Heart −− · Tide 0*

The man Bastien brought up to the study the next morning was called Kettle, and he looked like one: short, round, black-bottomed with old soot, and always, somewhere inside, on the boil.

He had run brandy through the Reach for twenty years before Bastien found him, and for the last three he had been Thornbury and Wilde's factor on the Salt Quay at Lowharbour, the crowded, stinking, fish-scale quarter at the foot of the cliffs where the white city kept its docks and its poor. He sat on the edge of the chair in front of the Count's desk with his hat on his knees and talked fast, the way men talk who have been turning an idea over in their heads for a week and are afraid of losing it.

"Grain, my lord," said Kettle. "That's where he's soft. Not the bank. Everybody watches the bank. The grain."

Edric waited.

"Seven years ago the Baron broke the northern merchants. Bought up the whole harvest out of the north, three years running, with ships of his own, and sold it in Solace a hair under their price till they went under. Now there's no grain comes into this city but his. Seven granaries on the Salt Quay, long stone sheds, full to the roofs. Every baker in Lowharbour buys from him, because there's nobody else to buy from. And the price goes up a penny every month, and nobody says a word, because what can you say?" Kettle leaned forward. "And here's the thing, my lord. He's borrowed against it. Every sack. To the Vossens, to the Bank of the North, to half the Street of Gold. That grain's holding up his credit the way a prop holds up a mine."

"Go on."

"Those sheds are old. Older than the city, near enough. They're built on top of the old cisterns, from before the King's time, when the quay was something else. My lads have been down there. There are sluice-tunnels running out from under the floors to the harbour, bricked over at the sea end. Rotten brick. Open them at the top of a spring tide, and the sea comes up through the floors like water up through a sponge." He spread his hands. "Brine in the grain. It sprouts, it sours, it rots. In a week it's not worth the shovelling. And every man who lent the Baron money on it will be at his door the same morning."

Edric sat very still.

He did not need the bowl. He could feel it as Kettle spoke: the grain in its seven sheds, dry and gold and heavy, and the currents running out from it into the city, thin and many, like roots. Pennies. Thousands of them. Pulled up out of Lowharbour every morning in bread and carried up the cliff to the house of white marble. It was one of the great currents of the city, and he had felt it before without knowing what it was. A slow pull, from the bottom of Solace to the top.

"And Lowharbour?" he said.

Kettle shrugged. "They'll go short. A month. Six weeks. Bread dear for a while. Then the southern ships come in, or the Vossens bring grain down from the north again, now there's room." He turned his hat on his knees. "It's the Baron that made them depend on him, my lord. Not us. We'd only be showing them what it was they were depending on."

"And it won't cost you a drop, my lord," said Bastien quietly, from the door. "Not one. That's why I brought him."

Edric looked at him. Bastien looked back, with his black eyes, and his face like a closed door. He knew. Bastien always knew. He had seen the Count come down to breakfast that morning grey to the lips, with the salt-sickness on him after the night at the bowl, and the Mark a finger further up his cheek.

*Not by pulling,* he had told himself. *By letting him pull himself.* And here was a way that was not pulling at all. He would not have to touch the sea. He would not have to reach, or draw, or drown anything. He would only have to say a word, and other men would open a rotten wall, and the sea would do what the sea always did, which was come in.

And Dorran would fall in a fortnight, not a season.

He thought of Hob Fennick's granddaughter, chasing a chicken round a well. Of the blight-cough, and the beacons that were never built, and four million marks gone into grain ships and southern loans. Into these sheds. Every sack of that grain had been bought with money raised from the fishing towns. The Lowharbour bakers had been paying for it twice: once in the tax, and once in the bread.

"Six weeks," he said.

"Six weeks, my lord. Two months at the outside."

"Do it," said the Count of Cristal.

---

The spring tide came nine nights later, at an hour past midnight, and he went down to watch.

He told himself he went to make sure no one was hurt. He stood in the dark at the end of the Salt Quay, in a plain coat with the collar up, with the seven long sheds of the Quell granaries black against the stars behind him and the harbour lapping at the piles below, and felt Kettle's men under his feet: four of them, down in the old tunnels, with lanterns and crowbars, working at the brick.

He felt the sea feel them.

It came up the harbour on the flood, full and black and slow, and lay against the bricked mouths of the tunnels, and leaned. He had not touched it. He had promised himself he would not. But it knew he was there; it always knew. He felt it press against the rotten brick the way a dog presses against a door when it hears its master on the other side.

The first wall went at a quarter past one. He heard it through the planking: a crack, a rush, a man's yell, half laughing, half afraid, and feet running back up the tunnel. Then the second. Then all of them. And the sea went in.

It went in quietly. That was what he would remember. No roar, no wave. Only a long, low, contented sound, like a sigh, under the quay, under the sheds, as the harbour water climbed the old cisterns and found the floors and came up through them, the way Kettle had said, like water through a sponge. He felt it spread through the grain. Felt the dry gold weight of it go dark and heavy and cold, sack by sack, row by row, in the dark.

He tasted salt. He had not reached for anything. He tasted it anyway.

*Yes,* said something, very far down, very faint, almost fond. *Like that.*

He walked home up the cliff stair before dawn, and did not look back, and did not sleep.

---

The smell reached the Street of Lanterns on the third day.

It came up the cliffs on the sea wind: a sour, wet, sweetish stink, like a brewery gone bad, like a cellar where something has died. By the fifth day you could smell it in the Sunspire gardens. By the seventh, the grain in the Quell granaries had begun to sprout and blacken and steam, and men with shovels and scarves over their faces were wheeling it out of the sheds in barrows and tipping it into the harbour, where it lay on the water in a grey scum, and the gulls would not touch it.

Dorran came down to the Salt Quay himself on the second morning. Edric did not see it. Kettle told him: how the Lord Treasurer had stood in the doorway of the first shed in his fine grey coat, with the brine over his shoes, and looked at the dark wet heaps going green in the half-light, and said nothing at all for a long time. And then had asked, quite quietly, how much grain was left in the city. And been told: none. None that was not his. None that was not this.

His creditors came on the ninth day. All of them, the same morning, as Kettle had promised. By the end of the month, the hole in the Blight Fund had grown by another six hundred thousand marks, and the house of Quell was paying its depositors out of next week's deposits, and the Lord Treasurer had stopped going home.

And in Lowharbour, the price of a loaf went from three pennies to eight. Then to twelve. Then the bakers stopped baking, because there was nothing to bake.

---

He went down on the twentieth day, as Master Wilde.

It was the oldest of his faces and the easiest: a fussy, foreign, bespectacled shipping agent with a cane and a high complaining voice, the kind of man nobody looks at twice. He walked along the Salt Quay and up into the lanes behind it, where the tenements leaned together over the gutters so close that the washing on the lines touched across the street, and he looked.

There was a queue outside the last bakery in Lowharbour that still had its shutters up. It went round the corner and down the hill, three hundred people, perhaps more: women with shawls over their heads and baskets on their arms, old men, children sent by their mothers, standing in the cold in silence. The baker came out at noon and said there would be forty loaves, and no more until Thursday, and the queue did not shout or push. It only went very quiet, and very still, the way a crowd goes when it has been hungry long enough that shouting seems like a waste of strength.

A girl of about seven was standing near the end, holding a smaller boy by the hand. She had a cough. Not the blight-cough; only a cold, the ordinary kind that a fed child shakes off in a week. She had had it a long time, by the sound of it.

Edric stood across the lane in Master Wilde's spectacles and felt the water in her: thin, and slow, and low, like a well at the end of a dry summer.

He went home and told Bastien to buy bread. All of it. Every loaf for sale between here and Harnmouth, and every sack of flour, and have it given out on the Salt Quay, in the Count's name or in no name at all, he did not care.

Bastien came back two days later.

"There isn't any," he said. "Not to buy. Not at any price. The Baron bought up the coast years ago, the same as the north. What there was, the city took in the first week. The southern ships are a month out." He looked at the Count. "I can buy gold, my lord. I can buy a whole street. I can't buy bread that isn't there."

Edric stood at the window of the study and looked down at the harbour, at the grey scum still drifting on the water under the Salt Quay, with the gulls wheeling over it and not coming down.

He had felt the currents of the city. He had felt them pulling from the bottom of Solace to the top, and he had thought he was cutting the rope at the top. He had not thought about what the rope was tied to at the bottom.

Hayda came up behind him. She had been down to Lowharbour herself that morning, without telling him, in a plain shawl, with a basket of the house's own bread.

"There's a saying in my country," she said. "About wells."

"I know it."

"I don't think you do." She stood beside him and looked down at the harbour. "It isn't that he who poisons a well drinks from it too. Everyone says that. It isn't true. Rich men never drink from the wells they poison." She turned her head. "It's that the well doesn't know whose it is. You've learned to drown things without touching the water, Edric. I didn't know that was possible. I don't think it's better."

He did not answer. Across the city, in a house of white marble, he could feel Dorran Quell sitting on the edge of his bed in the dark, with his ledger open, and his hands shaking. And for the first time, he could not find in himself whether he was glad.

### Later

They called it the bread winter in Lowharbour, afterwards. The southern ships came in at last, five weeks late, and the price came down by spring, but not before the old and the very young had paid it. Nobody kept a count. Nobody ever does, in places like that.

Edric heard the name again on the first terrace of Cristal, on the Long Day, a little after noon.

The watch had taken prisoners when they won the terrace back the second time: three Radiants, wounded, dragged up behind the barricade. One of them was hardly more than a boy, seventeen or eighteen, with a burnt hand and a novice's plain white surcoat, and a Lowharbour voice he had not quite trained out of himself. Edric came down from the citadel to look at them, between beams, with the smoke drifting over the stair.

The boy looked up, and saw the Tidemark, and knew him.

"Count of Cristal," he said. He did not say it like a title. "My mother cleaned the Vossens' counting-house. She heard them laughing about it. How the Baron's sheds flooded the same week the Count of Cristal's factor bought up the tunnel leases. They thought it was clever." He held his burnt hand against his chest. "My sister was four. She died in the bread winter. A cough. Only a cough. She'd have shaken it off if she'd been fed." His eyes were very steady. "The Order feeds its novices. Twice a day. That's why I joined. That's why I'm here."

Jac, beside Edric, said nothing. Hayda, on the stair above, said nothing.

"I'm sorry," said Edric.

"I know who you are," said the boy. "They told us on the ships. The tide-taken Warden. They said you drown children." He laughed, very short. "They had the wrong reason. But they weren't wrong."

Edric stood on the first terrace of Cristal with the sunfire falling on his city, and found that he had no answer. He had not touched the water. He had never touched the water. He went back up to the roof, to wait for the dark, and the taste of salt was in his mouth all the way.

## Undercut his prices with Cristal's trade instead

*Heart + · Tide 0*

Edric heard Kettle out to the end: the seven granaries on the Salt Quay, the old cisterns under their floors, the rotten brick at the sea end of the tunnels, the spring tide nine nights off. The grain that held up Dorran's credit like a prop in a mine. *It won't cost you a drop, my lord,* Bastien had said, bringing him in. And it would not. That was the worst of it.

"And Lowharbour?" he said.

"They'll go short," said Kettle. "Six weeks. Bread dear for a while."

Edric looked at the bowl of still water in the corner of his desk. He did not touch it. He did not need to. He could feel the grain from here: the thin gold roots of it going down into the lanes at the foot of the cliffs, a penny at a time, three pennies a loaf, every morning, in ten thousand kitchens.

*Not by pulling,* he had told himself, the night before.

He thought of the smile he had found on his own face, afterwards. The one he had not put there.

"No," he said.

Kettle blinked. "My lord?"

"No," said the Count of Cristal. "Leave his walls alone. Leave his sea alone." He drew a sheet of paper towards him and uncapped the ink. "You say every baker in Lowharbour buys from him because there's nobody else to buy from. Very well. We'll be somebody else."

---

It cost more than he had thought. Everything did.

His own fortune was not the difficulty; it was the means. Every mark the Count of Cristal drew in Solace went through Dorran's bank. That was the whole design: the unlimited credit, the drafts, the steady bleeding of the reserves. If he paid for grain out of the house of Quell, he would be propping up the very house he meant to bring down. And Thornbury and Wilde's gold, in Port Jasmine, was a month away by sea, and spoken for twice over.

So it had to be Cristal.

He wrote to the council that night, and to Tam, in the plain hand he used for the island and nobody else. He asked for the money set aside for the new harbour wall, the stone one that was to replace the old Tidewarden breakwater before the winter storms, and for the school roof, and for the second hospital ward. He asked for the *Slip* and the three Corrow ships to go to Mazrah and the southern ports and buy grain, Sardhi wheat and southern rice and anything else that would make bread, and bring it to Solace, to a factor called Kettle on the Salt Quay. He told them it would be sold at a loss. He told them it might be a year before they saw the money again, and possibly never.

Tam's answer came back by the *Slip*, five weeks later, with the first cargo. It was very short, and very correct, and Edric could hear the young schoolmaster's voice in every line of it.

*The council has voted the funds, eight to four. I voted against. The wall will wait until next summer. Olaf says the Sun will look after the breakwater. I said that was what I was afraid of. The school will meet in the Court of Tides until the roof is mended. The children are delighted. — T.*

Edric read it twice, and put it in his desk, and did not laugh, but very nearly.

---

The shop opened on the Salt Quay in the second week of summer, in an old chandler's warehouse three doors down from the first of the Quell granaries, under a new painted sign: WILDE & CO. GRAIN & FLOUR. SOUTHERN WHEAT.

Master Wilde ran it himself.

He stood behind the counter most mornings in his spectacles and his fussy foreign coat, with a pen behind his ear and a ledger he did not need, and sold flour to the bakers of Lowharbour at two pennies a measure under the Baron's price. Then three. The bakers did not trust him at first. They came in, one or two, and fingered the sacks, and smelled the flour, and asked what was wrong with it. Then they bought a little, and baked it, and came back.

The first to come back was a widow called Pask, who kept an oven at the top of Tanners' Lane and had fed half the street on credit through two winters. She was a big, slow, grey-haired woman with forearms like a docker's and flour in the creases of her knuckles that would never wash out, and she stood at Master Wilde's counter for a long time, looking at him over the sacks.

"You're losing money on every measure," she said at last. "I can count, mister. I know what it costs to bring wheat up from Mazrah. You're selling it for less than the freight."

"I am," said Master Wilde, in his high complaining voice.

"Why?"

He thought about it, polishing his spectacles.

"I had a father," he said, "who went hungry once, for want of a few marks that a cleverer man had put somewhere else. I've never forgiven the cleverer man. This seems a better way of saying so than some I could think of."

Widow Pask looked at him for a long moment with her small shrewd eyes. Then she bought twelve sacks, and paid in copper, and went away up Tanners' Lane without another word. The next morning, there were fourteen bakers in the shop.

---

Dorran came himself in the sixth week.

He did not give his name. He did not need to. He came in on foot, alone, at the end of the day, when the bakers had gone, in a plain grey coat that cost more than the shop, and stood just inside the door with his small neat hands clasped behind his back, looking at the sacks, and the scales, and the ledger, and the fussy foreign agent behind the counter.

Edric looked up at him over Master Wilde's spectacles, and held the stillness, and felt the water in the man: quick, and cold, and very frightened, all of it, every drop, running in tight circles like fish in a bucket.

"Master Wilde," said Dorran Quell, with his small smile. "I've heard a great deal about you. You've been taking my customers."

"I've been selling flour, sir."

"At a loss."

"At a loss."

Dorran walked slowly along the row of sacks. He put out a finger and touched one, and rubbed the flour between his finger and thumb, and smelled it, the way the bakers did.

"Sardhi wheat," he said. "Good. Better than mine." He turned. "I'll buy you out. All of it. The stock, the lease, the name. I'll pay you twice what you've spent. You can go back to wherever it is you came from with a profit, and I'll forget you were ever here."

"No, thank you."

"Three times."

"No."

Dorran stood very still in the middle of the shop.

"I don't understand you," he said, and for a moment, just a moment, the small smile was not there. "I've looked at your figures. My people have. You're bleeding. You'll be bleeding for a year before I break, if I ever do. My granaries are full. I can sell at a loss as long as you can." He took a step towards the counter. "What do you *want*?"

Edric looked at him across the sacks. At the pale, neat, clever face. At the hands, clasped behind his back to keep them still.

"I want the bread in Lowharbour to cost what it costs," said Master Wilde. "That's all. You can match my price, sir, any morning you like. I'd be delighted."

Dorran stared at him. Then he laughed, very short, the dry laugh of a man who has been handed a sum that will not come out.

"You're mad," he said.

He went out. Edric watched him go, down the Salt Quay, past his own seven granaries, in the dusk.

He did match the price. He had to. He matched it the next morning, and the morning after, and for every penny he took off his bread, Edric felt it: a thin new crack, very far down, in the great house of white marble. Not a flood. Nothing so quick. Only grain sitting in sheds that had been bought dear and must now be sold cheap, against notes that had been written on its old value, month after month, while the drafts from the Count of Cristal kept coming. It would take a season. Perhaps more. It would cost Cristal its wall.

That night, for the first time since he had come to Solace, the Count of Cristal ate his supper at his own table in company, a plain loaf from Widow Pask's oven, and Hayda watched him do it, and said nothing, and passed the salt.

### Later

The fleet burned the warehouses on the Long Day. Every one: the old Tidewarden sheds along the harbour where the island kept its stores, its grain and oil and salt fish, its winter. The beams came across the water at an hour past dawn and the stone ran like wax, and by nightfall there was nothing left behind the quays but slag and smoke. After the Teeth fell, after the sea turned blue, Cristal had four thousand people, eleven hundred Radiant prisoners, a broken harbour wall that had never been rebuilt in stone, and food for nine days.

On the sixth day, sails came up from the east.

Jac saw them first, from the ruined harbour tower, and swore, and rang the bell, and the whole island came running down the terraces with whatever it could carry, thinking the King had come back from the dead. But they were not white. They were brown and patched and grey, every colour of old canvas, eleven fishing boats and coasters and a fat Solace grain barge that had no business out of sight of land, riding low in the water, coming in through the reefs in a long ragged line behind a pilot from the Weir.

The barge came alongside what was left of the quay. A big, slow, grey-haired woman stood in its bow with her forearms folded.

"We heard you were hungry," said Widow Pask. She looked up the quay at the thin grey man standing at the end of it with the Tidemark gone from his face, and narrowed her small shrewd eyes. "You'll be Master Wilde, then. Without the spectacles." She sniffed. "The bakers' guild of Lowharbour sends forty tons of flour, at cost. Freight's paid. We had a whip-round." She jerked her head at the boats behind her. "Some of the lads brought fish. They said the sea's full of it, all of a sudden. They didn't know why."

Edric stood on the quay and found that he could not speak.

"Well," said Widow Pask. "Are you going to help unload it, or stand there?"

He helped unload it. It took all day. Tam stood at the top of the quay with a ledger, writing down every sack, and when it was done he closed the book and looked at Edric for a long time, and then said, with a great deal of dignity: "The harbour wall can wait until next summer."
