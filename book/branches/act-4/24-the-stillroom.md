# Event 24: The Stillroom

*Branches from Chapter 22, "The House of Valtor": in Heloise Valtor's stillroom, when she asks about drugs "that bring a sleep that does not end", and Edric thinks, "He could have said no."*

*The canon path, telling her about blackwater, continues in [Chapter 22](../../22-the-house-of-valtor.md) and leads to the deaths in [Chapter 24](../../24-still-water.md) and [Chapter 25](../../25-the-trial.md).*

## Refuse to tell Heloise

*Heart + · Tide 0*

He felt the currents in her. He felt what she wanted, cold and hard and patient, pulling her husband's fortune down the stairs and along the corridor to the room where a fat solemn boy lay asleep. He thought of Lyse at the end of the table, bracing for the wave. He thought of Valtor in a warm study, holding a letter in the fire until the flame reached his fingertips.

And he felt, very clearly, something open in front of him, like a door in a wall. A word. Only a word. He knew the word. He could say it, and step back, and watch what she did with it, and none of it would be his.

*A sleep that does not end.*

He laughed.

It was a good laugh: light, a little rueful, the laugh of a foreign count at a charming hostess's charming morbid curiosity. He had learned it in the salons of Port Jasmine. It cost him more than he would have believed.

"Madame," he said, "the Sardhi physicians have a saying. There is no such thing as a draught that brings a sleep that does not end. There is only a physician who has stopped watching." He shook his head. "The bazaars in Mazrah are full of men who'll sell you that sort of thing. Little bottles of coloured water, with a story attached. Love philtres. Inheritance draughts. Elixirs of youth. The rich buy them by the crate. They're a great deal more dangerous to the buyer's purse than to anyone's health."

Heloise Valtor looked at him for a moment longer, her head on one side, her pale bright eyes very steady.

"How disappointing," she said lightly. "I'd hoped you'd tell me something wicked." She set the amber phial back on its shelf, among the others. "But I'm glad, really. One hears such stories about the south. Shall we go down? My husband will think I've stolen you."

He felt the want in her as they went down the stairs. It had not gone. It had only turned aside, the way water turns aside from a stone, feeling along the edge of it for the next way round.

---

He did not sleep that night either. He sat in the study that had been Valtor's bedroom, at the window where Valtor had watched the ships come in, and did not light a lamp. And there was no voice telling him that he had done nothing, that it was her sin, not his. He had done nothing. That was true, this time. It did not make him feel any better.

*She'll find another way,* he thought. *A woman like that. She'll find it in a book, or from an apothecary, or she'll think of something no one has thought of. And I'll have done nothing, and I'll have to watch it.*

He had promised an old man. He had not yet sat in the chair across from Severin Valtor and let him look at his face; that was still days away. But he knew, sitting in the dark, that when he did, the old man's eyes would turn to the door and go fierce, and the board would say *Lyse*. He knew it the way he knew the tide.

So he watched.

He did not use the bowl. The Far Seeing cost too much, and he had a feeling, very strongly, that he would need everything he had before this was done. He used Bastien. Bastien had men on the Street of Lanterns within a day: a knife-grinder who set up his wheel on the corner, an old woman who sold lavender by the Valtors' gate, a boy who held horses. They watched the grey house. They watched who went in and who came out, and what was carried, and where Madame Valtor's maid went on her afternoons off.

Through the end of the summer, and the beginning of autumn, nothing happened at all.

The old Marquis of Aubray came up from his estates in the north for the season with the old Marquise, as he did every year, and stayed with his son-in-law, and caught a cold in the first week of the autumn rains, and was dosed with honey and hot wine, and recovered. Old Noll fetched his master's breakfast every morning and drank the old man's wine on the tray when he thought no one was looking, and lived. Lyse Valtor ate at her father's table and endured her stepmother's small sharp jokes and went to her window at night to talk to a young man in the alley, and nothing came up the stairs to her in a glass. Heloise Valtor paid calls, and gave little dinners, and worked in her stillroom among her copper stills and her bunches of dried herbs, and watched.

And then, in the week after the Lord Marshal fell on his sword, the lavender woman came to the house with the blue door and told Bastien that Madame Valtor's maid had carried a letter, sealed, not to any lady's house but to a set of rooms off the Street of Gold, where a young southern lord lodged who was to marry Baron Quell's daughter.

---

Edric felt it the next evening, at a reception at the Vossens', across a crowded room.

He had not been looking for it. He had been talking to the young secretary from the Ministry of the Interior, about nothing, and he felt it go past him the way you feel a cold draught from a door you did not know was open: a current, thin and sharp and quick, running between two people on opposite sides of the room. From a small, pretty, plump woman in pale blue by the fireplace, with her head on one side. To a tall, slim, beautiful young man by the window, laughing at something a Radiant lord's wife had said.

It ran from her to him. Want, and purpose, and a cold, careful, patient satisfaction, like a woman who has found the right tool at last. And from him, back to her, nothing. Nothing at all. A smooth flat surface, like a mirror, reflecting her own want back at her and giving her nothing to hold on to. Edric had felt it in a gambling den near the docks, in the spring. A well dry all the way down.

She had found him. Of course she had. She missed nothing. She had stood on the gravel path at Larkmere, the night the Baroness fainted, and while everyone else looked at the fainting woman, she had looked at the young man smiling by the roses, and seen what Edric had seen. A pair of hands that would do anything, and feel nothing after.

*She can't use poison,* he thought, standing very still with a glass in his hand. *Not on a girl of nineteen in good health. Any physician would see it. She needs an accident. A robbery. A death that has nothing to do with the house at all.*

And then, with a cold that went all the way to the bottom of him: *Maxim.*

The alley behind the garden wall, where a young Guard captain stood at night under a window and talked to a girl. Heloise would know about that. Heloise missed nothing. A girl found dead under her own window at midnight, and a secret lover seen in the alley, and his officer's knife in the gutter. A quarrel. A broken engagement. Everyone would believe it. The High Justicar's daughter, and a captain of the Guard hanged for it on the Sunspire steps, and the fortune of the Aubrays going quietly to a fat solemn boy of nine.

Across the room, Lord Andric Cavell turned his head from the window and looked at him, and smiled.

---

Bastien's boy who held horses saw Andric buy a knife the next afternoon, a plain sailor's knife, from a chandler on the waterfront. Not a gentleman's blade. The kind of knife a Guard captain might carry off duty.

Edric waited in the alley that night.

It was a narrow lane between high walls, behind the gardens on the Street of Lanterns, with a gutter down the middle and the backs of the great houses rising on either side, dark, with here and there a lit window. He stood in a doorway in the wall opposite the Valtors' garden, in the dark, with the Still Water on him like a cloak, so that anyone who looked would see a doorway, and shadows, and nothing.

At eleven, Maxim Moren came down the alley in his white and gold, and stood under a window on the second floor, and threw a little gravel against the glass, and the window opened, and a girl's dark head looked out. They talked for an hour, in whispers, he in the alley and she at the sill. Edric did not listen. He watched the far end of the lane.

At midnight, Maxim kissed his hand to the window and went away, whistling under his breath.

At a quarter past, the window was still open, and the girl was still at it, looking down at the place where he had stood, the way girls do. And at the far end of the alley, a slim figure came over the wall from the garden of the house beyond, light as a cat, and dropped into the lane without a sound, and began to walk towards the Valtors' garden gate, which had been left unlatched, keeping to the shadows, with something in its hand.

Edric stepped out of the doorway into its path.

Andric stopped. He did not start, or reach for the knife, or run. He stood in the middle of the alley in the dark and looked at the tall figure in front of him, and Edric let the Still Water go, a little, so that the young man could see whose face it was.

"Oh," said Lord Andric Cavell, quite softly, and smiled. "It's you." He sounded delighted. "I did wonder. At Larkmere. All that business with the box." He tilted his head. "Is she yours, then? The girl at the window? I'd no idea. Madame Valtor didn't mention it."

"Go home, Andric."

"She's offered me two thousand marks," said Andric conversationally. "Half now. I've had the half. Isn't that funny? A woman like that, with her little stills. She thinks she's terribly wicked. She has no idea." He turned the sailor's knife over in his fingers, idly, so that it caught the light from the window above. "I'd have done it for the fun of watching her face at the funeral, really. The money's just manners."

"Go home," said Edric again, "or don't go anywhere, ever again."

He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. He felt the water in the gutter at their feet begin to move, and the water in the young man's body, and the young man felt it too. For the first time, something went across that smooth bright surface. Not fear. Interest. The interest of a cat that has put its paw on something and found it hot.

"Well," said Andric, and laughed, and slipped the knife into his coat. "Well. Your game, then. I'll keep the thousand. Consider it a fee for my discretion." He bowed, a beautiful bow, there in the gutter. "Good night, my lord Count. Do give my regards to my sister."

He went back the way he had come, over the wall, light as a cat, and was gone.

Edric stood in the alley for a long time. *My sister.* He did not know, then, what the young man meant by it, or whether he meant anything. He only knew that above him the window was closing, softly, and a candle was being lit behind it, because the girl inside was afraid of the dark. And that she would go to sleep tonight and never know that anyone had stood in the alley at all.

The next morning, Bastien bought the house next door.

### Later

The trial of the man called Andric for the murder of Cade Rusk was held in the Great Court of the Hall of Justice, and the High Justicar of Valemont prosecuted it himself. And Andric rose in the dock, in his chains, and said that his name was Andric, and that he had been born at Larkmere twenty-three years ago and buried under the chestnut tree in a little oak box by a young, ambitious man with a great deal to lose. And turned his head, slowly, gracefully, and said, *Good morning, Father.*

That much was the same. Edric had known it would be.

What was not the same came after, while the court was still frozen, and Valtor stood six paces from the dock with the ice cracking in him.

"And since we're telling family secrets," said the prisoner pleasantly, "I should mention my stepmother. Is that the word? My father's wife. Madame Valtor. She's there, I think, in the second gallery, in the blue." He lifted his chained hands a little, and pointed, and the whole court turned. "In the autumn, Madame Valtor paid me a thousand marks to cut my sister's throat under her bedroom window and leave the knife in the gutter for her young man to be hanged with. She promised another thousand on completion." He smiled at the gallery. "I took the money. I didn't do the work. Somebody stopped me. I won't say who. But I thought my father ought to know, since he's so very fond of order."

Heloise Valtor stood up in the second gallery, with her hand at her throat, and said, in her soft voice, "That's a lie. That's a monstrous lie." And then she looked down into the well of the court, at her husband, and saw his face, and stopped speaking.

Valtor was looking up at the gallery. At his wife. And then along the row beside her, to the girl in grey at the end of it, very straight, with her grandfather's grave dark eyes. Lyse. Alive. Whom he had never once asked why she ate from the common pot.

He took off his gold chain, and laid it on the clerk's table, and said, "It's true." He did not say which part. He did not need to.

They took Heloise Valtor out of the gallery between two Guards, in front of all Solace, and a fat, fair, solemn little boy of nine ran down the gallery stairs after her shouting *Mother, Mother*, until a Guard caught him round the waist. Edric was nearer. He went down and knelt on the marble in front of the boy and held him by the shoulders while he screamed, and did not let go, and the boy kicked him and wept and called him names, and was alive.

He was alive. Edric held on to that. That night, and for a long time after. The grey house had lost a mistress and a master, and would never be whole again; but nobody in it lay on the stillroom floor with a wooden horse in his open hand.

And at the very end, at the bottom of the world, with the god's whole weight in him and the gold chains burning, the Deep asked him the question it had been saving.

*You could have given her the word, little one. In the little room with the jars. You wanted to. I felt you want to. Why did you stop?*

*Because she asked,* said Edric, *and I could hear what she was asking for.* He felt the pressure in him ease, a little, like a tide on the turn. *Because I was tired of watching what people do.*

## Tell her, then sabotage her supply

*Heart + · Tide 0*

"There's a thing the Sardhi call *blackwater*," he said.

He told her. All of it, as it is told: the urchin in the deep reefs where the blight is worst, the clear liquid with no taste and no smell, a few drops every day for a month, the lungs filling with water, slowly, the way the blight-cough does. No way to tell the difference. No cure, except one: a man who takes a very small dose every day for years, as the old Sardhi kings did.

He watched it go into her. He felt the phial stop turning in her hand, and the want in her go still, and then gather, the way a cat gathers before it jumps.

"How very interesting," said Heloise Valtor, and set the phial down on the shelf among the others. "You must tell me more, another time."

He went home and sat in the study in the dark and did not light a lamp.

And there was a voice in him that began to say: *You've done nothing. She asked, and you answered. What she does with it is hers.* He heard it start, cold and reasonable, the way he had heard it start a hundred times since the Keep. He listened to it for perhaps a minute.

Then he said, aloud, to the dark room, "No."

He had wanted to see what she would do. That was the truth, and he made himself look at it, the way Faro had taught him to look at a thing: the whole of it, every current. He had looked at a woman he knew to be a murderer in everything but deed, and he had handed her the means, because he hated her husband and wanted to watch his house burn. And if the girl at the end of the table died of it, that would be his doing, as surely as if he poured it himself.

*Very well,* he thought. *Then I'll see what she does. And I'll be there when she does it.*

---

Blackwater was rare. That was what he had told her, and it was true. It did not grow in a stillroom. It had to come from the south, from the Sardhi coast, from a man who knew a man who dived the deep reefs. In Solace, there might be three dealers who could get it. Bastien knew two of them by name before midnight, and the third by noon the next day.

They were too slow. Heloise was quicker.

On the fourth night, he felt it arrive.

He was sitting in the study with the window open to the sea, not reaching, only breathing, and he felt it across the whole length of the Street of Lanterns the way you feel a cold draught from a door left open in a warm house. Grey, and cold, and wrong: the sea's own sickness, distilled into a few ounces of clear liquid in a glass bottle, sitting somewhere at the top of the grey house at the far end of the street. He knew it the way he knew the blight in the child at Grey Point. It was the same thing.

He stood up and put on a black coat.

"Shall I come?" said Bastien, in the doorway.

"No. Watch the street." He thought. "If I'm not back in two hours, go to the front door with a lantern and make a great deal of noise about a fire in the next street."

---

The High Justicar's house had a high grey wall, and a lantern over the gate that gave less light than the others, and a garden at the back that ran down to the alley. It had an old vine on its rear face that had not been cut back in years, and a drainpipe of grey lead that ran from the gutter to the ground beside the stillroom window on the top floor.

He went over the wall from the alley, and up the vine, and the drainpipe, with the Still Water on him so heavily that he could feel it in his teeth. A watchman on the corner of the alley looked straight at him once, at a dark shape halfway up the back of the High Justicar's house, and saw what a watchman expects to see at the back of a great house at one in the morning: ivy, and shadow, and nothing. He looked away.

The stillroom window was latched. Edric laid his palm on the glass and felt the damp in the old wood of the frame, and drew it, very gently, until the wood shrank a hair's breadth, and the latch slid in its keep, and the window swung inward on the smell of alcohol and bitterness and something sweet underneath.

He climbed in.

The room was dark. The copper stills gleamed faintly in the light from the window. The glass jars stood in their rows on the shelves, dozens of them, and the amber phials, and the bunches of dried herbs hanging from the beams like bats. He stood still among them and breathed, and reached.

There. On the third shelf, at the back, among a dozen other phials exactly like it, in exactly the place she had put the amber one down when she said *how very interesting*. Cold. Grey. Wrong.

He took it down and held it in his hand, and laid the other hand over the stopper, and drew.

It came up through the glass into his palm the way the blight had come out of Nell's lungs: slowly, clinging, unwilling, cold as the bottom of the sea. It went up his arm and into him, and he tasted salt and rot, and saw for a moment, behind his eyes, a reef in very deep water, grey and dead, and things moving on it with too many spines. He held on. He drew until there was nothing left in the phial but clear water, with no taste and no smell, exactly as before. Then he put it back on the shelf, at the back, among the others, in exactly the place it had been.

He stood in the dark stillroom and swallowed the sickness down, and waited for his stomach to stop heaving, and felt for more.

There was more.

Not here. Below. On the floor beneath, at the front of the house. A second cold place, smaller, sharper. She had divided it. Of course she had. She was careful. Half in the stillroom, where anyone might find a bottle among a hundred bottles. And half somewhere close to her, where no one would ever look.

---

The corridor on the second floor was long and dark and smelled of beeswax. He went down it in his stockinged feet, with the Still Water on him, past a closed door, and another.

The third door opened as he passed it.

A small figure stood in the doorway in a white nightshirt, with fair hair standing up on end and a carved wooden horse clutched to its chest. Pip Valtor. Nine years old, fat and fair and solemn, blinking in the dark, looking straight up at him.

The Still Water should have held. The boy should have seen a dark corridor and nothing in it. But children do not expect things the way grown people do; they have not learned yet what is supposed to be there. And Pip Valtor looked up at the tall man in black standing in his mother's corridor at half past one in the morning, and his eyes went very round, and he did not scream.

"You're the sea-monster man," he whispered. "From dinner."

Edric crouched down, slowly, until his face was level with the boy's.

"Yes," he whispered back.

"Did you really kill one? With your bare hands?"

"Yes."

Pip considered this, gravely. Then he said, as he had said at the dinner table, "Show me."

Edric looked at him for a moment: the round pale face, his mother's pale eyes, the wooden horse. Then he looked past him into the little bedroom, at the cup of water on the table by the bed.

He moved one finger.

The water rose out of the cup. Not much: a handful. It rose up into the dark air of the bedroom and gathered itself, and turned, and became a fish. A little silver fish of water, no longer than a finger, with fins, and a tail, and a mouth that opened and shut. It swam once round the boy's head, slowly, glinting in the faint light from the window, and Pip turned to follow it with his mouth open and his eyes enormous. Then it swam back to the cup, and dived, and was water again.

"*Oh,*" said Pip Valtor.

"That's a secret," said Edric. "Between sea-monster men. You mustn't tell anyone. Not even your mother."

Pip nodded hard.

Edric put his hand on the boy's forehead, very lightly, the way you put your hand into a pool, and found the stillness, and held it over him: not deep, not the long sleep, only a little, only enough. The boy's eyes grew heavy. Edric caught him as he swayed, and picked him up, wooden horse and all, and carried him to his bed, and pulled the blanket over him. He was very warm, and much heavier than he looked, and he was smiling in his sleep.

Edric stood by the bed for a moment longer than he should have.

---

Heloise Valtor slept alone, in a pretty room at the front of the house, with the curtains drawn. The High Justicar slept in his study, mostly, when he slept at all.

The cold place was on her dressing table: a little crystal scent bottle with a silver stopper, among a dozen others, and a jewel case, and a hand mirror. He crossed the carpet to it without a sound and took it up and drew, as he had drawn upstairs, and the grey cold came up through the crystal into his hand and up his arm and into him, and he set his teeth against it.

Behind him, the bed creaked.

He went still. Every drop of him.

"Corvan?" said Heloise Valtor, from the bed, thick with sleep.

He did not turn. He held the Still Water. He held it the way you hold a breath under the sea, and he felt her look at him: at a tall, thin, dark figure standing at her dressing table in the dark. He felt her see what she expected to see. Her husband, who sometimes came to the door at night, she did not know why, and stood there, and went away again without speaking.

"Go to bed, Corvan," she murmured. "For pity's sake." And turned over, and slept.

He drew until the bottle was clean. He set it down among the others, in the place where it had been. And he went out of her room, and along the corridor, past the door where a boy was asleep with a wooden horse, and up the stairs, and out of the stillroom window into the night.

Bastien was at the corner of the alley with a lantern under his coat, an hour and three-quarters after he had gone. He looked at Edric's face in the dark and did not ask anything. He took his arm, and walked him home, and held his head in the courtyard, by the black marble pool, while he was sick into the drain: grey seawater, and something else, black and bitter, that smelled of a dead reef.

### Later

The old Marquis of Aubray came up from the north for the season with the old Marquise, and drank his granddaughter's health at dinner every night for a month, and went home again in the spring. Old Noll drank his master's wine on the tray every morning when he thought no one was looking. Lyse Valtor ate at her father's table and lifted her glass and drank. And nothing happened to any of them, because every few days a dark figure went up the vine at the back of the grey house in the small hours, and every few days the water in a crystal bottle and an amber phial was only water.

Heloise grew thin. Edric watched it at dinners: the small pretty plump woman, a little less plump each week, a little less pretty, her pale bright eyes going from face to face at her husband's table, from the old Marquis to her stepdaughter, waiting, and waiting. She went back to the dealer on the waterfront in the second month, in a veil, and paid him three times his price for a fresh bottle. The dealer was Bastien's by then. He sold her the finest blackwater on the south coast, and it was water.

The man called Andric stood in the dock in the Great Court and said *Good morning, Father*, as he was always going to, and the High Justicar took off his chain and walked out. But he did not run. There was nothing at home to run to. No phials, no ultimatum, no note in a neat round hand. He walked back along the great terrace very slowly, bareheaded, and went into his study, and shut the door, and sat there for three days, and the house went on around him.

Heloise left the city at the end of that week, for her family in the country, before the rumours could reach her door. Edric was standing at his own gate when the carriage went by down the Street of Lanterns, piled with boxes. A small pale face was pressed to the back window, fat and fair and solemn, with a wooden horse held up against the glass.

The boy saw him. His eyes went round. And then, very carefully, so that his mother would not see, Pip Valtor put one finger to his lips, and grinned, and drew a little fish in the mist on the glass with his fingertip.

Edric stood at the gate with his hand on the cold iron long after the carriage had gone.
