# Event 22: Elsabet's Escape

*Branches from Chapter 22, "The House of Valtor": in the weeks after Larkmere, when Lord Andric Cavell is "a great success" in society and "Baron Quell had begun to drop hints" about his daughter Elsabet.*

*The canon path, ignoring her, continues in [Chapter 25](../../25-the-trial.md), where she flees alone on the night of the betrothal.*

## Help her: fund her journey south

*Heart + · Tide 0*

She played the harp at her father's musicale in the first week of autumn, in the great hall of the white marble house on the terrace, and she played it as if she were trying to break it.

Edric had not expected that. He had expected a lady's accomplishment: something pretty and correct, to be applauded and forgotten. Instead a tall, thin, sharp-faced girl of nineteen in grey silk sat down at an instrument taller than herself and played a southern piece he had last heard in a harbour tavern in Port Jasmine, fast and fierce and full of hard bright edges, with her mouth set and her eyes on nothing, and the strings rang off the marble like hail. When she finished there was a moment of startled silence before the applause. Baron Quell, at the front, smiled his small smile and clapped a little too long. Lord Andric Cavell, beside him, clapped beautifully, and said something that made the ladies near him laugh.

Elsabet Quell did not look at either of them. She stood, and curtsied to the room as if to an enemy, and went out through the long windows onto the terrace.

A quarter of an hour later, when Edric stepped out onto the terrace himself for the air, she was waiting for him.

"My lord Count." She did not curtsy this time. She stood by the balustrade with her arms folded and the city spread out below her in the dusk, and looked at him with her mother's handsome cold grey eyes in a face that was all her own. "May I speak to you? I'll be quick. My father thinks I'm in the retiring room with a headache."

"Mistress Quell."

"You brought him here," said Elsabet. "Lord Andric Cavell. Everyone knows it. He's your protégé. You found him somewhere in the south, and you dressed him and you introduced him, and now my father wants to marry me to him for a fortune that's held in trust in Port Jasmine." Her voice was quite steady, and very low, and fast, as if she had rehearsed it on the strings. "I don't want him. I don't want anyone. I've told my father so three times and he tells me not to be a fool. He needs the money. I don't know why; he won't say. But he needs it the way a drowning man needs a spar, and he'll sign me over to your Lord Andric at noon on the day the notary names, whatever I say." She took a breath. "So I'm asking you. Since you made him. Unmake him. Or help me go."

Edric looked at her. He felt the water in her, as he felt it in everyone: and it was not what he had expected either. Not a spoilt girl's temper. Something hard and clear and cold, like a mountain stream: fear, a great deal of it, held very still, and under the fear, a want so straight and simple it almost hurt to touch. *South. The music schools. The great halls. To play, and be nobody's.*

"Why do you think I'd help you?" he said.

"I don't," said Elsabet. "I think you're probably as bad as he is. I think you're doing something to my father; I don't know what. He hasn't slept since you came. But you're the only person in Solace who might be able to." She looked out at the city. "And because there's something wrong with him. Lord Andric. You must know it. When he looks at me there's nothing behind it. Like looking into a window at night and seeing only the room you're standing in." She turned her head. "You must have seen it, my lord. You chose him."

*Yes,* Edric thought. *I chose him. For you. For this.*

It was all laid. It had been laid since the spring. The betrothal at noon in the white marble hall, with all of Solace watching. The quill in the gold ink. The doors opening, the captain of the Guard, the paper, the soldiers' boots ringing on marble, exactly as they had rung on the floor of the Three Anchors at the Feast of Lamps nineteen years ago. Dorran Quell standing at the end of his own great hall, with his whole fortune hanging on a marriage, and watching it turn to ash in front of everyone he had ever wanted to impress. He had dreamed it. He had wanted Dorran to feel the floor go out from under him in public, at a celebration, with the wine poured and the flowers on the tables. He had wanted it very much.

And the girl was the bride. He had known that. He had thought of her, when he thought of her at all, as a piece of scenery. A white dress at the marble table.

"What do you need?" he said.

Elsabet stared at him. She had not expected it, he saw. She had come out onto the terrace braced for a refusal, the way Lyse Valtor braced at the dinner table, and she did not know what to do with her face.

"Passage," she said, after a moment. "To Port Jasmine. For two. My friend Louisa will come; she plays the viol, and she's wanted to go for years. Enough to live on for a year, until we can find places. And a letter. To the Conservatory there. They don't take girls without a letter from someone they've heard of." She lifted her chin. "Your bank is in Port Jasmine. Thornbury and Wilde. They'll have heard of you."

"They have," said Edric.

---

It was done in four days. He did not give her gold; gold could be found in a girl's room, and traced. He gave her a letter of credit on Thornbury and Wilde in another name, enough for two years, not one, and a letter to the master of the Conservatory of Port Jasmine, written in his own hand and sealed with the silver wave, which said nothing at all about who she was, only that the bearer was the finest harpist the Count of Cristal had heard north of the Sardh, and that he would consider it a personal kindness. He gave her two berths on a Cristal ship lying at Harnmouth, under a captain who asked no questions. He gave her Bastien, on the night, to take her and Louisa and the harp in a closed carriage down the coast road in the dark.

Bastien did not like it.

"You've thrown away the betrothal," he said, standing in the study with his arms folded and his black eyes burning. "The whole stage. Five years of it. The Guard coming in at noon, with all Solace watching. That was the point." He was quiet a moment. "Now we'll have to take the boy in some back alley, when the time comes. Quell will lose his marriage in his own drawing room, with no one to see. And he'll know someone helped her. He'll know it within a week."

"Yes."

"And the boy will know." Bastien's voice went very flat. "Andric watches everything. His bride runs off a fortnight before the signing, with a letter of credit on your bank? He'll start to wonder whose game he's in. He'll start to wonder about you."

"I know."

Bastien looked at him for a long time. Then he unfolded his arms.

"She's nineteen," he said. It was not quite a question.

"She's nineteen," said Edric. "And she plays the harp as if she's trying to break out of it." He looked down at the desk. "I've had people make me a piece of their game, Bastien. I know what it costs. I'll take Dorran some other way."

---

She left on a night of thin rain, with her harp in its case on the carriage roof and a small bag and her friend Louisa, a quiet, freckled girl clutching a viol as if it were a child. At the gate of the house with the blue door, where Bastien had stopped the carriage to let the Count say goodbye, she put her head out of the window and looked at him for a long moment in the lamplight.

"I still don't know why you've done this," she said.

"Neither do I," said Edric. "Play well."

"I'll play better than well." She hesitated. "If my father. Whatever you're doing to him. He's not." She stopped, and started again, and he heard her mother's coldness in her voice, and under it, something that was not cold at all. "He was kind to me, when I was small. He taught me to count. With the coppers in his purse, at the kitchen table. He was always better with numbers than people." She pulled her hood forward. "That's all. Goodbye, my lord."

The carriage went away down the Street of Lanterns into the rain.

Dorran Quell found the note on her pillow in the morning. It said: *I will not be sold. Not even to a real lord. Goodbye, Father.* He came to the house with the blue door at noon, grey-faced, shaking, and sat in the hall of green glass and asked the Count of Cristal whether he had heard anything, anything at all; whether the Count's agents in Port Jasmine might look out for her; whether Lord Andric would still, perhaps, in time, when she had come to her senses. Edric listened to him with his still face and said all the right things.

That evening, at the theatre, Lord Andric Cavell came to the door of the Count's box between the acts, as he sometimes did, to pay his respects. He was very charming about his lost bride. He said he had always known she did not care for him and he wished her every happiness. And as he bowed to leave, he looked at the Count of Cristal with his laughing dark eyes, a long, curious, considering look, like a cardplayer who has just seen his partner discard a card he had expected him to keep.

"How very generous of someone," he said, "to pay for her passage." And smiled, and went.

### Later

Her letter came on the morning after the trial, while the city was still steaming from the storm. It had come up from Port Jasmine on one of his own ships, and it was addressed in a hand he did not know, very upright and quick, with the sharp hooks of someone who writes music.

*My lord. I am at the Conservatory. The master read your letter and frowned and made me play for an hour and then gave me a place, and Louisa too. I am writing because my father has written to me. He says he is coming south, soon, with money, and that we will have a house together in a city in the far south where no one knows our name, and I need never marry anyone. He says not to tell a soul. He says he will not come by sea, because the harbours will be watched, but by the north road, through the hills and the passes, and round by the border. He says it as if it were a present.*

*I know what the money is. I am not a fool, whatever he thinks. I knew before I left. I do not want it, or the house, or him, not like that.*

*Do what you must. But do not let them hurt him. Please. He taught me to count.*

*E. Q.*

Edric read it twice, standing at the window of the study, with the cuts on his hands still bandaged from the stillroom and the old man's word still in his head. *Enough.*

He had laid the trap in the Greyhills in the spring: a letter to Vasco Rook, and a price for bread written in his own hand. He had smiled, writing it. He had sent a rider north that morning with new orders, and the rider would take three days, if his horse held.

He looked at the girl's letter for a long time. Then he called Bastien, and told him to have a horse saddled in the stable, day and night, and a man watching the north gate, from now until the Lord Treasurer left the city.

He came up the ravine to the ruined monastery on the fourth day after Dorran was taken, not the eighth: before the last of the money was gone, before the boy with the squint stopped bringing bread. He found the Lord Treasurer of Valemont sitting on the straw in the cave with his saddlebags at his feet and his hands shaking, thinner, frightened, entirely sane. And he put Elsabet's letter into those shaking hands before he said a word, and watched a man who had always been better with numbers than with people read it, and read it again, and put his face down on it, and weep.

## Use her as leverage against Dorran

*Heart − · Tide 0*

She played the harp at her father's musicale in the first week of autumn, as if she were trying to break it, and afterwards she waited for him on the terrace in the dusk, with her arms folded and the city spread out below.

"You brought him here," said Elsabet Quell. "Lord Andric Cavell. Since you made him, unmake him. Or help me go."

He listened to all of it. The father who needed money the way a drowning man needs a spar, and would not say why. The suitor with nothing behind his eyes. The music schools of Port Jasmine, and the letter the Conservatory would need, and the friend who played the viol. He felt the water in her as she talked: fear, held very still, and under it a want so clean and straight it was like a blade.

And he felt, the whole time, at the edge of everything she said, something else. Something she did not know she was carrying.

"Your father won't say why he needs the money," he said, when she had finished.

"No."

"But you know."

Elsabet went still. It was very slight; a girl raised in that house would have learned early to be still. But he felt it in her blood, the small cold jump of it.

"I don't know what you mean."

"You're his daughter," said Edric. "You live in his house. You're clever; you play as if you're counting. I think you've lain awake and heard him get up at five every morning, and light a candle, and turn pages. I think you know where he keeps the book he turns them in." He leaned on the balustrade beside her and looked out at the city, so that she would not have to look at his face. "I can get you to Port Jasmine. Passage, money, a letter to the Conservatory that will open its doors. All of it. And I'll ask one thing in return."

She did not answer for a long time. Below them, the lamps were coming on along the terraces, one by one.

"You want me to steal from my father," she said at last.

"I want you to copy something. Nothing will be taken. He'll never know it was touched." He let the words lie a moment. "Three nights. Then you're on a ship."

"And what will you do with it?"

He told her the truth, because the truth was the best lie he had. "Make him stop," he said. "Make him stop doing what he's doing. You know what it is, Mistress Quell. I think you've known for a long time."

She looked at him. Her mother's cold grey eyes, in her own sharp face, and behind them the stream running fast and clear and frightened. He could feel her weighing it. The ship. The halls of Port Jasmine. Her father at the kitchen table, teaching a small girl to count with the coppers in his purse.

"Three nights," said Elsabet Quell.

---

She brought it to him on the fourth morning, in a music case, folded between the pages of a book of southern dances: forty sheets of close-written music paper, in a quick, upright hand with the sharp hooks of someone who writes notes, not numbers. She had copied it by moonlight, she said, in her father's bedroom, while he slept in his counting-house, which he did most nights now. She had copied it the way she learned a piece: a page at a time, by heart, and written it out in her own room before dawn.

He read it in the study, with the door locked. It was all there. Every column. Eleven years of it, in Dorran Quell's own figures, the real ones. The Sardh war loans. The borrowing from the Treasury. The Blight Fund, year by year, and the hole in it, growing like a tumour. The grain ships. The southern Empress. The bonds. The sums, at the foot of every page, which did not balance, and never had.

It was the rope to hang a Lord Treasurer. Edric sat and looked at it for a long time, and felt the cold come up in him, and the taste of salt, and something else under it, sweet.

He kept his word to her. He was careful to; it mattered to him, afterwards, that he had. A letter of credit on Thornbury and Wilde, in another name, enough for two years. A letter to the Conservatory under the silver wave. Two berths on a Cristal ship at Harnmouth. Bastien, and a closed carriage, on a night of thin rain. She did not say goodbye to him at the gate. She sat in the back of the carriage with her friend Louisa, and the harp on the roof, and did not look out of the window as it went by.

He went to see her father the next morning.

---

Dorran Quell received him in the counting-house on the Street of Gold, in the inner office, with the door shut. He looked very bad. His daughter had been gone a day. He had not slept. He had not, Edric thought, eaten.

"My lord Count," he said. "Forgive me. My daughter. A family matter. If it's another draft—"

Edric laid a sheet of music paper on the desk in front of him.

Dorran looked at it. At the columns, in the quick upright hand with the sharp hooks. At the figures he knew better than his own face. He looked at it for a long time, and the colour went out of him by degrees, the way a tide goes out over mud.

"That's Elsabet's hand," he said. His voice was a whisper.

"Yes," said the Count of Cristal. "There are thirty-nine more. And she's quite safe, before you ask. She's on one of my ships, going south. My captain has orders to treat her like a princess." He let that lie a moment, and watched Dorran Quell hear what had not been said: that a ship can be ordered to go anywhere, and a captain can be given other orders. He had no intention of giving them. He would have cut off his hand first. But Dorran did not know that, and Edric found, examining himself, that he did not hurry to tell him.

"What do you want?" said Dorran.

"Two million marks," said Edric. "By the end of the week. In gold. On my credit without limit, which your house accepted, and which your house must honour, on demand." He folded his hands. "Your reserves are gone. Your deposits are thin. You'll have to take it from the only place left. You know where that is."

"That will finish it," said Dorran. "The Fund. There'll be nothing left. When they audit—"

"Yes."

"And if I refuse?"

Edric looked at the sheet of music paper on the desk, and then at the man behind it, and said nothing at all.

Dorran Quell picked up a pen. His hand was shaking so badly that the nib spattered. He wrote the order, and signed it, and sanded it, and pushed it across the desk without looking up.

"She did this for you," he said, very low. "My girl. My own girl. Why?"

"Because she wanted to be free," said Edric, "and I was the only one who'd pay for it." He picked up the order and the sheet of music paper. At the door, he turned. "It's a common enough bargain, Dorran. You made one once yourself. At a corner table, in a tavern in Saltmere."

He shut the door on the man's face. And going down the stairs of the counting-house into the bright street, with the order in his pocket, he found that he was smiling again, the small cold satisfied smile, and that he did not stop it.

### Later

The house of Quell fell three weeks early, before the trial, before Lord Andric Cavell was ever arrested at any betrothal, because there was no betrothal: the bride was gone and the Fund was empty, and when the Ministry's auditors opened the Treasury's ledgers in the second week of autumn, on an anonymous tip that came with forty sheets of copied music paper, the hole was the whole of it. Dorran Quell fled north that same night, with what he could carry, into the Greyhills, where a price for bread was waiting for him.

When Edric came to the cave, in the end, the Lord Treasurer was lying on the straw with his face to the wall, and he did not turn round.

"I know who you are," said Dorran, before Edric had said a word. "Rook's boy told me the Count was coming. I've had days to work it out. I always was good at sums." His voice was a thread. "I don't care. Whatever I did to you, whoever you are. I don't care. You took my girl. You took my girl and you made her copy out my sins in her own hand, by moonlight, in my own room, and then you laid them on my desk." He turned his head at last, and looked up, and his sunken eyes were not frightened any more. Only very tired, and very sure. "Whatever I did to you, I never did that. I never once used a child."

Edric had no answer.

The last letter he ever had from Elsabet Quell came months later, after everything, to an island in the west. It was very short. *I play in the great hall at Port Jasmine now,* it said. *They say I am the finest harpist on the south coast. I have never once played without thinking of what bought my passage. I thought you should know that you were paid in full.* There was no signature. There did not need to be.
