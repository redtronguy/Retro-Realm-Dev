## Heal him

*Heart ++ · Tide +*

When he lifted his head from the old man's knee, the sun had moved across the floor, and the sound from the garden had not stopped.

He went down.

The garden behind the grey house was small and walled and sodden after the storm: a square of rank grass, a few dead rose-bushes, a stone bench, and a wall so high that the sun came into it only at noon. The High Justicar of Valemont was on his knees in the middle of it, in his black robe, in the mud. He had laid his son on the bench, wrapped in the robe's outer mantle, with the small fair head showing at one end and the little carved horse placed carefully on the boy's chest. And he was digging.

He had no spade. He was digging with his hands. He had made four holes already, in a ragged line across the grass, none of them deeper than his forearm, and abandoned each one, and begun another. His nails were torn and his hands were black to the wrist. He was talking as he dug, not loudly, in his clear, even, precise voice, every word placed like a stone.

"Not here. Not here. It was under a tree. There was a tree. I remember the roots. I'll find it. They're here somewhere. I put them somewhere safe. I only have to remember where."

Edric stood at the foot of the garden steps and looked at him.

*Leave him,* said something in him, cold and reasonable. *You've done enough. Severin said so. Let him dig. Let him dig for twenty years. It's no more than he's earned.*

And then, from somewhere further down, an older voice, thin and cross, the voice from the other side of a wall: *And what did you learn in the Keep, you fool, if not that a man in the dark is still a man?*

He thought of Lyse, in the dark, holding his hand. *My grandfather says a man is not the worst thing he has ever done.*

He thought, too, without wanting to, of what a madman's ravings were worth in a court of law. Nothing. The High Justicar had confessed to burying a child; very well. He had confessed it in a moment of shock, before his peers, and gone mad within the hour. In a month the Chamber would call it grief. In a year, slander. In ten years, a rumour. And the letter in the fire, and the Sun Warrant, and the hundreds of other boys sent down into the dark with no trial and no name, would go into the ground with the holes in this garden, and be filled in.

He went across the wet grass and knelt down in the mud beside Corvan Valtor.

Valtor did not look at him. "Help me," he said. "Please. It was under a tree."

"I know," said Edric. "I know it was."

He put his hand on the man's chest, over the heart, flat, the way he had put it on Nell Fennick's chest at Grey Point. And he breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. And reached.

---

It was not like the blight. He had thought it might be.

A mind is water too. *On the Healing of the Waters* had said so, in a chapter he had read once and not gone back to: *the waters of the mind are the deepest in the body, and the most easily troubled, and a madness is a whirlpool in them, that turns and turns about a single place it cannot bear to look upon.* He had not understood it then. He understood it now. He felt Valtor's mind the way he would have felt a stretch of sea from a cliff: and it was turning. All of it. A great slow grinding whirlpool, round and round and round, about a dark place at its centre where the water went down and did not come back. The madness was the turning. The turning was what kept him from falling in.

*Draw it out,* the Warden of the House of Cristal had written, *gently, as thou wouldst draw a splinter. But know that what thou drawest must go somewhere. And it will go into thee.*

He drew.

It came up through his hand like cold water up a pipe, and it was not cold water. It was a boy at a window in a house with a blue door, watching the ships come in, and a big furious voice behind him shouting about the King and the sea and cowardice, and the boy's hands gripping the sill. It was a fire in a study in Saltmere, and a letter in the fire, and the flame reaching the fingertips and not letting go, *not letting go*, because the pain was nothing to the fear. It was a face across a desk: a sailor of nineteen, sunburnt, honest, trusting, and so easy to read, so easy, like a page in large print. Edric saw his own face from the other side of the desk, and it was the worst thing he had ever seen. It was a garden at night, and a woman screaming in an upstairs room, and a small cold thing in a box that was not cold enough, not quite, and a spade, and roots. It was a little boy at a dinner table saying *show me*. It was the stillroom. It was the boy on the floor with a wooden horse in his hand.

It was thirty years of being afraid.

It went into him. All of it. He felt it go into his chest and spread along the Tidemark, cold, cold, colder than the blight, and the salt came up into his mouth so strong that he gagged, and the sea, which had gone down in him that morning in the stillroom, came up again to meet it, eager, glad, and he felt the Mark climb his cheek, over the bone, to the corner of his eye, and into the brow, like frost going across a window.

*Yes,* said the Deep, very far down, and he did not know whether it was pleased with him or with Valtor's grief, and he did not stop to ask.

He drew until the whirlpool slowed. Until it stopped turning. Until there was only the dark place at the centre, and the water lying still around it, deep and black and quiet, with nothing between it and the man any more.

Then he took his hand away.

---

Valtor knelt in the mud and looked at his hands.

He looked at them for a long time: at the torn nails, the black earth, the blood. Then he lifted his head and looked at the holes in the grass, one by one. Then at the stone bench, and the small shape on it, in the black mantle, with the wooden horse on its chest.

Edric watched it come into his face. Not the ice; the ice was gone, and would never come back. Not the madness. Only knowledge. Plain and whole and unbearable. The face of a man of forty-seven who knows exactly where he is, and what he has done, and that his son is dead.

He did not make the sound again. He did not weep. He got up, slowly, like a very old man, and went to the bench and knelt beside it, and put his hand on the boy's fair hair, and stayed there.

"Why?" he said at last, without turning round. His voice was clear, and quite steady, and quite empty. "I was somewhere else. It was better there. Why did you bring me back?"

Edric got to his feet. He swayed, and caught himself on the wall. He was shaking with cold, and his mouth was full of salt, and behind his eyes, if he let them close, a fire in a study in Saltmere was still burning.

"Because your father told me *enough*," he said. "And because Lyse once told me that a man is not the worst thing he has ever done." He leaned on the wall. "And because a madman can't stand up in the Great Court and say what he did. All of it. Every name. I need you to say it, Corvan. Not for me. For the ones who never got a Count of Cristal."

Valtor was silent for a long time, with his hand on his son's hair.

"Yes," he said. "Tomorrow."

---

He said it the next morning, in the Great Court of the Hall of Justice, where the trial of the prisoner Andric stood adjourned. He came in on foot, unshaven, in a plain grey coat, without his chain, and asked the old judge with the face like a walnut for leave to make a statement before the court, as a private man. And it was given, because nobody knew how to refuse him.

He spoke for three hours.

Edric listened from the gallery. He would remember it for the rest of his life: the clear, even, precise voice, every word placed like a stone, laying out thirty years of a life the way a clerk lays out a ledger. Larkmere, and the box, and the chestnut tree. A woman's name, which he spoke once and did not repeat. A physician's certificate signed under threat. A daughter he had let be poisoned, rather than have his wife tried. And then, in the second hour, the Sun Warrants.

He had signed four hundred and twelve of them, he said, in thirty years. Every one without trial. He gave the names. He gave them from memory, in order, with the dates, while the court clerks wrote until their hands cramped, and in the galleries people began, one by one, to stand up, as if for a hymn, when they heard a name they knew.

The forty-first name was *Edric Vane, master of the merchant ship* Gull's Promise, *of Saltmere, aged nineteen, for carrying a letter to my father.* Valtor did not look up at the gallery when he said it. He said only, "I am told he died in the Keep," and went on to the forty-second, and Edric, in the second row, understood that it was the last lie Corvan Valtor would ever tell, and that he had told it for him.

When he had finished, he asked for no mercy, and the court committed him to the cells under the Hall to await the King's pleasure.

---

Brother Faro went down to him that night. Prisoners of rank were allowed a priest. Nobody looked twice at a soft, grey, forgettable travelling friar with a brass lantern on a cord round his neck.

Valtor was sitting on the edge of the cot in a stone cell lit by one candle, writing. A list. He looked up when the door opened, and looked at the priest for a long time, and Edric felt him see through it, the way Mira had. There was nothing left in Valtor to expect anything.

"Edric," he said. "Have you come to watch?"

Edric sat down on the stool across from him.

"Lyse is alive," he said.

He watched it go into the man. He had watched a great many things go into a great many people that year. This was the only one he would ever be glad to remember.

Valtor did not speak for a long time. The pen had fallen out of his fingers. When at last he did speak, it was to ask how, and Edric told him, very low: the wall, and the bread, and the Stilling, and a cutter on the morning tide. Forty days. He did not say where.

"Don't tell me where," said Valtor. "I might be asked." He looked down at the half-written list on his knee. Then he picked up the pen again, and turned the paper over, and wrote a single line on the back of it, and folded it, and held it out. "Give her this. When she wakes. If you think she ought to have it."

Edric took it.

They found the former High Justicar of Valemont at dawn, sitting on the edge of his cot with his back against the stone, quite peaceful, with the candle burnt down beside him and the list finished on his knee. Four hundred and twelve names. The physicians could find no cause. The Sardhi one, who had been sent for, said that sometimes, when a man has been holding something up for a very long time, and then puts it down, the heart simply stops.

Edric, at the window of the study in the house with the blue door, with the Tidemark cold over his eye, thought that it was probably true, and that he had done it, and that it might, for once, have been a mercy.

Three days later, for the first time in three hundred years, the Chamber of Lords voted against an instrument of the Throne. By a majority of eleven, they suspended the Sun Warrant until the four hundred and twelve names could be inquired into, and named commissioners to go to the Drowned Keep and read its rolls. The King let it pass. The *Courant* said he had been heard to laugh.

### Later

The Valtor tomb stood in the gardens of the Temple of the Noon, under the Sunspire, where the light no longer burned. Lyse went to it in the spring after the Teeth fell, with Maxim, on a bright cold morning, with a sprig of lavender in her hand.

She had lain in it once. She did not like to think about that, and Maxim did not ask her to. She stood in front of the bronze door, where her own name had been cut into the marble the autumn before and then, at her request, cut out again, and below it, newer, a name that had been added without ceremony, because the Chamber had not known what ceremony a man like that deserved. *Corvan Valtor. He told the truth, at the last.*

She had the paper in her pocket. Edric had given it to her on Cristal, on the second day after she woke, when she was strong enough to sit up and ask where her father was. It said, in a clear, even, precise hand: *I was afraid all my life. It is not an excuse. It is only the truth, and I owe you that, at least.*

That morning, in the Chamber of Lords below the empty tower, the council that had no king was sitting for the first time under a new law. The clerks had already begun calling it the Valtor Rule, though it had another name in the statute book: that no man or woman of Valemont should be held one day in any prison without a charge read in open court. It was the first law the council had passed. Some of the old members of the Ebb, sitting on the benches where they had not sat for thirty years, had wept when the vote was read.

Lyse laid the lavender on the step, and knelt, and put her hand flat on the cold bronze the way you put your hand on a door you mean to come back to.

"I'll come again," she said. "Next spring."

And she did, every spring, for the rest of her life.

## End it

When he lifted his head from the old man's knee, the sun had moved across the floor, and the sound from the garden had not stopped.

He went down.

The garden behind the grey house was small and walled and sodden after the storm, and the High Justicar of Valemont was on his knees in the middle of it, in his black robe, in the mud, digging with his hands. He had laid the boy on the stone bench, wrapped in the robe's outer mantle, with the little carved horse on his chest. He had made four holes in the grass already, none of them deeper than his forearm, and left each one, and begun another. He was talking as he dug, in his clear, even, precise voice.

"Not here. It was under a tree. There was a tree. I'll find them. I put them somewhere safe."

Edric stood at the foot of the garden steps and looked at him.

He thought of the years. He could see them, the way he could see a current: a keeper, hired by somebody; a grey house that nobody visited; this garden, in all weathers, a hole here and a hole there, filled in and dug again, for twenty years, for thirty. A man who had sent four hundred boys into the dark without a trial, kneeling in the mud for the rest of his life, looking for the two he had put there himself. It was justice, of a kind. It was more exact than any justice Edric could have devised. It was the sea's justice: slow, and blind, and cold, and perfectly fitted.

And he could not bear it.

He told himself that afterwards, and it was true. He could not bear the sound, or the holes, or the boy on the bench with the horse on his chest. He could not bear to go back to the house with the blue door and sit at the window and know that across the street, at the other end of the Street of Lanterns, behind a high grey wall, this was going on, and would go on, every day, for as long as he lived in Solace and longer. He had brought it here. He had told Heloise Valtor the name of a poison in a room full of glass jars, because he wanted to see what she would do. And this was what she had done.

*Enough,* the old man upstairs had said.

He thought: *Then let it be enough. Let it be over. For both of us.*

That was true, too. It was not all of it. There was something else, under it, and he knew it was there, and he did not look at it. Something cold that remembered a study in Saltmere, and a letter held in a fire until the flame reached the fingertips, and wanted, very simply, for this man to stop.

He went across the wet grass and knelt down in the mud beside Corvan Valtor.

---

Valtor did not look at him. "Help me," he said. "It was under a tree."

"I know."

"They'll climb out. If I don't find them first. They always climb out." He dug. His fingers were bleeding. "I put them in the dark to keep everything in order. That's all I ever wanted. Order. You understand. Someone has to keep it." He stopped, with his hands in the hole, and for a moment something moved behind his eyes, very far off, like a face at a window in a house that is otherwise empty. "Is it you? The tide? Have you come in?"

"Yes," said Edric.

"I thought you would," said Valtor, and went back to digging. "Eventually."

Edric laid his hand flat on the man's back, between the shoulder blades, over the heart.

He did not need the books for this. He had felt it in the stillroom, an hour ago, with his hand on the forehead of a dead child: the real stillness, the one there is no waking from. He had felt it in the alley under the fig tree, running out of Cade Rusk between his fingers. He knew its shape now. And he had felt, in a dark room at the end of this street, how a heart can be slowed, and slowed, and held. *Not by force. By example.*

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. He found the stillness in himself, which was not hard, that afternoon; there was very little else left. And he laid it over Corvan Valtor the way you lay a blanket over a sleeper, and did not hold anything back from it, and did not keep a thread.

Valtor's hands slowed in the mud. His breathing slowed. His heart, under Edric's palm, beat, and beat, and grew quiet, like a man walking away down a long corridor.

He turned his head, at the very end, and looked at Edric. His eyes were clear. Edric would never know whether it was the madness lifting, at the last, or only the stillness; whether the man saw him, or saw a boy across a desk, or saw nothing at all.

"In order," said Valtor softly. "Good."

And then he was still, kneeling in the wet grass with his hands in the earth, and he did not move again.

---

Edric laid him down on his side beside the hole, with his head on his arm, the way the boy had lain in the stillroom with his head on his mother's arm. He took the black mantle from the bench and put it over both of them, father and son, and stood up, and found his knees would hardly hold him.

He did not feel the sea rise. He had been afraid that he would, and it did not. He felt nothing at all, the way he had felt nothing at the window the evening Fenrick died. Only cold, a long way down.

The Sardhi physician came at dusk, called by a maid who had gone out into the garden with a lamp and screamed. He knelt in the mud and looked at the High Justicar for a long time, and found no wound, and no poison, and no cause, and wrote *apoplexy, following a great shock*, because it was true, and because nobody in Solace would ever ask him for anything else. The *Courant* said the next morning that the former High Justicar had died in his garden of a broken heart, a few hours after the deaths of his wife and son, and that whatever his crimes, it was perhaps a mercy.

Edric went back up the stairs of the grey house before he left that evening, to the room at the top where the old man sat by the window.

Severin Valtor's eyes were on the door. They had been on it, Edric thought, since the screaming in the garden. They found his face.

He picked up the board and held it up. He did not say anything. He did not have to. The old man had heard everything; he always heard everything.

The dark eyes held his for a long time. Edric ran his finger along the first row of letters, slowly, the way Noll used to, and waited.

The old man did not blink.

He ran it along the second row, and the third, and all the way to the end. Nothing. He began again. Halfway along the first row, Severin Valtor closed his eyes, and did not open them again, and after a long while Edric understood that he was not going to, not while Edric was in the room. He put the board down on the table, and the rug back over the old man's knees, and went out.

### Later

Lyse asked on the second day after she woke, when she could sit up in the bed in the library at Cristal with Maxim holding her hand, and the colour beginning to come back into her face.

"My father," she said. "Is he alive?"

Edric told her. He had promised himself, on the *Faro*, that he would not lie to her: not about the stillroom, not about the blackwater, not about this. He told her about the garden, and the holes in the grass, and the sound, and his hand on her father's back. He told her what he had told himself afterwards. He told her, too, about the other thing, the cold thing under it that remembered a letter in a fire. He did not spare himself. It did not feel like honesty. It felt like handing her a knife.

Lyse listened without moving. When he had finished, she was quiet for a long time.

"I don't know if you were wrong," she said at last. "Grandfather would have said it was a mercy. Maybe it was." She looked at the window, at the sea. "I used to think, when I was small, that one day he'd be sorry. That he'd come to me and say so, and I'd forgive him, and it would be like taking off a heavy coat. I used to plan what I'd say." Her voice did not change. "You took that, Edric. Not from him. From me."

She went to Solace with Maxim, the spring after the Teeth fell. They saw Maxim's father, and walked on the Pale Cliffs, and went to the Temple of the Noon, where the light on the Sunspire no longer burned, and Maxim stopped at the gate of the gardens where the old families had their tombs.

"Shall we?" he said gently.

Lyse looked through the gate for a long time, at the paths among the marble, and the bronze door she could just see at the end of one of them, with her own name cut out of the stone above it, and her father's name cut in.

"No," she said. "There's nothing there for me to forgive. Someone saw to that."

She never went. Not that spring, nor any after. And when her children, years later, asked her about their grandfather the High Justicar, she told them the truth, all of it, in a clear, even voice; and then she told them that he had died in his garden, of a broken heart, a few hours after his son; and then she changed the subject, and they learned not to ask again.
