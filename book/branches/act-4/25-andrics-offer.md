# Event 25: Andric's Offer

*Branches from Chapter 25, "The Trial": the night after Cade Rusk dies in the alley, when Bastien stands looking at "a debt that has finally come due, twenty-three years late, and in the wrong coin", and before the betrothal is signed.*

## The visitor at the window

He came the next night, by the same way.

Edric heard the fig tree creak a little after one, as it had creaked the night before under a heavier man. He heard a light drop onto the gravel, so light it was hardly a sound at all, and the study window, which nobody had latched again, being eased open. He was sitting where he had sat for Cade: in the chair by the cold fireplace, in the dark. He had not put on the priest's face. He had not put on any face. He had not been to bed.

A slim figure came over the sill, quick and graceful, and stood on the floor of the study, and did not fumble for any lantern. It simply looked round the dark room, as if it could see perfectly well, and found the chair, and smiled.

"Good evening, my lord Count," said Andric. "I hope you don't mind. The front door seemed so formal, after everything."

On the other side of the study door, on the stairs, Edric felt Bastien stand up, very quietly, with his knife in his hand. He lifted one finger from the arm of the chair, and Bastien, who could not see it, stopped anyway, and did not open the door.

"Sit down, Andric."

"Thank you." The young man sat on the edge of the desk, swinging one foot, like a boy visiting an uncle. He was wearing the midnight-blue coat he was to be betrothed in, and there was no blood on it anywhere. "I've come to make you an offer. But I thought I'd better show you my cards first, so you'll know I'm worth talking to." He smiled. "Cade talked, you know. On the oar. He talked all night, every night, for two years; it was the only thing that kept him from screaming. About a tavern in Saltmere called the Three Anchors. About a pen that went from one hand to the other, and a letter, and a sailor called Edric Vane who was taken away from his own wedding feast and put in the Drowned Keep. About a priest who came to his inn, years after, and gave him a diamond and told him the sailor was dead." He tilted his head. "And last night, in the alley, before I. Well. He was very upset. He said the priest was in your house. He said the house was haunted. He said the priest had the dead sailor's face." Andric spread his hands. "I'm not clever about many things, my lord. But I'm very clever about faces."

Edric said nothing.

"And then there's the box," said Andric. "At Larkmere. Under the tree. You knew exactly where to dig. You knew exactly who'd faint. And you watched my father the whole time, not the lady on the ground." He swung his foot. "And Cade. You let him go out the window. You knew I was in the alley. You could have kept him till morning. You didn't." His smile widened. "I liked that. I didn't think you had it in you."

"What do you want?"

"The Guard will come for me," said Andric. "Won't they? Soon. There was a man in the alley. Somebody's paid him to remember my face; it was probably you. And Cade said my name, before he died; you were there." He did not seem troubled. "I expect it's all arranged. I expect it'll be at the betrothal. You'd like that. You have a taste for occasions." He leaned forward. "So here is my offer. I could run tonight. I'm very good at running. And you'd never see me again, and all you'd have against my father is a box and a bit of old linen and the word of a steward with a scar on his throat. Or."

"Or."

"Or I stay. I let them take me, beautifully, at noon, in front of everyone. I let them try me. And when they ask me in court who my father is, I tell them." His dark eyes were laughing. "Oh, I'll tell them. I've been saving it for years. I'll tell it better than anyone ever told anything in that court. I'll ruin him so thoroughly there won't be a man in Solace who can look at him without seeing a little oak box. You'll have your High Justicar on his knees in the Great Court, in public, by his own son's hand. Isn't that what you want?" He sat back. "And afterwards, when it's done, and they've condemned me, which they will, you get me out. Not the galleys. Not the rope. Out. A door, a horse, a ship. You can do that. I've seen what you can do." He paused. "And I never say your name. To anyone. Ever. Not in court, not after. Edric Vane stays dead."

He let it lie there in the dark between them, like a card turned face up on the table.

"And if I say no?"

Andric smiled his sun-coming-out smile.

"Then I run tonight," he said. "And one day, somewhere, I find someone who'd pay very well for the name of the man who calls himself the Count of Cristal. I expect there are a great many such people. I expect some of them live in a very tall white tower."

## Accept

*Heart 0 · Tide 0*

Edric looked at him for a long time.

He felt the water in him, the way he felt it in everyone. And there was nothing there. There had been nothing there in the gambling den, and at Larkmere, and there was nothing now: no fear, no want, no hate. Not even greed, really. A smooth flat surface, reflecting back whatever was in front of it. And what was in front of it, Edric realised, was himself.

It was a good offer. That was the trouble. It was exactly the offer he would have made, in Andric's place. It gave him everything he had come to Solace for. Valtor, in the Great Court, in public, at his own son's hand. The thing he had dreamed in the dark under the sea: the High Justicar's face as the ice broke. All of it, delivered by a man who would do it beautifully and feel nothing at all. And all it cost was a door, afterwards. A horse. A ship. One more piece of the world's darkness let back out into the world.

He thought of Cade, in the alley, with the tired surprised look on his face. *I said something. Didn't I?*

"One condition," he said.

Andric raised his eyebrows.

"You don't kill anyone else," said Edric. "Not in prison. Not on the way out. Not after. Not ever, while I'm alive to hear of it."

Andric laughed, delighted, the way he had laughed in the gambling den when he was offered fifty thousand a year to be a lord.

"Oh, my lord," he said. "Of course. I promise." He slid off the desk and bowed, a beautiful bow, in his midnight-blue coat. "Whatever you like. I'd promise you the moon." He went to the window and put one leg over the sill. "Noon, then. Three days. I'll wear this coat; it suits me." And he was gone, down the fig tree and over the wall, light as a cat, without a sound.

Bastien came in a moment later, with the knife still in his hand, and looked at the open window, and at Edric in the chair.

"You let him go," he said.

"I made a bargain."

Bastien looked at him for a long time in the dark. Then he put the knife away.

"I dug him up," he said quietly. "Twenty-three years ago. I put my hands in the earth under that tree and pulled him out of the box, and he wasn't breathing, and I breathed into his mouth until he cried." He turned to go. "I've wondered every day since whether I should have left him there. I suppose now you'll wonder too."

---

It went exactly as the young man had promised. That was the worst of it.

At noon on the third day, in the great hall of the white marble house, the notary dipped the quill in the gold ink and held it out, and the doors opened, and the captain of the Guard came in with six men and a paper. And Lord Andric Cavell did not vault the table or throw the quill or go through the window in a shower of glass. He laid the quill down beside the contract, very gently. He bowed to his bride, who looked at him as if he were a slug on a lettuce. He held out his wrists to the captain, smiling, and walked out of the hall between the soldiers with his chin up, in his midnight-blue coat, like a prince going to a coronation. As he passed the Count of Cristal at the door, he did not look at him at all.

At the trial, he did everything he had said. He rose in the dock in his chains and said that his name was Andric, and that he had been born at Larkmere, and buried under the chestnut tree in a little oak box by a young man with a great deal to lose. He turned his head, slowly, gracefully, the way a dancer turns, and said, *Good morning, Father*. And Edric, in the gallery, felt the ice in Corvan Valtor crack from the edges in, exactly as he had dreamed it, and tasted nothing at all.

What came after was the same, too. The High Justicar running bareheaded through the streets. The stillroom at the top of the grey house. The little carved horse. The storm. The cracked mirror. The old man's eyes, and the board, and *E-N-O-U-G-H*.

The man called Andric was condemned to hang on the Sunspire steps at noon, five days after the trial. Edric spent four of those days in the dark in the study at the back of the house with the blue door, with his hands bandaged and the old man's word in his head, and the thread of Lyse Valtor's life lying very faint beside his heart. On the fifth night, he got up and put on a black coat.

"You're going to do it," said Bastien, at the foot of the stair. "After that. After the boy."

"I gave my word," said Edric. "The sea hears." He looked at the steward's face in the lamplight. "I've broken enough things this month, Bastien. I'm not going to break that too."

Bastien did not come with him.

---

The cells under the Hall of Justice were cut into the living rock of the cliff below the Sunspire, and at noon they were the worst place in Solace for a man with the sea in him. At three in the morning, with the tide high at the foot of the cliff and the sun on the other side of the world, they were only stone.

He walked in through the gate wearing the face of the High Justicar's deputy. He had seen the man at the trial: a stooped, grey, tired lawyer with ink on his fingers, who had signed Andric's warrant. He put the man's face on with the Still Water, like a coat. The guard at the gate saw exactly what he expected to see at that hour, a deputy come with some last formality for a condemned man, and grumbled, and let him through. The guard at the stair saw the same. The gaoler at the bottom, who had a jug of wine on his table and very little interest in anything, did not see anything at all, because Edric laid two fingers on his wrist as he passed, and found the stillness, and held it over him, and the man put his head down on his arms beside the jug and slept.

Andric was sitting on the stone shelf in his cell, in the plain grey shirt, with his knees drawn up and his chains in his lap, wide awake. He looked up as the key turned. He looked at the deputy's tired grey face, and smiled, the sun coming out in the dark.

"My lord Count," he said. "You came. I did wonder, after the business with my little brother. I heard about that. I'm so sorry." He did not sound sorry. He did not sound anything. "I thought you might decide I was a debt you'd rather not pay."

"I pay my debts," said Edric, and unlocked the chains.

He walked him out the way he had come in: up the stair, past the guard who saw two deputies, through the gate, out into the cold dark of the great terrace under the Sunspire. There was a horse at the corner of the terrace, saddled, and a purse on it, and a letter of passage to a ship that would sail on the morning tide for a port in the far south where the King's writ did not run.

Andric swung up into the saddle as if he had been born there. He gathered the reins. Then he leaned down, and looked at the Count of Cristal, whose own face had come back now, in the dark, scarred and grey and very tired.

"Do you know," said Andric, "I think you're the only person who's ever kept a promise to me. Isn't that strange?" He considered it, with interest. "I don't know what I'm supposed to feel about it."

"You're not supposed to feel anything," said Edric. "You're supposed to keep yours."

"Oh, that," said Andric, and laughed, and put his heels to the horse, and was gone down the dark terrace towards the harbour road, and the sound of the hooves went away into the night, and did not come back.

### Later

The first letter came to Cristal in the spring, after everything, after the siege and the Teeth and the chains, a week before he meant to sail west. It came on an ordinary merchant ship from the south, addressed in a beautiful flowing hand to *the Warden, Isle of Cristal*, and Hayda brought it to him on the terrace with a face like a closed door.

*My dear Count,* it said. *Or Warden. Or whatever you are now. They tell such stories about you down here. I hope they're true; they're very good.*

*I am well. The south is charming. I have a new name, a new house, a new friend who owns a great many ships and does not ask what I did before. I have kept my promise to you. I have not killed anyone. It has been very dull.*

*But I find that I think about promises a good deal, now that I have kept one. I think about how long they last. I think, for instance, that you made me promise not to kill anyone while you were alive to hear of it. And I think about how very much less of you there is, these days, by all accounts. How the blue lines have gone. How the sea does not answer you the way it did.*

*Do take care of yourself. I should hate for anything to happen to you. It would release me from so much.*

*Your friend,*

*A.*

Edric read it twice, standing on the terrace in the spring wind, with the sea below him clear and blue and quiet, and the Tidemark gone from his face. Then he gave it to Bastien, who read it, and handed it back without a word.

There was a fire, that summer, in a merchant's house in a port in the far south. The merchant and his household got out. Most of them. The local magistrate said it was a lamp knocked over in the night, and nobody could prove otherwise, and the merchant's handsome young secretary was very helpful, and very charming, and had been asleep on the other side of the house the whole time.

Edric read about it in a southern news-sheet, months late, and sat for a long time looking at the sea.

## Kill him quietly

Edric looked at him for a long time.

He felt the water in him. And there was nothing there. A smooth flat surface, reflecting back whatever was in front of it: Edric's own face, in the dark, and the thing behind it.

He thought of the woman who had raised this boy, who had taken a baby out of a box in a stranger's garden and fed it and loved it and told it the truth when it was fifteen. *I burned her house down a week later.* He thought of Cade in the alley, with three holes in his belly and his hands pressed over them, and the tired surprised look. He thought of a name, Edric Vane, in this mouth, in the mouth of a man who could say anything to anyone and feel nothing, carried up a white tower to a golden King. Of Hayda, and Jac, and Bastien, and the people of Cristal, and the girl lying asleep in the library with her hands folded on her breast, all of them hanging on whether this smiling creature decided, one day, on a whim, to be bored.

And he thought, colder than all of it, so cold he hardly knew it was a thought: *There's nothing there. There's nothing in him to hurt.*

"No," he said.

Andric's smile did not change. He shrugged, gracefully, and slid off the desk. "Then I'll run," he said. "It's been lovely, my lord. Truly. I'll think of you." He went to the window and put one hand on the sill.

Edric reached.

Not for the sea. For the water in the young man. It was the easiest thing he had ever done. Andric was made of water, like everyone; he had blood in him, and breath, and the wet of his lungs and his eyes and his mouth. And it was all there, smooth and empty and unguarded, because there was no one inside it to guard it. Edric found the water in his lungs, and the water in the air he was breathing, and the water in the vase of flowers on the desk, and the water in the fountain in the courtyard below. And he drew them together.

Andric stopped, with his hand on the sill.

He turned round. He put his other hand to his chest, as Cade had in the alley, puzzled, the way a man touches a pocket he thought had something in it. He opened his mouth to speak and nothing came, only a small wet sound, like water moving in the bilge of a boat that is slowly settling. He looked at the man in the chair by the cold fireplace.

And there, at last, for the first time, something went across the smooth bright surface of him. Edric felt it. A current. Thin and quick and astonished, the first true current he had ever felt in the young man: not fear, quite. Surprise. The look of a clever boy who has finally found a trick he cannot work out.

"Oh," said Andric. Very softly, as if it were interesting.

He sat down on the floor under the window, with his back against the wall, the way Cade had sat down against the wall in the alley. He did not struggle. Perhaps he did not know how. He sat there, looking at Edric with his laughing dark eyes, with the surprise still on his face, while the water rose in him, quietly, the way the tide rises over sand, the way the blight-cough takes the children. And then he was only sitting there.

It had taken less than a minute. There was no blood. There was nothing at all to see, except a young man in a midnight-blue coat sitting under a window with his eyes open and a little water at the corner of his mouth, as though he had drowned in a dream.

Edric sat in the chair and looked at him. He was very cold. He could taste salt. The Tidemark on his chest burned like ice, and he felt it creep, a little, a finger's breadth, up the side of his throat, as if the sea had leaned in to see.

He waited for something to come. Horror, or guilt, or the honey-sweetness that had frightened him in this room over the bowl, watching Dorran. He waited for a long time.

Nothing came. It had been like putting out a lamp in an empty room.

---

The study door opened.

Bastien stood in the doorway with the knife in his hand. He had heard nothing; there had been nothing to hear. That was what had brought him, in the end. The silence. He looked at the young man under the window. He looked for a long time.

Then he crossed the room and knelt down on the floor in front of the body, and put out his hand, and touched the dark curls on its forehead, once, very gently, the way you touch a sleeping child.

"I dug him up," he said.

Edric said nothing.

"Twenty-three years ago." Bastien's voice was quite flat. "I'd gone to Larkmere to kill his father, and I watched his father bury him instead, and when his father had gone, I put down my knife and dug with my hands. He wasn't breathing. I breathed into his mouth until he cried." He did not look round. "I've hated what he became every day since. I've wondered every day since if I should have left him in the box. I stood in that alley outside the gambling den in the spring and couldn't go in, because I couldn't look at him." He took his hand away. "But I never once thought you'd be the one to put him back in."

"He knew my name, Bastien. He'd have sold it. He killed Cade. He'd have killed again."

"Yes," said Bastien. "All of that's true." He stood up. "You'd have stood him up in the Great Court and let him tell them about the box, and he'd have hanged, or got away, and either way the whole of Solace would have seen what Corvan Valtor did under that tree. I'd have stood up there beside him, if you'd asked. I'd have told them what I saw. I've waited twenty-three years to tell them." He looked down at the young man under the window. "Now there's no one to tell it. There's just you and me, and a boy in a blue coat, and the sea at the bottom of the cliff."

He carried him down, in the end. Edric could not have stopped him. He wrapped the young man in a sailcloth from the cellar and carried him in his arms, down the narrow stair cut in the cliff below the house with the blue door, to the rocks where the sea came in, and he would not let Edric touch him. Edric followed with a lantern. At the bottom, Bastien stood for a long time with the bundle in his arms and the black water sucking at the rocks round his boots. Then he gave it to the sea, the way the Keep gave its dead, and the tide took it out, and it did not come back.

At noon three days later, the captain of the Guard came through the doors of the white marble house with six men and a paper, and found the betrothal hall full of flowers and wine and gold leaf and a weeping Baron, and no bridegroom. Lord Andric Cavell had disappeared. His rooms off the Street of Gold were empty, his bed not slept in, his trunks unpacked. He was never found. People said he had run, knowing the Guard was coming. People said a great many things.

### Later

Bastien left Solace a week later.

He did not make a scene of it. He came into the study one morning with the household accounts, as he always did, and laid them on the desk, squared and balanced to the mark, as they always were. And beside them, he laid his knife: the long, plain, worn steel he had carried for twenty-three years, since the night he went over a garden wall at Larkmere to kill a man and dug up a child instead.

"I'm going back to the Reach," he said. "Jac will find me a berth. Don't send for me."

"Bastien—"

"I'll not tell anyone what I saw. You needn't fear that. I owe you my life, and my brother's justice, nearly." He looked at the knife on the desk. "But I can't stand on the stairs outside your door any more and wonder who's in the room with you. I did that for Valtor, once. In a garden, in the dark. I'm too old to do it twice."

He went down the Street of Lanterns on foot with a single bag, and did not look back.

Corvan Valtor was still High Justicar of Valemont when the King's fleet sailed for Cristal. No son had stood up in the Great Court. No box had been named before the judges. Rumour came, and rumour went, and the High Justicar went on walking from his house to the Hall of Justice every morning at six and home every night at ten, thinner, greyer, the crease between his brows a little deeper; and if he sometimes stopped in the street and looked at nothing, as if he had heard a child crying, nobody dared to ask him why.

It was his hand that signed the Sun Warrant against the man who called himself the Count of Cristal: sorcerer, heretic, enemy of the Throne. Edric saw it, nailed to a post on the harbour front the morning he sailed, with Valtor's seal at the foot of it in red wax, every letter placed like a stone. The second Sun Warrant Corvan Valtor had signed with his name on it. The first had sent him to the Keep.

He stood in front of it for a long time, with the sea at his back. On the other side of the harbour, where there should have been a black-browed man with a scar across his throat, standing at the foot of the gangway with his arms folded and his eyes on the crowd, there was nobody at all.
