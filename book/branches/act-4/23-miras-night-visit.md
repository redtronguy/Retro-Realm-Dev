# Event 23: Mira's Night Visit

## Lie and let the duel happen

*Heart and Tide: see the two outcomes that follow.*

"Know what?" said Mira.

He looked at her, sitting on the floor of his study with her back against the door and her cloak around her, the way she used to sit on the landing stage in the Weir, waiting for the boats. He could feel the water in her: the long cold grief of nineteen years, and on top of it, raw and new, the shock of him. And under both, so deep she did not know it was there, eighteen years of a house with a fire in it, and a physician for her mother, and a son. A whole life, built on a bed of sand that she had never seen, because no one had ever shown her.

He could show her now. It would take a minute. The Three Anchors. The pen passing from the right hand to the left. The soldier on his knees in the sawdust after the others had gone. He could put it into her like a knife, and watch it go in, and she would go home and tell the boy, and the boy would not come to the Pale Cliffs, and he would live.

He would be buying his life with that. With her face, when she understood whose bed she had lain in for eighteen years.

*No,* he thought. *Not that. I've taken enough from her.*

And under that thought, so far down that he would not find it until much later, another one, colder, that did not want the boy to stay at home. That wanted to stand on the turf at dawn and look at Fenrick Morrow's son across twenty paces, and see what happened.

"Nothing," he said. "It doesn't matter who. Someone in Saltmere who wanted me gone. An anonymous letter. They're all the same, those letters. It was a long time ago."

Mira looked at him.

"Who?" she said again.

"I don't know," said Edric. "I never found out."

It was the first lie he had ever told her. He had not lied to her when they were children, not once, not even about the time he lost her father's net. He felt it leave his mouth and go across the dark room to her, and he felt her not believe it, and then, because she was tired, and because she had believed him all her life, decide to.

"You'd tell me," she said slowly. "If you knew."

"Yes," said Edric.

She closed her eyes.

---

They talked about Aren, afterwards. She begged him. She did not weep, because the Weir-folk did not, but she got up off the floor and stood in front of him and begged, in the low, hissing Weir voice she had trained out of herself for twenty years. *He's sixteen. He's never fired at a man. You'll kill him.* And he told her that he would not kill him. That he would stand on the turf, and let the boy fire, and not fire back. And she called him an idiot, and a coward, and a monster, and asked him what she was supposed to tell her son, and he said, *Tell him nothing. Let him have his father's honour. It's the last thing I'll leave him.*

She went out without another word. He heard her on the stair, and in the courtyard, and the gate. He stood at the window and watched the lantern of her hired chair go down the Street of Lanterns, and he did not tear up the will.

---

The Pale Cliffs at a quarter to dawn: a long headland of white chalk, turf cropped short by sheep, the sea on three sides and the white city on the fourth, and a crowd. Half of fashionable Solace, in carriages, in cloaks, with flasks of brandy against the cold, come to watch the son of the disgraced Lord Marshal fight the Count of Cristal.

Aren Morrow was already there, at the far end of the turf, with his back to the sea and his two friends behind him, in the plain black coat. The pistol case was open on the grass at his feet.

He had not slept. Edric could see it from across the headland: the white face, the red swollen eyes, the stiffness of a boy who has sat up all night holding himself together with both hands. He did not know. Nobody had told him anything. He had sat up all night in the Marshal's palace believing that his father had been destroyed by a foreign adventurer for reasons of greed or malice, and that it was his duty, as his father's son, to stand on a clifftop at dawn and put it right.

Bastien and the boy's seconds measured the ground. Twenty paces. They loaded the pistols in front of each other, as the custom was, and offered them, butt first. Edric took his without looking at it. It was cold and heavy in his hand, and he could feel the water in the oil on its lock, and the dew on its barrel.

"Gentlemen," said the elder of the boy's friends, in a voice that cracked. "At the drop of the handkerchief, you may fire at will. Each man one shot."

Edric walked to his mark and turned, and stood with the pistol hanging at his side.

The sky in the east was going from grey to pink. The sea below the cliffs was dark, and very quiet, and he could feel all of it, the whole vast slow breathing body of it, from the foot of the chalk to the horizon, waiting. If he called it, it would come. It would turn any ball from any pistol in the world.

He did not call it. He looked down the twenty paces of wet turf at Mira's son, and the boy looked back at him with Mira's eyes, and lifted his pistol, and his hand was shaking so badly that the barrel wandered across the sky.

And at the edge of the crowd, by the carriages, a woman in a dark cloak with the hood up had come out to the front, and stood with her hands pressed to her mouth.

The handkerchief dropped.

## Lie and let the duel happen: let Aren shoot you

*Heart +++ · Tide +*

He stood still.

It was harder than the Keep. He had not known that anything could be harder than the Keep. Every drop of water in him wanted to move. The sea under the cliffs wanted to move; he could feel it gathering, lifting, a great slow swell coming up the face of the chalk like a hand reaching for a ledge. *Let me,* it said. *Let me, little one. One breath. He will never know.*

*No.*

In for eight.

He thought of the will on the desk in the study, signed and sealed. Cristal to its council. The books to Tam. Money to Maxim Moren and Lyse Valtor, and the rest of everything to Hayda, with a paper saying once more that she was free. He thought of Hayda, behind the door of her room in the dark, praying in High Sardhi to the Thirst. He had not said goodbye to her. He had not known how.

He thought of Mira at the edge of the crowd with her hands over her mouth, and the lie he had told her, sitting there between them like a stone. *You'd tell me. If you knew.* He thought that if he died on this turf, she would never know why her son had shot a stranger at dawn, and that perhaps that was the only clean thing he had ever given her: a husband she could still bury without hating.

Out for eight.

The boy's hand steadied. Not much. Enough. Edric saw him set his jaw, the way Mira set hers before a squall, and close one eye, and aim, for the first time, properly.

Aren fired.

He heard it before he felt it: a flat hard crack, and a puff of white smoke torn sideways by the wind off the sea. Then something struck him high on the left side of the chest, under the collarbone, like a blow from a hammer, and turned him half round, and the turf came up and hit him in the face.

He lay on his side on the wet grass. There was no pain at first, only a great cold surprise, and the smell of crushed turf and powder smoke, and somewhere very far away a woman screaming a name that was not the Count of Cristal's. Then the pain came, all at once, enormous, white, and with it, through it, *up* through it, like a spring bursting out of rock, the sea.

He had not called it. It came anyway.

It came up out of the wound, out of the place under the collarbone where the ball had gone in, a hand's breadth above the root of the Tidemark over his heart, and it was not his. It was the Deep's. It had poured itself into him under the Teeth and made him its channel, and someone had put a hole in the channel, and the Deep did not like it. He felt it rush into him, through the hole, from every direction at once: from the sea below the cliffs, which heaved up the chalk in a great grey swell and broke over the edge of the headland in a sheet of spray that soaked the crowd at the back; from the dew on the turf, from the air, from the blood running out of him, which turned cold and thin and salt. He felt the Tidemark flare. He felt it race up his throat like fire up a fuse, across his jaw, his cheek, his brow, and burn there, blue, bright as a lamp, so that the people nearest him cried out and stumbled back. He felt it spread down his arm to his fingertips.

*They shot you,* said the Deep, vast and slow and astonished, in the water, in the wound, in him. *They shot you, little one. And you let them.*

*Yes.*

*Why?*

He did not answer. He had something to do first.

He rolled onto his back. He could not feel his left arm at all. But the pistol was still in his right hand, cold and heavy, and he lifted it, slowly, the whole weight of it, until it pointed at the pink sky over the Pale Cliffs, at nothing, at the gulls. And he fired.

The crack went out over the sea. The gulls rose shrieking from the cliff face. And then everything was very quiet, except for the swell breaking at the foot of the chalk, and the woman, who had stopped screaming, and was running.

---

Afterwards, people would say that they had seen the Count of Cristal's blood run blue. That it had come out of him onto the white turf of the Pale Cliffs like seawater, and soaked into the chalk, and that the grass there grew salt-white ever after and the sheep would not touch it. People in Solace said a great many things.

What Edric saw was the sky, and then Bastien's face, grey as ashes, bending over him with his hands pressed hard to the wound. And the surgeon. And then, between them, shoving the surgeon aside, a woman with her hood fallen back and her black hair coming down, on her knees on the wet grass, with her hands on his face.

"You idiot," said Mira. "You idiot. You *idiot*." She was weeping. The Weir-folk did not weep in front of anyone. She was weeping in front of half of Solace. "You let him. You stood there and let him."

"Told you," said Edric. It came out as a whisper. His mouth tasted of salt. "Told you I would."

And over her shoulder, standing three paces off on the turf with the empty pistol still in his hand and his face the colour of the chalk, he saw Aren Morrow. The boy was staring at him. At the blue fire on his face. At the blood, which was not quite the colour of blood. At his mother, the Countess Morrow, on her knees in front of all Solace, holding the Count of Cristal's face in her hands and calling him by a name the boy had never heard.

"Mother," said Aren. His voice was a child's. "Mother, who is he?"

Mira did not answer him. She did not look round. And Edric, lying on the turf with the sea pouring into him through the hole the boy had made, and the Deep asking *why, why, little one, why*, closed his eyes, and let the dark come up over him like a tide.

### Later

The ball had broken the collarbone and stopped against the shoulder blade, and the surgeon who dug it out said that any other man would have died of the bleeding on the turf. Edric did not tell him why he had not. He lay in the study at the back of the house with the blue door for a fortnight, with Hayda sitting by the bed in the day and Bastien by the door at night, and the Tidemark burning blue on his face from brow to jaw where it had never been before, and it did not go back down. When he could stand, he looked in the glass and saw it reaching for the corner of his left eye. The Deep had come in through the wound. Some of it never left.

Aren came on the twelfth day. He came alone, in his black coat, and stood at the foot of the bed, and Edric saw at once that he knew. Someone had told him. Hayda, perhaps; she would not say. Or his mother, after the turf, when she had finally understood what the lie in the study had been for.

"You knew," said Aren. "About my father. You knew all of it, the whole time, and you didn't tell her. You let me shoot you rather than tell her." His voice shook. "Why?"

"Because it would have broken her," said Edric. "And I'd broken enough."

The boy stood there for a long time. Then he did a thing Edric had not expected. He came round the side of the bed and knelt, awkwardly, the way young men kneel when they have been taught to do it in church and never meant it, and took the Count of Cristal's good hand, and pressed it to his forehead.

He was on the walls of Cristal on the Long Day, sixteen years old, with a borrowed sword and his mother's eyes, carrying water to the wounded along the burning terraces until his hands blistered. And Mira was there too, with the Weir-folk, in the citadel with the lamps. When the mirrors burned and the Tide rose in Edric like a flood and he could not find the bottom of himself, it was her voice he heard, from the citadel, singing the old song of the tide coming in. He found that he could follow it down.

## Lie and let the duel happen: shoot Aren

*Heart −−− · Tide 0*

The boy fired at once, too fast, without aiming, the way frightened people fire.

Something plucked at the side of Edric's head. A sting along the cheekbone, hot and sudden, like the lash of a wet rope, and a warm trickle down his jaw. The ball went past him and out over the sea, and somewhere behind him a gull screamed.

The smoke blew away on the wind. Twenty paces off, Aren Morrow stood with the empty pistol in his hand and his mouth open, and then shut it, and let the pistol fall to his side, and stood up very straight with his chin up and his heels together, the way he had been taught to stand. He had fired. He had missed. The custom of the Pale Cliffs was older than either of them, and it said that he must stand now and wait for the Count of Cristal's shot.

He was shaking. He could not stop shaking. But he did not run.

Edric lifted his pistol.

He meant to fire into the air. He had meant to all night. He had told Mira so. He lifted the pistol, and the weight of it was cold in his hand, and he looked down the twenty paces of turf to see where the sky was, so that he could put the ball into it.

And he saw the boy's face. Fenrick's face. Not the eyes; the eyes were Mira's. But the heavy brow, the jaw, the set of the mouth when he was afraid and would not show it. He had seen that face on a walkway in the Weir, in the dark, a lifetime ago, with a hand on a sword. He had seen it at the Feast of Lamps, by the door, white and wet and waiting. *Fenrick posted it.* Nineteen years of a house with a fire in it, and a woman in a green gown, and a son. Everything that had been meant to be his.

The blood was warm on his jaw. He could taste salt.

*He'll come again,* said a voice in him, very reasonable, very cold. *He'll grow up. He'll have his father's name, and his father's grievance, and your face to hate, and a sword. You know what sons do. You came to this city because of what sons do.*

*And Fenrick took everything,* said the voice. *Let him lose something. Let him lose one thing. Only one.*

It was not the Deep's voice. That was the thing he would have to live with. He listened for the Deep, afterwards, a thousand times, and he could never hear it in that moment. The sea was quiet below the cliffs. It had not moved. The voice was his own.

He lowered the pistol, a little. From the sky to the boy.

At the edge of the crowd, a woman in a dark cloak screamed his name. His real name.

He fired.

---

Aren Morrow sat down on the grass.

That was all he did, at first. He sat down, as if his legs had been taken out from under him, and put his hand to his side, under his coat, and looked at the hand, puzzled, as if it belonged to someone else. Then he lay back, slowly, on the wet turf, with his face to the pink sky, and the gulls going over.

Edric stood with the smoking pistol in his hand and did not move.

He heard it all as if from very far away. The crowd: a great indrawn breath, and then voices, rising. The boy's friends running. The surgeon running. Bastien, beside him, saying something in a voice Edric had never heard from him before, low and appalled, a single word in the Saltmere dock-tongue, over and over. And the woman. Mira came across the turf with her hood fallen back, past the seconds, past the surgeon, as if none of them were there, and went down on her knees beside her son and gathered him up against her, his head on her arm, the way you hold a sleeping child.

She did not look at Edric. Not then. She was looking at Aren, and talking to him, low, fast, in the hissing tongue of the Weir, which the boy had never learned; and Aren was looking up at her with Mira's own eyes, puzzled, frightened, sixteen, and trying to say something, and then not trying.

The surgeon knelt on the other side of him. He was there a long time. Then he took his hands away, and sat back on his heels, and shook his head, very slightly, and did not look up.

The sun came up over the eastern hills and shone straight into Edric's eyes.

Mira lifted her head.

She looked at him across the turf. And it was the same look as the dinner in the hall of green glass, the first night, across a hundred people: the look of someone who has heard a voice she knows is dead. Only now it was the other way round. She looked at Edric Vane, standing on the Pale Cliffs with the smoking pistol in his hand and her son's blood on the grass at her knees, and he watched her see him, properly, all the way down, for the first and last time. And then he watched her stop.

It was like a lamp going out in a window. She simply stopped seeing him. Her eyes were still on him, but there was nobody behind them looking. She bent her head again over her son, and drew the edge of her cloak up over his face, against the sun.

"You could have turned it," she said, without looking up. Very quietly. In the common tongue. "You could have turned the ball. You could have done anything you liked. You told me so."

He did not answer. There was no answer. He had not turned the ball. He had aimed it.

He dropped the pistol on the turf and walked back to the carriage, and Bastien did not follow him, and he rode back to Solace alone.

### Later

He went to Saltmere once more, before the end. He did not know why. He told himself it was on the way to the Teeth, which it was, nearly.

The Weir was as he remembered it, and not: the same stilt-houses, the same swaying walkways over the black marsh water, the same smell of mud and smoke and salt. But half the houses stood empty now, and the nets on the poles were grey and rotten, and the people he passed on the walkways stepped back against the rails to let him go by and did not look at his face. The Weir-folk had heard. The Weir-folk always heard.

She was at the end of the long walkway, where it ran out over the open water, where they had sat as children with the boat. She was sitting on the edge of the planks with her feet over the water and an old grey shawl around her shoulders, and she was looking out at the Teeth.

He stood on the walkway behind her for a long time. She knew he was there. She had known him by his step, once, across a hundred yards of swaying walkway in the dark. She did not turn round.

"Mira," he said.

She did not answer. She did not move. She sat with her back to him and the grey shawl around her and looked at the black rock on the horizon where he had spent fourteen years, and the wind off the marsh lifted the ends of her hair, and that was all. After a while, he understood that it would be all. That she would sit there, with her back to him, until he went away; and that if he came back in a year, or ten, or twenty, she would sit there in the same way, and not turn round.

He had Ysolde's wedding crown in his coat. He had brought it to give to her, though he could not have said what for. He took it out, and held it, and put it back.

At the chains, at the end, at the bottom of the world, with the god's whole weight in him and the gold light burning and the choice in front of him, he listened for a woman's voice singing the old song of the tide coming in. He had heard of it. A thing the Weir-folk did, for the drowned, to show them the way home.

There was no one singing. There was only the sea, and the Deep, and the voice that was his own.

## Still Water: make her forget

*Heart − · Tide +*

"Know what?" said Mira.

He looked at her, sitting on the floor against the door, and he understood two things at once, the way you see two ships come round a headland together.

The first was that she did not know. That she had lain beside Fenrick Morrow for eighteen years and borne his son and never known. And that if he told her now, he would watch it go into her like a blade, and she would never in her life be whole again.

The second was colder, and he did not like it, but it was true. She knew who he was. The only person in Solace, except Hayda and Bastien and a mute old man, who knew who the Count of Cristal was. She had known it at the first dinner. She was married to his enemy. She had a son who had struck him in public that evening. Whatever she felt for him, she was a door standing open in a house he had spent five years building, and tomorrow the Chamber would strip her husband of his command, and the next day, or the next, Fenrick would be in a corner with nothing left, and a frightened man in a corner will take anything his wife tells him and use it.

He could close the door. He knew how. He had read the chapter with *Do not* written in its margin in red ink.

*She'd be free of me,* he thought. *She's been grieving me for nineteen years. She'd be done with it. She'd never have to know any of it. Not Fenrick. Not the Keep. Not what I've become.*

It sounded like mercy. He made himself look at it until he could see what else it was.

He did it anyway.

"Mira," he said, and came away from the window, and knelt down on the floor in front of her, and took her face in his hands. "Look at me."

She looked at him. Her eyes were very close, dark green, the colour of deep water in shade.

"Edric," she said. "What are you doing?"

"Look at me," he said again, gently. "Just look."

---

He went down into the stillness, and reached.

She did not fight him. That was the worst of it, afterwards. A stranger would have clung to what was his. Mira did not. She looked up at him with her face in his hands and let him in, all the way, without a struggle, because it was him, and she had trusted him with everything since she was seven years old.

Her mind was full of water: deep, and dark, and very old, like the marsh under the Weir. He went down into it and found himself everywhere.

He found a boy of ten on a walkway, holding up a fish too big for him, laughing. He found a young man in a sailor's coat coming up the long walkway in the dusk, and her heart going up like a gull. A wedding crown. A feast of lamps. Soldiers at the door. And then nineteen years of grey: the end of the walkway, every spring tide, a woman sitting with a boat and looking at a light on a black rock, not knowing what it was. A lieutenant's coat. A grey dress, and not smiling. A house with a fire. A baby with her own eyes. And through all of it, every year, every day, like a thread through cloth, the boy who had died. Never quite gone. Never quite let go.

And then, a few weeks ago, bright and terrible and new: a hall of green glass, a tall man in black, and a face she knew the way she knew the sea. A light on a roof across the city, watched until dawn. A name said once, to a window, in the dark.

He held his stillness over it. Over all of it from the hall of green glass to this room. He pressed, the way the book said: not by force, by example, until the waters lay as quiet as his own.

It went. The hall. The light on the roof. The nights at the window. The stair, and this room, and his face in the dark, and her hands, and *you're not dead, are you*. It went still and dark and silent, like a stream freezing, and it took with it, because it was all one water, the thing that had been under it for nineteen years: the hope. Not the boy; he could not take the boy, the boy was too deep, he was in everything. But the small stubborn foolish secret hope that she had kept under the grief all that time, without admitting it to herself, that one day the door would open. He felt it go dark. He felt it freeze. And he felt her, very deep down, under the ice, give a small sigh, as if she had set something down that she had carried for a very long time.

He took his hands away.

Mira blinked. She was sitting on the floor of a strange room, in the dark, with her back against a door, and a man she did not know was kneeling in front of her. The Count of Cristal. Her son's rescuer. Her husband's ruin.

"My lord," she said. She put a hand to her head. "I'm so sorry. I. I don't know why I. I came to ask you." She stopped. "About my son. About the duel. I came to beg you not to kill him." She looked at him, and her face was a stranger's face, polite and frightened and very tired, and there was nothing in it at all that knew him. "Please. He's sixteen."

"I won't kill him, madam," said the Count of Cristal. "You have my word."

She thanked him. She thanked him three times, with her stranger's face, and apologised for the hour, and for intruding, and for sitting on his floor like a fishwife, she could not think what had come over her; and he helped her to her feet and walked her down the stair to the courtyard, where Bastien stood by the gate with a lantern and a face like stone, and put her into her hired chair. She did not look back at the house. Why would she? There was nothing in it of hers.

He went back up to the study and stood at the window. He could feel her going away down the Street of Lanterns, the water in her moving calm and slow, calmer than it had been since the night in the hall of green glass. He had done that. He had given her that.

He told himself so until the sky began to grey, and found that he could not make it sound like anything but what it was.

---

He did not stand to be shot, the next morning, on the Pale Cliffs. He had meant to, once. That had been a different man, the night before, with nothing left to give but that. Now he had nothing left to give at all, and it made things simpler.

When the handkerchief dropped and the boy fired, he moved one finger at his side, and the wind came up off the sea in a single gust that nobody else on the headland thought anything of, and the ball went a yard wide and out over the water. He fired into the air. Honour was satisfied. The crowd murmured, and got back into its carriages, disappointed. Aren Morrow walked off the turf alive and humiliated and white with rage, still believing every word his father had ever told him.

It had cost almost nothing. A gust of wind. It was astonishing, Edric thought, riding home, how little things cost, once you stopped minding who paid.

### Later

Fenrick fell on his sword anyway, two days later, alone in his study, with the house still full of his wife's jewels and his son's voice. He had come to the house with the blue door at noon, wild, with his sword half drawn, and Edric had come down into the courtyard and let the stillness go and said *the Three Anchors*, and watched it reach him. That part had not changed. Only now there was no one waiting at home who knew why.

The Countess Morrow went back to Saltmere after the funeral, with her son, to the Weir, where she had been born. She sold the palace on the terrace and everything in it, and gave most of the money to the fishing towns, and nobody in Solace could think why.

He saw her once more. In the last days, sailing south to the Teeth, he put in at Saltmere for an hour, he could not have said why, and walked out along the long walkway over the marsh in the evening. She was at the end of it, where it ran out over the open water, sitting with her feet over the edge and an old grey shawl around her shoulders, looking at the black rock on the horizon. Not waiting for anything. Only looking. She turned her head at his step, as anyone would.

The Still Water was on his face. He had not had to put it there. It was always there now.

She looked at him: a tall stranger in a black coat, with a foreign lilt in his voice, wishing her good evening. She looked at him for a long moment, quite kindly, and he waited for it, for the colour to leave her face, for her hand to go out to the rail.

"Good evening, sir," said Mira Solen, and turned back to the sea. "It's a fine night for it."

It was the mask working, at last. The one face in the world it had never fooled. She saw exactly what she expected to see: no one.

He had Ysolde's crown in his coat. He did not take it out. At the chains, at the end, there would be no one to sing him home, and he had known that since the night in the study, when he took her hope and felt her sigh as she set it down. He walked back along the walkway to the boat. She did not watch him go. There was nothing to watch.
