# Event 33: Olaf's Temple

*Branches from Chapter 28, "The Siege of Cristal": the hour past noon on the Long Day, when the flagship swings its great mirror towards the citadel "and found the third terrace in its way", and Edric, on the roof, sees the beam about to fall on Big Olaf's temple.*

## Save Olaf

*Heart 0 · Tide 0*

He had held the wall all morning.

It was the only thing he could do, in the sun, and it was very nearly more than he could do. From the roof of the citadel, at the broken ring of pillars where the dome had been, he could just reach the inner end of the harbour: the old Tidewarden sea-wall at the root of the hook, a long curve of black stone, older than anything else on the island, and behind it the boat-sheds and net-houses of the Weir-folk, and in them, the people who could not climb. The old ones. The sick from the hospital on the second terrace, carried down when the beams began falling on the terraces. Old Bettan and her daughters-in-law and a dozen babies. Everything the Weir-folk had left, now that their hundred stilt-houses had burned like candles in the shallows.

He could not stop the mirrors. He could not lift the ships, not in this light. But water drinks the sun, a little; that was the oldest thing the Tidewardens knew about sunfire. So since an hour past dawn he had been holding the sea up against the outside of that wall, in a long standing curtain of spray, thin and white and glittering, twenty feet high. When a beam struck it, the spray boiled, and roared, and flashed into steam, and the beam came through weakened, and the black stone glowed red and did not run. Twelve times it had happened. Twelve times the wall had held.

Each time, it was like taking a spike through the chest. The sun pressed down on him like a hand. The Tidemark burned cold on his throat. He had been standing there, holding the curtain, for six hours, and he did not know how much longer he could stand.

And then the flagship's mirror turned.

He saw it swing on its great brass swivel, the biggest of them all, slowly, the way a man turns his head, looking for the citadel. Looking for him. He saw it tilt, and stop, and find, between it and the citadel, a little whitewashed temple on the third terrace with a golden sun painted over its door.

He heard the singing.

*Light of the morning, light of the world.* Very loud, and very bad, and very deep. Olaf. Still there. Jac had told him to go. Tam had begged him. Hayda had argued with him in the doorway for ten minutes while the beams fell. And he had shaken his great fair head and polished the brass sun over the altar with his sleeve.

*Somebody has to stay and tell the Sun it isn't true.*

The mirror flared.

And Edric had time, the length of a breath, to understand exactly what he could do, and exactly what it would cost. He could not hold both. He did not have enough. If he let go of the harbour wall, he could throw the sea up in front of the temple instead, a single great heave of water, and the beam would strike that, and Olaf would live. And the curtain at the wall would fall. And the next beam, or the one after, would find the black stone with nothing in front of it.

There was no time to think about it. There was only time to choose.

He let go of the wall.

---

The curtain of spray at the harbour fell back into the sea with a long tired hiss, like a breath going out.

And he reached, with everything he had, across half a mile of burning island, down the face of the terraces to the sea at the foot of the third, and took hold of it, and *lifted*.

It came up out of the harbour in a single green wall, the way a whale comes up, all at once, enormous and shining. It rose up the face of the island, higher than any wave had a right to rise, and hung there, between the little temple and the white ship, for one heartbeat, a sheet of living water with the noon sun blazing through it.

The beam struck it.

The sound was like the end of the world. The water did not boil; it exploded. A cloud of white steam burst out across the third terrace, scalding, roaring, so thick that from the roof he could see nothing at all, only white, rolling up the face of the island towards him like smoke from a burning ship. He felt the sunfire tear through the wall of water and lose itself in it, and spread, and weaken, and break. He felt it strike the stone of the terrace below the temple and spatter like hot fat. He felt the heat. He felt the sea he had lifted fall back down the face of the island in a long hot grey rain.

He was on his knees on the roof. He did not remember kneeling.

The steam thinned.

The little whitewashed temple was still there. Its walls were scorched black on the seaward side. The golden sun over its door had blistered and run. Water was pouring off its roof and out of its doorway and down the steps of the third terrace in a hot smoking stream.

And out of the doorway, through the steam, came a voice. Very loud, and very bad, and very deep, a little hoarse, and slightly scalded, but not stopping.

*Light that is mercy, light that is true.*

Edric put his face in his hands.

---

It was Hayda who told him about the wall, afterwards, because nobody else would.

The flagship's next beam had gone to the harbour. Perhaps the mirror's master had seen the curtain fall; perhaps it was chance. It had struck the old Tidewarden sea-wall at the root of the hook full on, with nothing in front of it, and the black stone that had stood for a thousand years had glowed, and run, and opened like a door.

And the Radiant boats, which had been circling outside the harbour mouth all morning, waiting for exactly that, came in through it.

There were not many of them. A dozen boats, sixty knights. But there was nothing behind the wall to meet them except the people who could not climb. The Weir-folk men fought them on the shingle with boathooks and gutting knives and oars, and held them long enough for the women to carry the old and the sick and the babies up the first flight of the processional stair; and the knights burned the boat-sheds and the net-houses behind them, every one, with all the boats inside, and all the nets, a hundred years of knotting, and the little things the Weir-folk had saved from their stilt-houses that morning when the stilt-houses burned.

Five Weir-folk died on the shingle. One of them was Davy Bettan, Old Bettan's youngest son, who had carried his mother up the stair on his back and then gone down again for her lamp.

"She is not weeping," said Hayda. "The Weir-folk do not weep in front of their enemies. But you are not her enemy, and she is not weeping in front of you either." She looked at him, sitting on the floor of the library with his back against the wall and his head in his hands. "I am telling you so that you know. Not so that you can carry it. You will want to carry it. Do not. It is hers."

"I chose," said Edric. "I had a breath. I chose Olaf."

"Yes," said Hayda. "And if you had chosen the wall, you would be sitting here with your head in your hands telling me you chose the wall." She sat down beside him. "There was no choice that kept everyone, Edric. There was only the one you could bear to make."

He did not answer. After a while, she took his hand.

Down in the harbour, in the long red light of the sunset, the white ships' mirrors were turning to drink the last of the sun. And on the third terrace, outside a scorched little temple with a blistered sun over its door, a huge fair-haired man was sitting on the steps with his head bowed, not singing.

---

Olaf came to find him at midnight, when he was going down to the harbour.

He was standing at the foot of the processional stair, in the dark, in the place where the boat-sheds had been, among the smoking ruins. His face was red and blistered down one side from the steam. His great hands hung at his sides.

"Warden," he said.

Edric stopped.

"Hayda told me," said Big Olaf, in his slow deep voice. "About the wall. And Davy. She said you'd want to know I knew." He looked down at the black wet ruins round his feet. "I'd have stayed in there and been burned, if you'd let me. I was ready. I'd made my peace. I want you to know that too."

"I know," said Edric.

"I'm not saying it to make you sorry. I'm saying it because it's true." Olaf was quiet for a while. "Davy used to come up and listen to me, mornings. Sometimes. Sat on the step outside. Said it was the worst singing he'd ever heard, and he'd heard gulls fighting over a herring." He swallowed. "I don't know what to do with it, Warden. Being alive when he's not."

Edric looked at him for a long time, in the dark.

"Neither do I," he said. "I never have. But I think you do what I'm going to do now. You go on. You live. You find something worth being alive for, and you do it as well as you can, and you carry the ones who aren't." He turned towards the broken quay, and the black sea, and the twelve white ships with their riding lights burning half a mile out. "And you sing. Loudly. So he can hear it."

### Later

Old Bettan would not let them carry Davy up to the burying-ground on the hill. The Weir-folk did not bury their dead. They gave them to the sea.

So on the evening after the fleet surrendered, when the white ships lay on the Long Bank with their mirrors smashed and the High Radiant's sword lay on the shingle at the inner end of the harbour, the Weir-folk wrapped their five dead in sailcloth and laid them in a fishing boat, the only one left that would float, and pushed it out on the ebb with lamps at its bow and stern. And the whole of Cristal came down to watch, and stood on the burned quays in silence while the little boat drifted out towards the harbour mouth and the open sea.

Big Olaf stood at the back. He did not sing. It was not his song.

Cassia Vell stood beside him. She had come ashore that morning under a white flag, and had asked, before she asked for the Warden, for the man in the temple. She had been on the flagship's deck when the mirror fired. She had seen the sea rise up out of the harbour in a green wall to stand between a white ship and a man singing a hymn to the Sun, and take the Sun's fire into itself, and break it.

"The sea saved him," she said, to no one, watching the lamps drift out. "The sea. While he was singing to the Sun." She was very pale. "We teach the novices that the sea is a demon. That it drowns children."

"It drowned none today," said Olaf. "We did. Your lot and ours. Between us." He did not look at her. "I'll teach you the hymn, if you like. You'll want to sing it, I reckon. After."

Twelve days later, in the *Faro*'s longboat, in the dark of the moon, there were ten of them rowing towards the Teeth, not nine. Jac had told Olaf to stay. Tam had begged him. He had shaken his great fair head.

And when, in the temple under the Teeth, a woman from the Weir knelt at the edge of a pool of dead grey water and began, very softly, to sing a song older than the King, a second voice came in beneath hers on the second verse, very loud, and very bad, and very deep, in a language it did not know, getting every word wrong. It did not matter. The sea was not listening to the words.

## Spend Tide to do both

*Heart 0 · Tide ++*

He had held the wall all morning.

It was the only thing he could do, in the sun, and it was very nearly more than he could do. From the roof of the citadel he could just reach the old Tidewarden sea-wall at the root of the harbour's hook, a long curve of black stone, and behind it the boat-sheds and net-houses of the Weir-folk, and in them, the people who could not climb: the old, and the sick from the hospital, and the babies. Since an hour past dawn he had held the sea up against the outside of that wall in a long standing curtain of spray, thin and white and glittering, so that when a beam struck it the water boiled, and roared, and drank the worst of the fire, and the black stone glowed red and did not run.

Each time, it was like taking a spike through the chest.

And then the flagship's mirror turned, and found, between it and the citadel, a little whitewashed temple on the third terrace, and he heard the singing. *Light of the morning, light of the world.*

He had a breath to choose.

He could not hold both. He did not have enough. He had given half of himself into the sea under the Pale Cliffs and poured most of the rest into the *Faro* for ten days, and what was left was a trickle, and the trickle was holding the wall.

But there was more. There was always more. He could feel it, at the very bottom of him, under the mud and the stones and the weed, under the small warm thing that had woken Lyse. The door. The place where the sea came in. He had sworn to himself, standing on this roof at dawn, that he would not open it in daylight. Not in the sun. Not like this. Not after the stillroom.

*Somebody has to stay and tell the Sun it isn't true.*

He opened it.

---

It came in like a wave through a breached dyke.

Not the slow tide he had planned for tonight. Not in the dark, at high water, with the stars over him and the sun gone down. At noon. With the sun standing straight overhead and pressing on him like a hand, and every drop of the sea that came into him meeting that pressure and screaming. It was like being scalded and frozen at once. It was like the shaft in the Sunspire, a thousand times. He heard himself make a sound. He did not stop.

He held the curtain at the wall.

And with the other hand, the hand he had not had a moment ago, he reached down the face of the island to the sea at the foot of the third terrace, and *lifted*.

The water came up out of the harbour in a single green wall, enormous and shining, and stood between the little temple and the white ship, with the noon sun blazing through it. And the beam struck it, and the water exploded into steam with a sound like the end of the world, and the sunfire tore into it and lost itself and broke.

And in the same moment, as if the flagship's mirror-master had seen the first beam fail and lost his temper, two more beams came across the water at the harbour wall, one after the other.

He held.

He felt them strike the curtain. He felt the spray boil, and the stone behind it glow, red, orange, almost white. He felt the heat come back up the water into him, along every line of the Tidemark, as if he had laid his own hands on the stone. He held the curtain, and the wall of water at the third terrace, and the sun pressed down on him, and the sea poured up into him from the bottom of the world, more and more and more, faster than he could use it, filling him.

The stone did not run.

The temple stood.

And out of its doorway, through the steam, came a voice, very loud and very bad and slightly scalded, not stopping. *Light that is mercy, light that is true.*

---

*Yes,* said the Deep.

It was very close. It had never been so close in daylight. It was not three hundred miles away at the bottom of the world; it was here, in him, filling him up to the throat, and he could hear it the way he heard his own heart.

*Yes, little one. You see? You see what you can do, when you let me in? The wall stands. The singer sings. And there are twelve ships out there with their mirrors turned towards you, and two thousand men on them who came to burn your city. And you are full now. Full of me. You could take them now, in the noon sun, in front of all your people. You would not even have to wait for the dark. Down. Take them down.*

He felt it. He felt the twelve hulls in the water, the anchor cables, the men on the decks, the way he felt his own hands. He felt how easy it would be. The sea was already in his hands. It only wanted closing.

*Down.*

He breathed. In for eight. It came out ragged, shaking. Out for eight. It would not go slow. He tried again.

*Not like this,* he said. *Not in the sun. Not in front of Olaf.*

*Why not? He lives. You chose that. Choose this.*

*Because I chose that,* said Edric. *Because I want him to go on singing. Not over two thousand graves.*

He let go.

Not of the door. He could not close the door; it was too late for that, there was too much of the sea in him already, and it would not go back. But of the wall, and the curtain, and the green sheet of water at the third terrace. He let them fall back into the harbour, gently, now that the beams had stopped, and the mirror on the flagship was swinging away, slowly, as if puzzled, to look for something it could burn.

Then he sat down on the roof of the citadel, very suddenly, among the broken pillars.

---

Hayda found him there a quarter of an hour later.

She came up onto the roof at a run, with Bastien's long knife in her hand and blood on her sleeve, and saw him sitting with his back against a pillar and his hands in his lap, and stopped.

"Edric," she said.

He looked up at her.

She did not say anything for a moment. Then she put down the knife, very carefully, on the stones, and came and knelt in front of him, and put her small hand on his face. On the left cheek, below the eye.

He knew what she was touching. He could feel it, under her fingers: cold, and branching, and new. The Tidemark had come back. Up his throat, over his jaw, almost to the eye, where it had been on the night of the storm over Solace. Where he had torn it out of himself on the beach under the Pale Cliffs, one line at a time, until he could not stand.

"Your eyes," said Hayda quietly.

"What about them?"

She did not answer. She did not have to. He saw himself in hers: a man sitting against a pillar in the noon sun, with a face he knew, and in it, where the grey had been, two pools of black water, without bottom.

"Olaf's alive," he said.

"Yes," said Hayda. "And the wall held. And Old Bettan is sitting behind it with forty babies, cursing the Radiants in the Weir-tongue." She did not take her hand from his face. "I know what you did. I know what it cost. Is it done? Is it going back?"

He reached for the door. He tried, very gently, to push the sea back out of it, the way he had on the beach.

It did not move.

"Not today," he said.

### Later

Mira saw it before he had said a word.

She was sitting on the edge of the planks at the end of the walkway in the Weir, with her feet in the grey water and her grey shawl round her shoulders, and she had heard his step a hundred yards away, as Weir-folk always did, and said "You're late" without looking up. And then she looked up. And her face changed.

"You said you'd given it back," she said.

He sat down beside her, and took off his boots, and put his feet in the water. It was cold. Under it, faint, the screaming.

"I did," he said. "Under the Pale Cliffs. Half of it." He touched his own cheek, where the lines were. "And then the King's fleet came to Cristal, and burned a man's temple with him singing in it. Or tried to." He looked out at the grey sea. "I had a breath to choose. I could save him or the harbour wall, and there were forty babies behind the wall. So I opened the door at noon and did both. And the sea came in, and didn't go out again."

Mira was quiet for a long time.

"Is that why you're still standing?" she said. "When the King's drinking it dry? When you should be nothing by now?"

He had not thought of it like that. He thought of it now. The King was draining the Deep, and the Deep's strength out of everything it had touched; every day at sea from Cristal, he had felt it go out of him, the lines growing fainter on his chest and arms. But not on his face. Not the new ones. Those had come in at noon, in the sun, against the fire, and they had set like iron in a forge.

"Yes," he said slowly. "I think it might be."

"Then it's a door," said Mira. "And at the end of all this, you'll have to close it. All of it. And it'll be harder, because you opened it wider." She reached down beside her and brought up something wrapped in a grey cloth, and put it in her lap. "I'm coming with you."

"No."

"I'm coming with you," she said again, quite calmly. "You'll need someone who can hear the sea when yours is gone. And you'll need someone who can tell you, when the time comes, which of the voices in your head is yours." She looked at him, at last, with her dark green eyes, and then at the black water where his had been grey. "Because I don't think you can tell any more. Can you?"

He did not answer. She did not need him to.
