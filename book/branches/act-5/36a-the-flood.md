# Event 36a: The Flood

*An alternative ending (Kept + Vengeance). Branches from Chapter 30, "The Chains", after the seventh chain breaks and Aurelian turns to dust, at the moment the free sea speaks inside Edric: "Now, said the sea, in him. Now, little one. I am free. Let it come."*

---

*Now,* said the sea, in him. *Now, little one. I am free. Let it come.*

He remembered who he was.

That was the trouble. The song had given it back to him, all of it, the way the tide gives back what it takes: a landing stage in the Weir, a girl laughing at a heron, an old man's face coming out of a hole in a shower of dust. He had held on to those things through the breaking of the chains as a drowning man holds a spar. And now he had them, and he was himself again, Edric Vane of Saltmere, and Edric Vane had woken every morning for fourteen years in the dark and said four names.

He remembered those too.

He remembered his father. Not the man who had met him at the top of the stairs on Ropewalk Lane, the night he came home a captain. The other one. The one he had never seen, and had seen every night since in the dark behind his eyes: an old sailor at a window over the harbour, watching for a ship that did not come, getting thinner. Selling the chairs. Selling the bed. Sitting on the floor by the window, at the end, because there was nothing left to sit on, until he could not sit.

He remembered Faro dying in the dark with his face turned to the floor, listening to the King drink.

He remembered a voice singing a hymn to the Sun, very loud and very bad, and then the roar of melting stone.

He remembered the Weir-folk's stilt-houses on Cristal burning, every one, a hundred of them, standing in the shallow water like candles, while the Weir-folk watched from the terraces and did not weep.

He remembered the Street of Gold, and the Chamber of Lords, and a thousand fine people in Solace who had eaten and danced and bought and sold for three hundred years on top of a sea that was dying, and never once looked down. Who had cheered the Sun King's birthday every year with fireworks over the harbour while the fish came up empty and the children coughed in the fishing towns. Who had sent a nineteen-year-old to the Keep for natural life on a letter he never read, and gone home to dinner.

He felt the wave, standing up out of the sea on every coast of the world. Higher than any cliff. Waiting.

*Let them feel it,* he thought. *Once. Just once. Let them know what it was like, at the bottom. Let them know what they were standing on.*

*Yes,* he said.

---

He did not let go all at once. He would remember that, afterwards, in the long time when there was nothing left of him to remember with except the sea. He did not open his hands and drop it. He thought he could do it carefully. He thought he could choose.

He reached out with the sea towards Solace, to the white city on its cliffs, to the Street of Gold and the Chamber of Lords and the palaces along the cliff road where the great houses lived. *There,* he thought. *Those. Not the fishing towns. Not the Weir. Not Cristal. Those.* And he let the wave go a little, towards them, the way a man lets out a line, an arm's length at a time.

And the sea laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh. It was not anything. It was the sound a great water makes when a little dam gives way in front of it: a rushing, a release, a joy so vast and so simple that there was no room in it for anything else.

*Little one,* said the sea, very gently, the way you speak to a child who has asked whether the rain could fall only on the bad people's houses. *A wave does not ask whether the cliff deserves to fall. You knew that. I told you, at the beginning.*

*I can choose,* he said. *I'm choosing. Those. Only those.*

*You said yes.*

And the line ran out through his hands, all of it, at once.

---

He felt it come in over the world.

He felt it everywhere, all at once, the way he had felt the sea's three hundred years: not one shore, but every shore. Not one city but every city on every coast, from the ice of the far north to the beaches of the southern empires. And he could not close his eyes to it, because he did not have eyes. He had only the sea. And the sea felt everything it touched.

He felt Solace.

The wave came up the face of the cliffs like a hand. He felt the white stone under it, the stairs cut into the rock, the harbour below the cliffs where the *Faro* had lain with her sails loosed. He felt the water reach the top of the cliffs and go on, over the edge, into the streets. The Street of Gold. The coffee houses, where they had said every morning for a season that the Count of Cristal was the richest man in the world. The counting-house of Quell, with its doors shut. The Chamber of Lords, where the lamps were lit for an emergency session nobody would finish. The house with the blue door and the black marble pool, where the fountain had stopped dancing. He felt the water fill the courtyard. He felt it fill the study at the back, where he had sat in the dark with a bowl of still water in front of him and counted days.

He felt the grey house at the far end of the Street of Lanterns. The walled garden behind it, and the chestnut tree, and a man in the garden in the dark, kneeling, with a small spade in his hand, digging. Corvan Valtor looked up as the water came over the wall. His ruined grey face was quite calm.

*There,* he said, to nobody. *There. They're coming up out of the dark.* And he put down the spade.

The Sunspire stood. It was too well built to fall. The water climbed it, a long way, and the glass at the top caught the first grey of the coming dawn and threw it back, and in the great round room a white cup on a small table trembled, very slightly, and did not fall.

---

He felt Saltmere.

He felt the sea draw back from the harbour, two miles, three, leaving the boats lying on their sides in the mud and the light tower standing at the end of the harbour wall over nothing. He felt the people on the quays, the fishwives and the rope-makers and the children, standing at the edge of the bare mud in the dark, staring. He felt an old man among them, seventy, with a stick, and his coat buttoned wrong, looking out to sea the way he had looked out to sea on a summer afternoon twenty years ago, waiting for the *Gull's Promise*.

*Not him,* said Edric. *Not Moren. Please. Not him.*

The sea did not answer. It did not hear words any more. It was a wave.

He felt Ropewalk Lane. The tall crooked house at the top, with the window looking out over the harbour. The window where his father had watched. He felt the water come up the hill and fill the street and go in at the door, and up the stairs, the way he had come up them with a sack of potatoes and a ham and a bottle of good red wine, and fill the room at the top where there had been no chairs at the end, and no bed.

He felt the Weir.

The Weir-folk did not run. That was the thing he would never be able to stop feeling. They had heard the sea crying for three weeks, the ones who were left, and they had heard it go silent, and then they had heard it breathe, and they knew what that meant. They knew before anyone in Saltmere. And when the water went out from under their stilt-houses, out of the channels and the reed-beds and away past the mouth of the marsh, leaving their boats in the mud and their walkways hanging over nothing, they did not climb the hill to the town. They came out of their houses, and sat down on the edges of their landing stages, with their feet hanging over the bare mud where the water had been, the oldest way of sitting, and waited for it to come back.

Some of them were singing. He could feel the shape of it. *Take us out.*

It took them out.

---

He felt Cristal.

He had not meant to. He had sworn, a breath ago, *not Cristal*. But Cristal was an island in the sea, and the sea was everywhere at once, and he was the sea.

He felt the harbour empty. He felt the burned quays, and the Long Bank standing up out of the bare sand a mile off with the white ships still leaning on it, and the people on the terraces by the lamps. He felt the wave come in over the reefs.

He felt Tam, on the second terrace, at the door of the school, shouting. Shouting at the children to go up, up, not down, up the stair, to the citadel, to the roof, and the children running past him, Weir-folk children and Sardhi children and smugglers' children, and Tam standing in the door until the last one was past, and then running after them.

He felt the water come up the processional stair behind them. The first terrace. The second. The school. The third terrace, where a smoking hollow lay in the rubble and a lump of half-melted brass that had once been a sun. He felt it come up past the hospital and the houses and the market square, up and up the face of the island, towards the citadel on the summit with its broken dome, and he felt the people in the library at the top.

Lyse Valtor was at the window, beside her grandfather's chair. Maxim Moren was beside her, with his arm round her. They had watched the sea go out. Now they watched it come back.

Severin Valtor sat in his chair with the rug over his knees and his dark eyes open, looking at the sea. He had asked to see it free before he died. He was seeing it. Lyse had the board in her hands. She was holding it up to him, with her hands shaking, the way she had held it up to him every day of her life.

He did not look at it. He looked at the wave.

And then, very slowly, he closed his eyes.

The water came up over the third terrace and the second gallery of the citadel and the walls of the library, and stopped. Just below the broken ring of pillars where the dome had been. It hung there, a long moment, lapping at the stone where forty children and a schoolmaster lay flat on the roof in the dark, holding on to each other. Then it began to go back down.

He did not know why it had stopped. He would never know. Perhaps it was only that the citadel was very high. Perhaps the sea had simply reached as far as it was going to reach. He would have liked to think it was something else. He did not let himself.

When the water went back down the face of the island, it took the library windows with it, and the fire, and the bed in the corner, and the chair by the window with the rug over its arms. Lyse and Maxim were on the roof, among the children, when the dawn came. Nobody ever told him how they got there. Nobody ever told him anything again.

---

And he felt the temple.

He had forgotten it. That was what he could never forgive. In all the wide world going under, he had forgotten the cave under the black rock where his own body lay on a salt-crusted ledge, and the people who had come there with him.

The pool was rising.

The dead grey water, that had shrunk twenty feet in the weeks of the King's drinking, came up out of the dark under the Teeth all at once, the way water comes up a well when the sea outside rises. It came up over the lowest ledge, where a little heap of grey dust lay that had been a King. It came up over the ledge where his own body lay, and lifted it, and turned it, gently, face down.

He felt them. Jac, with her one good hand twisted in his collar, trying to drag him back up the ledge, swearing. Hayda on her knees beside her, with her arms round his chest. Bastien and Aren, coming down the ledges from the longboat with the water round their knees, their thighs, their waists. Cassia Vell, sitting up by the boat with her burned black hands in her lap, looking at the water coming up the cave, very calm, like a woman who has finally been told the truth about something. The Hundred, on the ledges, old men in white and gold, beginning to climb.

And Mira. Kneeling at the edge of the pool where the chains had gone down, with her hand still in the water, where she had laid the crown. She had not taken it out. She had not moved. She was looking down into the water, at the place where he was, and her lips were moving.

*Please,* he said. To the sea. To himself. He could not tell any more which was which. *Please. Not them. You can have everything else. You can have the whole world. Just not them.*

He reached for them. He reached with all of himself, down through the roaring, for the small warm thing at the bottom, the well with water in it, the thing that had woken Lyse and laughed in a small boat when a gull stole a bread roll. If he could find it, he could hold back one cave. One pool. One handful of water out of all the water in the world. Surely he could hold back that.

He reached down, and down, and down. He could not find the bottom.

There was no bottom. There was only the sea, all the way down.

The water reached Mira's shoulders. Her mouth. She did not get up. She had sat on a landing stage every spring tide for two years watching the light on the Teeth, not knowing he was under it. She had said she would not sit and watch it again.

He felt her singing as the water closed over her. Not with her voice. With the water in her. The oldest song there was, older than Saltmere, older than the King, in the oldest form of the Weir-tongue.

*Take us out.*

The sea took them out.

---

The tide went back in the morning.

All over the world, on every coast, the water drew back off the land, slowly, the way a breath goes out, and lay down in its bed again, and was still. It left behind it a world he did not recognise. Mud, and wreckage, and silence. Harbours full of the wrecks of ships. Cities with their lower streets filled to the eaves with sand and weed. Sails and timbers and rope and the furniture of ten thousand houses strewn across the fields a mile inland, where the wave had laid them down. And the birds, coming back. Thousands and thousands of them, circling over the shore, screaming.

And the sea.

The sea was blue.

It was the most beautiful morning there had been in three hundred years. The sun came up over the edge of the world, red, then gold, and the water under it was the deep, clear, living blue of the open ocean on a summer morning, the colour he remembered from the deck of the *Gull's Promise* off the Ember Coast, when he was nineteen and believed in endings. It stretched away in every direction, glittering. It was full of fish. Shoals of silverfin turned the sea white for a mile, the great shoals that Halloran used to talk about, rising to the surface in the dawn light and turning all together like a field of stars. Out past where the Teeth had been, something vast and dark rose out of the water and blew a great plume of spray into the morning air, and sank again, and its tail rose after it, black and enormous, and fell, and the sound of it came across the water like a bell.

The sea was well. The blight was gone out of it. The sickness was gone. The weed would grow back on the rocks and the fish would run thick on the banks, and the storms would come at the right time of year, for the first time since the Drying. He could feel it. He could feel it in every drop of himself.

*Thank you,* said the sea.

It said it quite simply, with enormous tenderness. It was not cruel. It had never been cruel. It was the sea.

*You gave me back everything. Every life he drank. Every year. With interest.*

---

He was standing on the water.

He did not know how long he had been standing there. Above the place where the Teeth had been, where now there was only blue water, very deep, with the morning light going down into it. He looked down at himself. He did not know why he still had a self to look at. Perhaps the sea had simply kept the shape of him, the way it kept the shape of a wreck on the bottom, out of habit.

The Tidemark covered every part of him. His hands, his arms, his chest, his throat, his face. It was not blue any more. It was the colour of the water around him, so that when he looked down, he could not quite tell where the water stopped and he began.

He walked.

He did not decide to. He walked across the sea towards the land, the way a man walks home, without thinking where he is going, and the water held him up. It took a long time. He did not get tired. The sun came up and climbed, and stood straight overhead at noon, and did not hurt him at all. The sea was too strong now for any sun to hurt.

He came to Saltmere in the evening.

There was not much left. The harbour wall stood, mostly. The light tower was gone. The quays were heaped with wreckage and sand. Up the hill, the town was a long scar of mud and broken timber and roofless walls, going up and up, until about halfway, where the wave had stopped, and above that, the houses stood whole and white with their red roofs in the evening light, and people moved among them, very slowly, carrying things. Nobody looked at him. He was only a shape on the water, far out, in the glare of the sunset, and they did not see him.

He went to the Weir.

It was gone. Not ruined. Gone. The stilt-houses, the walkways, the landing stages, the lamps; the whole lamp-hung web strung across the marsh, that had been there before Saltmere, before the harbour wall, before the town, before the King. There was only open water, and reed-beds, and here and there a post standing up out of the channel where a house had been, black and dripping. He walked between them. He could not tell which had been the Solens'. He could not tell where the end of the walkway had been, where an old blind woman had sat for fifty years and listened to the sea.

Something was floating in the channel.

A small boat, with no oars. Old, its paint long gone, its timbers grey. It had been under a house, on the mud, wrapped in sailcloth, for twenty years; an old woman had kept it there, against the day. The sailcloth was gone now. It floated in the middle of the channel on the evening tide, empty, turning slowly, the way a leaf turns on a stream. There was nothing in it at all. No garland. No sea-lavender. No white shells.

He stood on the water and looked at it for a long time.

---

The laugh started very small.

It started somewhere in his chest, where the scar from the sunfire had been, before there was nothing there but water. It was not happiness. It was the sound a man makes when he has been struck so hard that the pain has gone past being pain, and comes out the other side as something else. He had made a sound like it once, in a cell under the sea, when the wall he had dug through for weeks turned out to be the wrong wall.

It came up into his throat. It came out of his mouth.

And the sea took it up.

That was the worst of it. He felt the sea feel it, the way it felt everything, and answer. The sound he was making was the sound the sea made when it broke on a beach, when it ran up the shingle and drew back hissing, when it came in over a reef. It was the oldest sound in the world. It was joy, to the sea. It did not know any other kind.

He laughed. He could not stop. He stood on the water among the black posts where the Weir had been, beside an empty wedding boat, and laughed until the sound of it rolled out across the channel and the marsh and the ruined town, and the people on the hill stopped what they were doing and looked down at the water, and saw a shape out there in the dusk, and heard it, and went inside and closed their doors.

*It looked out of her eyes,* a book had said, in a library at the top of a citadel, of the last of the three tide-taken Wardens the books did not like to name. *And there was nothing human left in them. And the storm she raised drowned the city of Ys and everyone in it, the guilty with the innocent, the Warden's enemies with the Warden's children. And when it was done she walked into the sea, laughing.*

He had read it once, and closed the book very carefully, and sat for a long time with his hands flat on the table.

He walked into the sea.

He went down off the surface the way a man goes down a stair, and the water closed over his head, warm and blue and full of light, and the evening sun came down through it in long gold bars, and silver fish went past him in their thousands. He went down, and down, and the laughing went on, all round him, in the water, everywhere, and he could not tell any more whether it was his, and then it did not matter, because there was no more him to tell.

---

In the library at the top of the citadel on Cristal, under the broken dome, the shelves were empty. The sea had taken the books.

All but one. Tam had found it on the roof on the first morning, among the children, where someone had carried it up the stair in the dark and could not afterwards remember doing it: *The Book of the Masters of the House of Cristal*, swollen with seawater, its pages stuck together, its green leather cover gone black. He had dried it by the fire, a page at a time, for a month. Most of the writing had run. Some had not.

When it was dry, he sat down at the table where the Warden used to sit, and turned to the end of it, past the last chapter in a hand that shook, *On the Return*, to the blank pages after, and dipped his pen, and wrote.

He wrote it down as plainly as he could. Who the Warden had been. Where he had come from. What had been done to him, and what he had done. The Long Day. The white ships on the Long Bank. The King at the Teeth. The sea going out on every coast, and coming back. He set down the names of the dead that he knew, which were not many, out of all that there were. He set down that Severin Valtor had closed his eyes. He set down that the Weir in Saltmere was gone, and the Weir-folk with it, all but the families who had come west, and that those sat now on the shore below the citadel every evening with their feet in the water, and did not sing.

He set down the last part as the old book had set down the Warden of Ys, because he could not think of any better way to say it.

*And when it was done, he walked into the sea, laughing.*

Then he sat for a long time with the pen in his hand. And then he wrote one more line, below it, smaller.

*The children who can hear the sea say that it was not laughing. They say it was the other thing, the thing that sounds like it, and that he is doing it still.*

He closed the book.

Outside, beyond the broken pillars, the sea lay blue and calm and glittering to the edge of the world. The fish were back. The fishing boats that were left came home every evening low in the water. The blight was gone. The blighted things had stopped coming ashore. It was, everyone agreed, the kindest sea anyone had ever known.

Nobody on Cristal went down to it after dark.

*THE END*
