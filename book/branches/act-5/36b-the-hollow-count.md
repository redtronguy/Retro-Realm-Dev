# Event 36b: The Hollow Count

*An alternative ending (Given + Vengeance). Branches from Chapter 30, "The Chains", after the seventh chain breaks and Aurelian turns to dust, at the moment the free sea speaks inside Edric: "Now, said the sea, in him. Now, little one. I am free. Let it come."*

---

*Now,* said the sea, in him. *Now, little one. I am free. Let it come.*

*No,* said Edric.

He held the stillness. He held it at the bottom of everything, under the rage, under the wave, under the whole free and furious sea, the way you hold a door shut against a storm. And he looked at what the sea wanted, standing up out of the water on every coast of the world, higher than any cliff, and he understood that he did not want it.

Not because it was cruel. He had stopped minding cruelty a long time ago, somewhere between a cave in the Greyhills and a stillroom on the Street of Lanterns. Because it was *careless*.

The sea would drown them all. The Street of Gold and the Chamber of Lords and the white palaces on the cliff road, yes. But also the fishwives on the quays at Saltmere, and the Weir, and the children in Tam's school, and an old shipowner with his coat buttoned wrong. It would drown them all the same, the guilty and the innocent, in a single breath, and never know the difference. And when it was done, the men who had put him in the Keep and sat on the Chamber's benches while the fishing towns starved would be no more punished than a fisherman's child. They would simply be gone. They would never know it had been him. They would never know why.

*A wave doesn't ask whether the cliff deserves to fall,* the Deep had said, under the Teeth, at the beginning.

*No,* he had said, on a beach under the Pale Cliffs. *But I do.*

He did. He still did. That was the one thing the sea could not take from him. It could have his strength, his Tidemark, his hands and his heart. It could not have the asking.

*It isn't yours,* he said to the sea. *It was never yours. They didn't chain me in the Keep for fourteen years. They didn't starve my father. You don't get to spend what they owe me on people who never owed me anything. You don't get to make it quick.*

*Then what will you do with it, little one?*

*Give it back,* said Edric Vane. *All of it. Every drop. And then I'll do the rest myself.*

---

He gave it back.

It was like the beach under the Pale Cliffs, and nothing like it. On the beach, he had given back the silt of five years because he had looked in a mirror and seen the sea looking out, and been afraid. He was not afraid now. He reached for the sea in him, all of it, three hundred years and more, and pushed it out of himself the way a man pushes a heavy door that has stuck, with his shoulder, steadily, without hope or tenderness or anything at all but the will to have it open.

It did not want to go.

It clung to him. It wanted the wave. It pushed against him, the whole free furious weight of the sea, and he felt himself begin to crack, the last of himself, and he did not reach for the small warm thing at the bottom to hold himself together. He did not need it. He had something colder, and harder, and older, that had held him together through fourteen years in the dark. He had four names.

He pushed. He felt the sea begin to go out of him. Slowly. Out of his blood, out of his breath, out of the lines of the Tidemark, one by one, like lamps going out along a quay at dawn. He felt the wave, far out on every coast, hesitate.

And lie down.

It sank back into itself, all over the world, without breaking. The sea came back over the bare mud in a long, slow, gentle tide, and filled the harbours, and stopped at the feet of the people on the beaches, and lay still.

The last of the sea went out of Edric Vane.

*You gave back,* said the Deep, from very far away, as he sank. *All of it. Every drop.*

It was quiet for a long time, in the dark.

*But you did not give it back for me,* it said. *Did you, little one?*

He did not answer. He was drowning.

---

They pulled him out. Mira, and Hayda, and Jac Teague with her one good arm. He lay on the salt-crusted stone of the lowest ledge with the sea coming out of his lungs in grey gouts, and the chains gone, and the gold light gone, and the first grey of dawn coming in through the open sea-gate. He lifted his hand and looked at it. The lines were gone. Only skin.

He reached for the sea. It was there, very faint, like the sound in a shell held to the ear. It was not screaming. It was singing.

He listened to it for a moment, lying on the stone. Then he took his hand away from the water.

"Good," he said.

Mira, kneeling beside him with her hair plastered to her face, looked at him.

He saw her see it. He saw her look for the thing she had seen across the room at the dinner in Solace, the thing that had made her know him in an instant under every face he wore. He saw her find it, and find something else lying on top of it, the way silt lies on the bottom of a harbour after the tide has gone out. Cold, and grey, and settled.

She did not say anything. She took her hand off his face.

---

He did not go back to Cristal.

The others went: the *Faro* and the *Slip*, west, across a sea so blue and so full of life that the crews kept leaning over the rails to stare at it. Lyse would be waiting there, and Maxim, and Tam, and an old man in a chair by a window who had died at dawn on the day the sea turned blue, with his eyes open. Edric heard about that afterwards, in a letter from Jac. He read it standing up, and folded it, and put it in a drawer.

He went to Solace.

He went the way the Count of Cristal had come to Solace the first time, a season ago: by the coast road, with a coach and four, and Bastien beside him, and a line of wagons behind, and Rook's men riding outriders in plain leather on good horses. He did not wear any face but his own. He could not have worn another. There was not enough of the sea left in him for Still Water. But he had never really needed it in Solace. He had always had the other things.

He had money. Five years of the Tidewardens' treasure, and five more of Cristal's trade, and every mark of it turned, in a single season, into banks and debts and ships and men. And he had paper. Nineteen years of it. The black ledger from the counting-house of Quell. Valtor's private files, taken from the grey house the night of the trial. Fenrick's letters from the Sardh. And everything else he had gathered in the house with the blue door, in the long nights when he could not sleep, about every lord in the Chamber and every merchant on the Street of Gold: what they owed, and to whom, and what they had done to get it, and what they were most afraid of. He did not need Undertow to feel where the money in Solace went now. He had written it down.

The Chamber of Lords had been sitting in emergency session for a month when he arrived. It had agreed on nothing. There were lords who wanted a new King, and lords who wanted no King at all, and lords who wanted to be King themselves, and none of them had any money, because the Treasury had been empty since the fall of the house of Quell, and none of them had any army, because the Lord Marshal was dead on the floor of his study and his veterans were rioting for their pay.

Edric paid the veterans. Out of his own pocket, in gold, on the first morning, in the square before the Chamber. Then he walked into the Chamber of Lords and took a chair at the end of the long table, and began, one by one, very quietly, to talk to them.

It took him nine days.

---

They named him Lord Protector of Valemont, for a year.

He did not ask for it. He let them offer it, which was better; it let them think they had chosen. The year became two. The two became ten. Nobody ever quite decided to make it permanent. Nobody ever quite dared to decide to stop.

On the morning after the vote, he climbed the Sunspire.

The shaft of gold light was gone. The door at the foot of the tower opened for anyone now, and beyond it, where the column of burning sunfire had been, there was a spiral stair, a thousand feet of it, winding up through the heart of the white stone, that no one had known was there. He climbed it alone. It took him most of the morning. He stopped every hundred steps, with his hand on the wall and his breath going hard, a thin grey man of thirty-eight who could no longer drive a ship with the sea or walk through sunfire or make stone sweat. He climbed it anyway.

The great round room at the top was empty, and cold, and very bright.

The glass walls looked out on the whole of Valemont: the white city on its cliffs, the hills, the coast running away north and south, and the sea, blue now, glittering all the way to the edge of the world. In the centre of the floor there was a well, going down into the dark, and nothing coming up out of it at all. And beside the well, a white chair. And on a small table beside the chair, a white cup, like a cup for milk, and a teapot, and two other cups, very fine and very old, that nobody had ever drunk from.

He stood and looked at the chair for a long time.

Then he sat down in it.

---

He was very good at it. He found that out in the first year and went on finding it out for a long time.

He abolished the Sun Warrants. He opened the old King's granaries. He sent the Blight Fund's two million marks, every letter of credit, back to the fishing towns and the hospitals, with an audit nailed to the door of every beacon on the coast so that anyone could read where the money had gone. He had the histories of the Drying rewritten in every school in Valemont, as they had really happened. He took the Radiant Order apart, knight by knight, and sent the Hundred, the old men who had stood on the ledges at the Teeth with sunfire in their hands, to live out their lives in a fortress in the far north, where they could do no harm. He put the Chamber of Lords on half-rations of power and kept it there.

And the sea was free, and the fish came back, and the blight went out of the coast towns, and the children who had been dying of the blight-cough got up out of their beds and breathed. People said the Lord Protector had done it. He did not correct them.

He was not cruel. He was careful never to be cruel. He was only exact.

---

They brought Dorran Quell back from the south in the spring.

He had been found in a city on the coast of the southern empires, in a small room above a ships' chandler's, working as a clerk in a counting-house, keeping someone else's books. Edric's agents had known where he was for a year. They brought him north in a closed carriage, thin and grey and quiet, with his hands in his lap, and put him on trial before the Great Court of Solace for the theft of four million marks from the Blight Fund.

He did not deny it. He stood in the dock in a plain brown coat and confessed to every mark, in a steady clerk's voice, column by column, from memory. When the High Justicar asked if he had anything to say in his defence, he looked up, for the first time, at the Lord Protector sitting at the side of the court.

"I thought it was finished," said Dorran Quell. "In the cave. You fed me bread with your own hands. I thought it was finished between us."

"It was," said Edric. "Between us. This is for the fishing towns."

The court sentenced him to ten years' labour, tending the beacons on the coast he had let go dark. Dorran bowed, very slightly, the way a clerk bows when a sum comes out right, and let them take him away. He died in the fourth year, of a fever, in a stone hut beside a beacon on a headland north of Grey Point. The keeper said he had kept the beacon's oil accounts until the day before, in a neat small careful hand, and that they balanced to the drop.

---

He had Corvan Valtor brought before the Great Court too.

Valtor did not know where he was. He stood in the dock where his son had stood, and looked about him with his ruined grey face at the galleries and the judges and the crowd, and smiled, vaguely, like a man at a party where he does not know anyone, and asked twice if anyone had seen his sons, because he had put them somewhere in the dark and could not remember where. The court found him unfit to plead.

Edric had him sent to an island in the far south anyway, a rock with a fortress on it, to be kept. *For the safety of the realm.*

Lyse came to Solace to ask him not to.

She came up the thousand steps of the Sunspire on her own feet, with Maxim behind her, and stood in the great round room in front of the white chair, pale and straight, in grey, with her mother's pearls at her throat. She had been married a year. She was carrying her first child.

"He doesn't know you," she said. "He doesn't know anyone. He digs holes in the garden and fills them in. He's harmless. He's being punished every hour of every day by whatever's in his own head. You don't need to do this."

"He buried me alive," said Edric. "Fourteen years. And his own son. And he'd have let you die too, to keep his name clean."

"I know," said Lyse. "I was there." She looked at him with her grave dark eyes. "You saved my life. You put me to sleep for forty days and carried me across the sea, and you came back for me when everyone said you couldn't. I'll be grateful to you as long as I live." She was quiet a moment. "And I knelt in the mud in my father's garden last spring, and held his hand, and forgave him. Not for him. For me. So I wouldn't have to carry it." She looked at the white chair, and the white cup, and the man sitting there. "I thought you of all people would understand that."

He did not answer.

Lyse waited. Then she turned, and took her husband's hand, and went back down the thousand steps. She did not come to Solace again. When her son was named, on Cristal, at midsummer, under the tree in the Court of Tides, the Lord Protector was sent a letter afterwards, very civil, very short, to tell him it had been done.

---

Andric was taken in the Sardh in the third year, running a gambling house in Mazrah under the name of a prince. Edric's agents brought him back in chains, and the Great Court, which had condemned him once already for the murder of Cade Rusk, condemned him again, and this time there was no inn in the hills, no unlocked chains on a carriage floor. The Lord Protector signed the order himself.

Bastien Corso came to the Sunspire the night before the hanging.

He did not sit. He stood in front of the white chair with his black brows drawn down and his face like a closed door, and looked at the man in it for a long time.

"I dug him up," said Bastien. "Out of the earth under the chestnut tree. With my hands. He wasn't breathing. I breathed for him." He was quiet a moment. "I told you, after the trial. Whatever he does, I'll carry it."

"He's a murderer," said Edric. "He killed Cade. He'd have killed Elsabet Quell, if she hadn't run. He'll kill again, if he lives."

"Yes," said Bastien. "Probably." He took the long knife from his belt, the one Hayda had carried on Cristal, on the third terrace, at the top of the stair, and laid it on the small table beside the white cup. "I've carried a lot of things for you, Warden. I carried your gold. I carried your letters. I held the stair at Cristal. I rowed you to the Teeth." He straightened up. "I'll not carry this."

He went down the thousand steps in the dark. He did not come back. Jac wrote, months later, that he was on Cristal, working on the quays, and did not talk about Solace, and that if Edric had any sense, which she doubted, he would not write to him.

Edric did not write.

---

Hayda stayed the longest.

She stayed for five years. She was the only one of them who did. She sat on the council of the Lord Protector, in the chair that had been Dorran Quell's, and ran the Treasury better than he had ever run it, and nobody on the Street of Gold dared to cheat her twice. She came up the thousand steps every evening, at sunset, and sat in the great round room with him while the light went out of the sky over the sea, and talked about the day's business in her low clear voice, in High Sardhi, so that the servants would not understand.

She never once mentioned a gull.

On the last evening, in the autumn of the fifth year, she came up the steps and did not sit down. She stood at the glass wall, with her back to him, looking out at the sea.

"I have had a letter," she said. "From Qasr. From the people who were my father's people. The emir who took his place is dead, and his sons are fighting, and the city is starving. They want someone to come. Someone with my name, who can make them stop." She did not turn round. "I am going."

"I can send ships," said Edric. "Gold. Men. Rook would go, if I asked."

"I know you can." She turned round then. She looked at him, sitting in the white chair, with the teapot on the table beside him and the white cup and the two other cups, very fine and very old. "You told me once, on the deck of the *Slip*, in Mazrah, that I was free. That I was no one's. You said it was my choice from then on, always, everything." She was quiet a moment. "I chose to stay. For five years. I want you to know that I chose it. I want you to know I am choosing this."

"Why?" said Edric.

She did not answer at once. She looked at him with her desert-well eyes, steadily, the way she had looked at the room in the house with the blue door on her first night in Solace, to learn where everything was kept.

"Because I came up here every evening for five years," said Hayda bint Tariq, "and sat with you while the sun went down over the sea. And in five years, you never once looked at it."

---

The year became ten. The ten became twenty.

He was very good at it. Valemont had never been so well governed, everyone said. The roads were safe. The granaries were full. The Sun Warrants were a memory, and the Drowned Keep was open water, and nobody went hungry in the fishing towns. The sea was blue and generous and singing, and the fish ran thick on the banks, and on Cristal there were children who could hear it, a dozen of them now, and then a score, learning their letters from a schoolmaster called Tam. And at the top of the Sunspire, in a white chair beside an empty well, the Lord Protector of Valemont sat and read the reports, and signed them, and set them aside, and read the next.

People called him the Hollow Count. Not to his face. He knew it anyway. He did not mind. It was accurate. He had always valued accuracy.

Mira did not come to Solace. She did not write. He had not expected her to. But in the spring of every year, at the full moon, at the spring tide, the luckiest tide of the year, a single line arrived at the Sunspire by the ordinary post from Saltmere, with no name, in a hand he had once known better than his own.

*The tide is high tonight.*

He read it every year. He did not go.

In the twenty-first year, it did not come.

He waited for it until midsummer. Then he sent a man to Saltmere, quietly, to ask. The man came back a fortnight later, and stood in front of the white chair, and told him, as gently as he could, that the Countess Morrow, who had gone back to calling herself Mira Solen, had died in the winter, in her house in the Weir, with her son and her son's children round her. That she had been buried, the Weir-folk way, in the sea. That her son had sent her off on the morning tide in a small grey boat with no oars, very old, garlanded with sea-lavender and white shells, and the boat had gone out of the channel on its own, and away past the harbour mouth, and nobody had seen it since.

Edric thanked him, and sent him away.

---

He sat in the white chair for a long time after the man had gone.

It was evening. The light was going out of the sky over the sea. He could see the whole of Valemont spread out below him through the glass, the white city and the hills and the coast running away north and south, and the sea, endless and glittering, all the way to the edge of the world.

He did not look at it.

He looked, instead, at his own hands, lying on the arms of the white chair. Thin and scarred and old, with nothing on them. No lines. Only skin.

Every morning for fourteen years, in a cell under the sea, he had woken in the dark and said four names. Dorran. Fenrick. Valtor. Cade. He had said them every morning in Cristal, too, and in the house with the blue door, and for a while after. And one by one he had crossed them out. Dead on the floor of his study with his own sword through him. Dead in an alley with a knife in his back. Dead in a hut by a beacon, keeping the oil accounts. Shut up on a rock in the far south, digging holes in a courtyard and filling them in.

He had won. All of it. Everything he had ever sworn to do in the dark, he had done. There was no one left to beat.

There was no one left at all.

He reached out, without thinking, the way a man reaches for something that has always been on the table beside him, and picked up the white cup. It was cold. It had been cold for twenty years. He held it in his hands for a while, looking down into it.

Then he leaned over the edge of the well, and dipped it in.

Nothing came up. There was nothing to come. The well went down into the dark, a thousand feet, and down through the cliffs of Solace, and under the sea, three hundred miles, to a place where a black rock had stood once, and now there was only blue water, very deep, with the light going down into it. And at the bottom of the water, something vast and old and free, turning slowly in the dark, singing.

He could not hear it. He had given back too much. Or perhaps it was only that he was a thousand feet up, behind glass, in a white tower on a cliff, and the sea was a long way down.

He lifted the empty cup to his lips anyway, and held it there.

*I was afraid,* the King had said, kneeling at the edge of the pool with his ancient clawed hands raised in front of his face. *I was so afraid. I only wanted not to be afraid.*

Edric Vane was not afraid. He had not been afraid of anything in twenty years. There was nothing left that he could lose.

He set the cup down on the table, beside the teapot and the two other cups, very fine and very old, that nobody had ever drunk from. And he sat in the white chair at the top of the Sunspire, with his back to the sea, while the last of the light went out of the sky, and did not call for a lamp.
