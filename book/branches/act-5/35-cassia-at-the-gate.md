## Refuse and break the gate yourself

*Heart 0 · Tide +++*

Edric looked at her for a long time, at her plain square face, and her pale eyes, steady in the gold glow coming up out of the water.

He thought about her hands. Big and square and scarred, lying in her lap by the fire in the library on Cristal. The hands that had knelt for six hours a day for twenty-two years. The hands that had laid her sword on the shingle. He thought about Olaf's hands, polishing a brass sun with his sleeve. Jac's arm, hanging useless in its sling. Faro's hand, the one that had never worked again after the first stroke.

*Everyone pays,* he thought. *Everyone but me. I've been paying with the sea's coin for six years, and the sea's coin is free, until it isn't.*

"No," he said.

Cassia frowned. "Warden—"

"No. Sit down, Cassia. Please." He was already standing in the bow, with his coat off, looking at the burning bars. "You'll need your hands. Afterwards. Whatever's left of the Order will need someone to follow who can still hold a sword and set it down again." He did not look round. "This one's mine."

"You can't," said Jac, from the tiller, flatly. "You couldn't raise a cup of water. You said so yourself. You've been getting emptier every day since Cristal."

"I know."

"Then what are you going to break it *with*?"

He did not answer. He looked down, past the gate, through the dead grey water, to where the seven chains went down from the foot of the rock, blazing, into the dark. And at the bottom of them, so far down that he could barely feel it, so faint that it was almost nothing, something breathed.

Jac followed his eyes. He felt her understand. He felt her go cold.

"Edric," she said. "No."

---

He knelt in the bow of the longboat and put both hands into the water.

It was cold, colder than anything, the cold of something dying. He went down into the stillness. It was easy to find; there was nothing left in him to disturb it. Past the mud and the stones and the weed, past the small warm thing at the bottom that had woken Lyse, down to the door. He opened it. Nothing came. A trickle. A breath. Faro's drop of water on a stone floor.

So he did not wait for it to come. He went through the door himself, and down, the way you go down a well on a rope, hand over hand, three hundred feet, a thousand, along the gold of the chains, into the dark, to the place where the Deep lay bound. And when he found it, he did what he had sworn he would never do.

He took.

Not a cup. Not a trickle. He reached into the faint, clouded, dying thing at the end of the chains with both hands, the way the King reached into the light, and *drank*.

It came up into him. Thin, and weak, and very cold, the last of the sea's strength, three hundred years of it drained down to the dregs, and it came up into him like the last water in a well coming up into a bucket, with mud in it, and weed. And he felt the Deep feel it. He felt something vast and old and nearly gone flinch at the bottom of the world, the way it flinched when the King drank. From him.

*Little one,* said a voice, so faint it was almost not there. *You too?*

*I'm sorry,* he said. *I'll give it back. All of it. I swear I'll give it back.*

*He says that,* said the Deep. *Every day, for three hundred years. Tomorrow I will drink less.*

He did not stop. He could not stop. If he stopped, Cassia would put her hands on the gate.

He drank until the Tidemark came back down his arms like frost spreading across a window. Until it climbed his throat, and his jaw, and his cheek, and touched the corner of his eye. Until the cold was so deep in him that he could not feel his hands in the water. Until he could taste the salt, and under the salt, memories that were not his: a white city burning, a young prince with his arms raised, three hundred years of being drunk. He felt the sea's rage, what was left of it, come up into him with the rest. Thin, now. Not a storm any more. A fever. A low, sick, burning hatred, like the hatred of a starving man for the one who keeps the bread.

He stood up in the bow of the longboat.

---

He did not bend the bars. He did not look for the joins. He had not been trained in the King's workings for twenty-two years, and he had no patience left for them. He took hold of the sea in front of the gate, the dead grey water, all of it, for a hundred yards in every direction, and lifted it up the face of the rock, and drove it at the bars like a ram.

The first blow boiled away. The water struck the sunfire and flashed into steam with a scream, and the bars blazed white and did not move, and the longboat rocked under them so that Bastien had to throw himself on the far gunwale to keep her upright.

He did it again.

And again. He did not count. He took the sea up the rock, and drove it down, and drove it down, with the sun's oldest working blazing in front of him and the Deep's last strength pouring through him from the bottom of the world, and somewhere behind him Mira was saying his name and he could not hear her properly over the roaring. And on the seventh blow, or the tenth, or the twentieth, he felt one of the bars bend, the way an iron bar bends in a forge when the smith keeps hammering. And then crack. And then go out, like a candle.

And then the next.

He broke four. A gap wide enough for a boat. He stood in the bow with the sea falling back round him in a hot grey rain and the steam rolling off the water and his face, he knew without seeing it, lit from beneath with a cold blue light, and said, in a voice he did not entirely recognise, "Row."

Nobody moved.

He turned his head. They were all looking at him. Jac, at the tiller. Bastien and Aren, at the oars. Hayda, in the waist, with the knife across her knees. The Sardhi brothers, very still. Mira, in the stern, with something wrapped in grey cloth in her lap and her lips pressed together. And Cassia Vell, with her whole unburned hands raised a little in front of her, as if she had been about to reach for the gate and forgotten to put them down.

He saw himself in her eyes. A man in the bow of a boat with the Tidemark across his face, up to the eye, and in the eyes themselves no grey at all. Only black water. Without bottom.

"Row," he said again. Quieter.

Bastien looked at Aren. Then he dipped his oar. And the longboat slid forward, through the steam, through the gap where four bars of the King's fire had been, into the dark of the tunnel beyond.

---

Behind them, the gate did not close. The broken bars did not mend. They had been the King's, and he had made them three hundred years ago when he was young, and nobody in all that time had broken them; and now they were broken, and the rest of the gate flickered, and steadied, and burned on, with a hole in it like a missing tooth.

"He'll have felt that," said Cassia Vell. Her voice was quite steady. She was looking at her hands. "He'll know we're here. He'll know what you did."

"Yes," said Edric.

"I was ready to do it. I'd chosen." She turned her hands over in the dark of the tunnel, back and front, as if she did not know what to do with them. "You took that from me."

He did not answer. He was standing in the bow, with the cold in him so deep that it hurt, and the salt so strong on his tongue that he could taste nothing else, and somewhere at the bottom of the world, at the end of seven chains, the Deep was whispering. Not *little one*. Not *interesting*. Something lower, and slower, and sick, the way a fever talks.

*Drown him,* it said. *Drown him, drown him, drown them all.*

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. It came very hard.

Ahead of them, the gold glow grew brighter, and the walls of the tunnel opened out.

### Later

"Edric," said Aurelian, Eternal, from across the dead grey water, and then stopped smiling. "Oh. Oh, look at you. You've been drinking too."

He had. The King felt it at once, the way one drunkard smells wine on another, and Edric saw something cross the golden face that he had not seen there even in the Sunspire. Not fear. Kinship.

It made the next part harder. When the King raised his hand, the sunfire did not knock him flat; it struck him and he staggered and stayed on his feet, the sea in him flaring black against the gold, and on the ledges all round the cave the Hundred cried out and covered their eyes. When Cassia Vell stepped up beside him and raised her own two whole hands against her King, her fire did not falter after a single moment. It held. It held for a long time, gold against gold, his and hers, and the Hundred watched their High Radiant stand on the ledge with both hands raised and lowered their own, one after another, until only a handful were still burning.

But when Edric knelt at the edge of the pool and put his hand into the water, and the clouded eye opened in the dark, the Deep did not say *you came back*.

It said: *You drank me. At the gate. While I was dying.*

*I know.*

*Then you know what I want. You have tasted it.* The whisper had a thread of fever in it that had not been there before. *Take the rest. Take all of me. And let it come.*

And later, when the chains were broken and the sea was free and the wave was standing up on every coast of the world, waiting, it was not the sea alone that said *now* inside him. Part of it was his own voice. He could hear the difference. He was not sure, for one long terrible moment, which part was which.

Cassia Vell lived, after. She kept her hands. She used them, for the rest of her life, to hold the hands of novices and teach them the true histories of the Drying; and once a year, on Cristal, at the Midsummer lanterns, to set a candle on the black water for a man who had sung in a temple. She never called down sunfire again.

She never told Edric she had forgiven him for the gate. He never asked.

## Mira and the Weir-folk sing it open

*Heart + · Tide 0 · Requires Ysolde's crown, a high Heart and a low Tide · Opens the way to the secret ending*

Edric looked at her for a long time, at her plain square face, and her pale eyes, steady in the gold glow coming up out of the water.

"Wait," said Mira.

She was standing up in the stern, beside Jac. She had been sitting very still since they came into the dead water, with her grey shawl round her shoulders and her eyes half closed, listening. Now her eyes were open, and she was looking not at the gate but back, over the stern, the way they had come.

"Wait," she said again. "Not yet. They're nearly here."

Edric turned.

Out on the dead grey sea, a mile behind them, there were lights. Small and gold and wavering, very low on the water, a dozen of them. Then two dozen. Then more. Coming on slowly, across the flat dead calm, with no sound at all but the faint creak and splash of oars.

Lamps. Little oil lamps, set in the bows and on the thwarts of fishing boats, the way the Weir-folk set them out on Midsummer night to light the way for the souls of the drowned.

---

He had not known they were coming.

He had gone ashore at Saltmere alone, on the seventh day, and walked through the silent town to the Weir, to the end of the walkway, and found Mira sitting there with her feet in the grey water. He had told her everything. She had said she was coming with him, and he had said no, and she had come anyway.

He had not known that she had spent the next five nights going from landing stage to landing stage along the half-empty walkways, to every Weir-folk family who had not gone west, with her grandmother's crown wrapped in grey cloth under her arm. That she had sat with them, with their feet in the water, and told them what was happening at the bottom of the world. That she had said to them, in the Weir-tongue, *the sea is dying, and it's crying, and you can hear it, I know you can. Gran said one day it would agree, if we asked it nicely. Well. It's time to ask.*

He had not known that four fishing boats had followed the *Faro* out of Saltmere on the morning tide, a long way behind, with no lights, and then eight, and then more, and that the old Weir-folk at the oars had found their way across three hundred miles of dead sea in the dark the way their grandmothers had found it, by listening.

"Forty-one of them," said Mira. "I counted, at Saltmere." She was looking at the lights. "There'll be more from the coast villages. They heard."

The boats came up out of the dark round the longboat and stopped, a little way off, in a wide ragged half-circle facing the gate: battered grey fishing boats with patched sails furled and lamps in their bows, and in them, old men and old women, and a few young ones, and children, in their brown fishing clothes with their shawls round their shoulders. Weir-folk. Edric knew some of the faces. Old men who had sat on the landing stages when he was a boy, mending nets. A woman who had danced at the Feast of Lamps, twenty years ago, the night before his wedding that never happened. They looked at him. They looked at the gate. Nobody spoke.

Cassia Vell, standing in the bow with her big scarred hands half raised, slowly lowered them.

"What is this?" she said.

"A wedding," said Mira. "Of a kind."

---

She took off her boots. She sat down on the edge of the stern, beside Jac, and put her feet over the side into the dead grey water, the oldest way of sitting, and all round them in the fishing boats, the Weir-folk did the same: forty pairs of bare feet, old and young, going into the sea.

Then Mira unwrapped the crown.

She did not lay it on the water. She held it in her lap, in both hands, the circlet of woven sea-lavender faded to the colour of dust, with its small white shells, two of them missing. And she began to sing.

It was not the wedding song. Edric knew the wedding song; he had heard it once, a lifetime ago. This was something older and simpler, only a few notes, going round and round, rising a little and falling. A lamp song. The one the Weir-folk sang on Midsummer night, standing on the walkways with their candles, as the little lights drifted out on the black water. He had heard it every summer of his childhood and never understood a word of it.

He understood it now. Faro had taught him the old tongue in a cell, and the Deep had spoken it to him in the dark, and he understood it the way you understand a voice you have known all your life.

*Come home,* the song said. *Come home, you who were taken. The water remembers you. Come home.*

And all round the longboat, in the fishing boats, one by one, the Weir-folk took it up. Old cracked voices and young clear ones. Not loud. They did not need to be loud. Forty voices, then fifty, as the last boats came up out of the dark, singing very softly across the dead grey water to the burning gate, the way you sing to a child who has been crying for a long time and has finally gone quiet.

*Come home.*

---

At first nothing happened.

The bars of the sea-gate burned on, gold and white and fierce, a dozen of them, a hand's width apart, from the top of the arch down into the water. The heat came off them like the mouth of an oven. The dead water steamed.

And then Edric felt it.

Not in the gate. In the water under it. Under all of them. Very faint, very far, at the bottom of the world, at the end of seven chains, something that had almost stopped breathing drew a breath. Small. Shallow. Like a sleeper hearing their name called from a long way off, and turning, a little, towards the sound.

And the gate heard it too.

He saw it, before anyone else did. One of the bars, near the waterline, the one Cassia had been going to take hold of, at the join. The gold light in it was moving. Not burning. *Moving*. Running down it, from the top of the arch to the water, the way rain runs down a window. And where it ran, the light went thin, and clear, and silver.

*He made them out of what he drank,* Edric thought, and it came to him all at once, the way the answer to a sum comes after you have stopped trying. *The chains. The gate. The fire in the Hundred's hands. All of it. Three hundred years of the sea's life, stolen and burned into sunfire. That's what the gate is made of. That's what it's always been made of.*

*And it's hearing its name.*

*Come home,* sang the Weir-folk. *The water remembers you.*

The bar near the waterline shivered, and ran, and was not fire any more. It was water. A single thin bright stream of it, falling from the top of the arch into the sea with a sound like a spring in the hills.

Then the bar beside it.

Then the next. One after another, all along the gate, the bars of the King's oldest working let go of the shape he had forced on them three hundred years ago, and remembered what they were, and fell. Not with a hiss. Not with a roar. With a long soft rushing sound, like a summer rain beginning. Until where the sea-gate had been there was only a curtain of falling water across the mouth of the arch, clear and silver and glittering in the lamplight, and the sound of it falling, and the smell of it, which was not lightning any more, but rain.

The Weir-folk sang.

Cassia Vell sat down in the bottom of the boat, quite suddenly, as if her legs had gone. She was looking at her hands. They were whole. There was not a mark on them.

"Twenty-two years," she said, in a strange voice. "I know his workings. I know where every join is. I've studied every thread he ever wove." She looked up at the falling water. "Nobody can open that gate but him. Nobody."

"Nobody did," said Mira, without stopping singing. She said it between one note and the next, very softly. "It wanted to go home."

---

They went through the water. All of them.

The longboat first, with Bastien and Aren at the oars, under the falling curtain; and it was warm. That was the thing Edric would remember. It came down on them like a summer shower, soft and warm, the warmth of something that had been fire a moment ago and was glad not to be, and ran down his face and his neck and into his collar, and for a moment, under the arch, in the falling water, he thought he could hear voices in it. Very faint. Very many. A white city. People standing in the sea with their hands raised. A girl of nine in a fishing village in the far north.

*Home,* they said.

Then they were through, into the dark of the tunnel, and behind them, one by one, the Weir-folk's fishing boats slid under the curtain with their lamps in their bows, still singing, and came after them into the rock.

He looked back, once. The falling water was thinning. Slowing. Dwindling to a few bright threads across the arch, and then to drops, and then to nothing. Where the King's gate had stood for three hundred years, there was only an open arch, and beyond it, the dead grey sea, and on the sea, beginning very faintly to move for the first time in weeks, a ripple.

He reached for the sea in himself. It was the same trickle it had been at Saltmere: a breath, a drop, almost nothing. He had not spent a single drop of it. He had not had to.

But down at the bottom of the world, at the end of the chains, something very faint and very old had turned its clouded eye towards the tunnel, and the lamps, and the voices, and was listening.

### Later

They came out of the tunnel into the blazing light of the temple, and the King turned from the pool, golden, and smiled at the longboat in the tunnel mouth, at the thin grey tired man standing in its bow.

And then his eyes went past it. To the boats coming out of the dark behind. One, then four, then a dozen, grey and battered and patched, with little oil lamps in their bows, crowded with old men and women and children in brown fishing clothes, singing softly in a language nobody in the Sunspire had spoken in three hundred years.

For the first time, Aurelian, Eternal, did not know what he was looking at.

"Edric," he said. His voice filled the cave like music, but there was a question in it. "You came. And you've brought—" He stopped. "Who are these?"

"The Weir-folk of Saltmere," said Edric. "They heard the sea crying. They came to see what was wrong."

The Hundred, on the ledges, with sunfire in their hands, were looking at the boats too. At the lamps. At the children. Some of them, the oldest, were looking at the open arch of the sea-gate behind the boats, where the King's bars had been and were not. One of them lowered his hands, very slowly, before anyone had told him to.

And in the stern of the longboat, Mira Solen sat with her feet over the side in the dead grey water, and her grandmother's crown in her lap, and went on singing, very softly, and did not look at the King at all.
