# Event 31: The King's Offer

## Pretend to accept

*Heart 0 · Tide ++*

"Join me," said Aurelian.

Edric looked at him.

"I mean it," said the King. "Truly. I've been alone up here for three hundred years. I'm so tired of it. And you're the first person in all that time who could understand. Who could feel what I feel." He gestured at the well, the chains, the light. "There's enough for two. More than enough. You could drink too. You'd never age. You'd never die. You'd have Solace, and Valemont, and whatever else you liked." His golden eyes were very gentle. "And you'd never be lonely again. Neither of us would."

Edric looked at the white cup on the little table. At the light coming up out of the well. At the chains, shuddering. *Boom.*

He thought about an old man in a cell, raising a single drop of water, and weeping.

And then he thought about the distance between this room and the Teeth. Three hundred miles of sea. A cave under a black rock, with a gate of fire across its mouth that nothing could open but the King. A temple full of light, and seven chains going down into the dark, and at the end of them something vast that had been waiting for three hundred years. He thought about how empty he was. He had felt it coming up the shaft, with the sunfire taking the skin off him: half of what he had been, less. He could not fight this man. Not here, in his house, in his light. Perhaps not anywhere.

But a man who has been lied to for fourteen years learns how lying is done.

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. He reached down, past the cold and the emptiness, for the last of the stillness in him, the thing Faro had called Still Water, and drew it up over his face like a sheet drawn over a mirror. Not a mask. He had no strength left for a mask. Only a stillness, so that the surface showed nothing but what the man looking at it expected to see.

And what the King expected to see, Edric understood, was a tired man. A lonely man. A man who had come a very long way, and done terrible things, and found at the end of them that he did not know what he was for.

It was not hard to show him that. It was very nearly true.

"You'd stop," said Edric slowly. "Hunting them. The Tidewarden children. The ones who come to the archives."

"If you asked me to." Aurelian leaned forward in his white chair. "I'd have no need, would I? With you beside me. You'd know them before they came. You'd feel them, the way you feel the sea."

"And the blight? The fish?"

"We'd drink carefully. Two of us, careful, instead of one of me, bored." The King smiled. "I've been careless, Edric. I admit it. Loneliness makes a man careless. You'd make me better."

Edric let the silence go on for a long time. He let his eyes go to the cup, and away, and back. He let his hands, which had been fists, open.

"Yes," he said.

---

The King did not move at once. He sat in his white chair in the gold light and looked at Edric with an expression that it took Edric a long moment to name, because he had not expected to see it on that face. It was hope. Plain, frightened, human hope, like a child's.

Then it was gone, and he was only gracious again.

"I can't read you, you know," said Aurelian. "Not the way I read the others. You're all water, underneath. Still water, at the moment. Very still." He picked up the white cup from the table, and turned it in his long golden fingers. "So you'll forgive me if I ask for something more than a word. Words are cheap, in Solace. I've bought a great many."

He dipped the cup into the light coming up out of the well, the way a man dips a cup into a stream, and held it out.

By the shaft, Cassia Vell made a small sound. Edric did not look at her.

He took the cup.

It was warm. The light in it was not water and not fire. It moved like honey, slow and heavy and gold, and it gave off a faint sweet smell like summer grass, like bread from an oven, like the skin of someone you love. He looked down into it and felt, through his fingers, through the porcelain, the same faint tug he had felt every time the King drank: a pull, a draining, running down the chains three hundred miles into the dark. Only this time the pull went through his own hand.

*It's only once,* he told himself. *Once, to get to the Lock. Once, and then never.*

He drank.

---

It was sweet.

That was the worst of it, and he would remember it for the rest of his life. It was the sweetest thing he had ever tasted. It went down his throat like warm wine, and spread out through his chest, and everywhere it went the cold went out of him: the cold he had carried since the night under the Teeth, the cold that had been in his blood and his bones for six years. For a single heartbeat he was warm all the way through, the way he had been warm at nineteen, on the deck of the *Gull's Promise* off the Ember Coast, with the sun on his back and his whole life in front of him.

And then the taste changed, and under the sweetness there was salt.

Salt, and a memory that was not his. A white city on an island, burning. People standing in the sea up to their waists with their hands raised. A girl of nine in a fishing village in the far north, waking up screaming. He tasted them. He was drinking them.

And far away, at the bottom of the world, something flinched.

Not from the King. From him.

*Little one,* said a voice, very small, very far, like a man calling out in his sleep. And then nothing.

He lowered the cup. His hand was perfectly steady. He had made it be.

"Well?" said Aurelian softly.

"It's sweet," said Edric.

"Yes." The King's eyes were bright. "Yes, it is. Nobody ever tells you that. Three hundred years, and it's still sweet." He took the empty cup back, and set it down beside the teapot, very gently, as if it were precious. "And look at you."

Edric looked down at his hands.

The Tidemark had come back. He had stood in the sea under the Pale Cliffs for half the night and torn it out of himself like veins, and now, in the space of a single swallow, it had come flooding back down his arms, past the elbow, to the wrists. He felt it on his throat. He felt it climbing his jaw, cold and branching, towards his cheek. The sea, rising in him to meet what he had stolen from it. The cold came back with it, and the salt, and under them both, the sweetness, lying on his tongue like a coin.

*The moment it becomes sweet,* Faro had said, in a cell under the sea. *The moment you start to enjoy it.*

"It does that," said the King, watching the lines spread. "The first time. The sea in you rises to meet it. It'll settle." He stood up from his chair, and came round the well, and put his golden hand on Edric's shoulder. It was warm. It was the first time anyone had touched him in a month. "Oh, Edric. I'm so glad."

---

They talked for an hour more. Afterwards, Edric could remember almost none of it. He remembered the King pouring tea and drinking it, with great pleasure, like a man who had forgotten that tea was for sharing. He remembered being told about the weaving: that there was no sharing the drinking from a white chair in Solace without first being bound into the Lock, at the Teeth, at the dark of the moon, by the King's own hand. *Seven chains,* Aurelian said, almost shyly. *I made seven, when I was young. I'll make an eighth. For you. From your heart to the Deep's.* He remembered the King saying it as another man might talk of a wedding.

And he remembered the fleet.

"I sent it this morning," said Aurelian, apologetically, as if confessing to a small discourtesy. "To your island. Before I'd met you. I'm afraid I can't call it back; my towers don't reach the open sea." He held up a hand before Edric could speak. "Your people will be safe, if they don't fight. I'll send word after the fleet by Cassia's cutter: no one is to be harmed who lays down arms. But the books must burn. You understand. A library of Tidecraft, on an island that listens to the sea? I can't allow it, Edric. Not even for you. And you won't need them now."

Edric thought about the library at the top of the citadel. The shelves glowing in the firelight. The bed in the corner, and the girl lying on it with her hands folded on her breast, perfectly still. *Eleven days.*

"There's someone on the island I left in my care," he said. "Asleep. I have to wake her, or she'll never wake. Let me go to Cristal. Let me bring my people off before the fleet arrives. I'll bring them to Solace. And then I'll come to you. At the Teeth. At the dark of the moon."

The King considered him for a long moment, with his golden head on one side.

Then he took a ring from his finger: a plain band of gold, very old, with a sun cut into it, worn almost smooth.

"Show this at the sea-gate," he said, "and it will open for you. No one else in three hundred years has carried it." He folded Edric's fingers over it with his own warm ones. "Don't make me sorry, Edric. I've been sorry so many times."

---

Cassia Vell took him down the shaft.

She did not look at him, all the way down. She stood in the column of burning light with her face turned to the white stone and her hand on her sword, and the sunfire scalded him, and the Tidemark flared, and this time, he noticed, it hurt less. He knew why. He did not let himself think about it.

At the bottom, she laid her hand on the stone, and the door remembered itself. She did not step aside.

"You drank," she said.

He looked at her. Her plain square face had closed like a shutter. The doubt he had felt in her that morning, stirring at the bottom of the well, had gone back down into the dark. He could feel it going. He had done that.

"Ask me again," he said, very low, "at the Teeth."

"I won't need to," said Cassia Vell. "I saw your face." She stepped aside, into the light of noon, and let him pass, and did not look at him again. "Twenty-two years I've knelt to him, Warden. At least I never pretended he wasn't a god."

He went down through the city to the harbour below the cliffs at a run, with the sun on his back like a hand. The taste was still in his mouth. He spat, twice, on the steps, and it stayed. Salt, and honey, and a girl of nine in the far north, screaming.

When he stood at the bow of the *Faro* that afternoon, with the whole of the western sea in front of him, and reached down for the water beneath her keel, it came to him faster than it had come in a month: eager, cold, full. More than he had given back under the Pale Cliffs. More than he had ever had.

He did not ask himself where it had come from. He knew.

### Later

The sea-gate opened for the ring.

He held it up in the bow of the longboat, in the dark of the moon, with the bars of gold light a dozen feet away and the heat of them on his face, and the bars bent apart for him like reeds in a current. Not a hiss. Not a roar. Only a long, low, musical hum, like a struck glass. Jac, at the tiller, swore under her breath. Cassia Vell, in the waist, in her white and gold armour, said nothing. She had said nothing to him all the way from Cristal. Her hands, folded in her lap, were whole.

The King was waiting on the lowest ledge.

He was alone, or nearly. Twelve of the Hundred stood on the ledges behind him, old men in white and gold with their hands at their sides, not raised. "I sent the others home," said Aurelian, smiling, as the boat grounded. "We don't need them, you and I. I wanted it to be quiet." The chains went down into the black water beside him, taut and shuddering. The water was black, not grey. The fish in the Reach were thin but living. He had never had to drain the sea dry. He had never had to. He had thought he had an heir.

"Come," said the King, and knelt at the edge of the pool, and held out his golden hand. "Kneel with me. It doesn't hurt. Not much."

Edric knelt beside him. He put his hands into the light where the chains entered the water, beside the King's. He felt the drinking begin, the sweet pull, the coin on his tongue. He felt the King feel him, and relax.

And he reached down past the chains, into the dark, as far as he could reach.

*Give it to me,* he said. *All of it. Now.*

The answer did not come at once. That was the cost, and he had known it would be. In the dark at the bottom of the world, something very old turned slowly towards him, and he felt it remember the taste of him, from a white cup in a tower.

*You drank me, little one,* said the Deep. *In his house. I felt your mouth.*

*I know. I'm sorry. I'll give it back. All of it. Every drop.*

A long silence. Beside him, the King's head was turning. The golden face was changing, slowly, from love to understanding.

*Interesting,* said the Deep at last, very quietly. *We will see.*

And the sea came up into him, and Aurelian, Eternal, who had not been surprised by anything in a hundred and forty years, was surprised for the second time that season, and for the last.

## Accept for real

*Heart − · Tide +++ · Ending: The Twin Kings*

"Join me," said Aurelian.

Edric looked at him.

"I mean it," said the King. "Truly. I've been alone up here for three hundred years. I'm so tired of it. And you're the first person in all that time who could understand. Who could feel what I feel." He gestured at the well, the chains, the light. "There's enough for two. More than enough. You could drink too. You'd never age. You'd never die. You'd have Solace, and Valemont, and whatever else you liked. Morrow's army. Quell's Treasury. Valtor's courts. Your island. Anything." His golden eyes were very gentle. "And you'd never be lonely again. Neither of us would."

Edric looked at the white cup on the little table. At the light coming up out of the well. At the chains, shuddering. *Boom.*

He tried to think of the old man in the cell. The drop of water rising off the stone, and Faro weeping. He reached for it the way he had reached for it on a hundred bad nights, and found it, and held it up in front of himself.

And found that he could not see it clearly any more. There was something in the way. A small boy on the floor of a stillroom, with a wooden horse in his hand.

He had done that. Not the King. He had stood in a garden on the Street of Lanterns with the sea in him and told a frightened woman how blackwater worked, because he wanted to see what she would do. And the sea in him had enjoyed it. He had looked into a cracked mirror and seen it looking back out of his own face, smiling. And a month ago, in the storm, he had felt it pour through him into the sky over Solace and very nearly not stop. *Drown them all.* He had heard it. He had wanted it.

*I'm not a tyrant,* the King had said. *I'm a dam.*

He looked at the chains, and for the first time since Faro had shown them to him from the hole in the temple roof, twelve years ago, he did not see a crime. He saw a wall. A wall between the land and the thing he had felt in himself in the stillroom. And a man who had stood behind it alone for three hundred years, holding it up, so that nobody else would ever have to look at what was on the other side.

He was so tired. He had been tired since the Keep. He had been angry for nineteen years, and the anger had carried him like a current, and now the anger was spent, and the current had set him down here, at the top of a white tower, in front of the only person in the world who knew what it was to carry the sea and be afraid of it.

"If I said yes," he said slowly. "The fish. The blight. The children with the blight-cough."

"We'd drink carefully," said Aurelian. "Two of us, careful, instead of one of me, bored. You'd make me better, Edric. I know you would."

He believed it. Edric could feel that he believed it. No ripples. No lie.

"Yes," said Edric Vane.

---

The King wept.

He did it quite quietly, sitting in his white chair, with his golden hands in his lap and the tears running down his beautiful face, and he did not seem embarrassed by it at all. "Forgive me," he said. "It's been so long since anyone said yes to me who wasn't afraid." Then he wiped his eyes, and laughed, and reached for the teapot, and poured, and this time Edric took the cup and drank the tea, which was very good, and very old, and tasted of smoke.

Then the King dipped the white cup in the light, and held it out.

By the shaft, Cassia Vell said, "Majesty," in a voice like something breaking. Neither of them looked at her.

Edric drank.

It was sweet. He had not expected that. It went down his throat like warm wine, and spread through his chest, and everywhere it went, the cold went out of him: the cold he had carried for six years, in his blood and his bones, since the night the Deep took him under the Teeth. He felt the Tidemark on his arms prickle, and fade, and go out, like frost on a window when the sun comes round to it. He felt warm. All the way through. For the first time since he was nineteen.

Underneath the sweetness there was salt. Underneath the salt there were memories that were not his. A white city burning. People standing in the sea with their hands raised. Children coughing in their beds.

And at the bottom of the world, something flinched. And a voice, very small, very far, said: *Little one?*

He held out the cup.

The King filled it again.

---

Afterwards he could not have said, if anyone had asked him, exactly when he stopped hearing the Deep. It was not that first afternoon. That first afternoon he heard it every time he drank: *little one, little one*, smaller each time, like someone calling from the far side of a widening river. It was not the second day, when he sat in the white chair beside the King's and they talked until the stars came out over the sea, about everything, about Faro and the Drying and the eleven Tidewardens and the earthquake at Port Amber, and he laughed out loud at something the King said, and was startled by the sound. Perhaps it was the third day. Perhaps the fourth. Only at some point the calling stopped, and he noticed the silence, the way you notice a clock has stopped, and drank, and stopped noticing.

The *Faro* sailed at sunset on the first day, as he had ordered, with Hayda and Bastien and Maxim Moren and the old man in his chair. He felt her go. He stood at the glass wall of the great round room and watched her clear the harbour below the cliffs and turn west, a long black ship with a curling wave on her bow, getting smaller.

"You could call them back," said the King, beside him. "I'd find them places. The girl especially. A princess of Qasr. I'd like to meet her."

"No," said Edric. "Let them go."

He did not want Hayda to see him. That was the truth. He did not want her to look at him with her desert-well eyes and see what she would see.

The fleet sailed on. He asked the King to call it back, once, and the King explained, gently, that he could not: his towers did not reach the open sea. "But I'll send word after it, by Cassia's cutter. No one is to be harmed who lays down their arms. Your people will live, Edric. I promise you that." He paused. "The books must burn. You understand. Not for my sake. For theirs. A city full of people who listen to the sea, and a library that teaches them how to listen? It ends one way. With someone like you. Someone like me." He smiled. "We're the last, you and I. Let's let it end with us."

Edric looked out at the sea for a long time.

"Yes," he said. "Let it end with us."

---

The thread beside his heart went out on the fortieth day.

He felt it begin to go in the morning: a faint fluttering, beside his heart, like a moth against a window. He was sitting in the white chair. He put the cup down. He closed his eyes and reached for it, down the long bright line of it, three hundred miles west across the sea to a library at the top of a citadel, where a girl lay on a bed in the corner with her hands folded on her breast, and Jac Teague sat beside her, with a mirror in her hand, not using it.

He knew how it was done. *Let the Warden be the tide that comes in. Let him move, and let the other's waters remember how to move with him.* He reached down into himself for the tide.

There was no tide. There was gold. Warm, still, golden light, filling him from the bottom to the brim, the way the light filled the well at his feet. It did not move. It did not need to. It had never needed to move in three hundred years. He reached through it for the small warm thing at the bottom, the thing that had once laughed at a heron from a landing stage, and could not find the bottom. There was only gold, all the way down.

The thread fluttered, and fluttered, and went still.

And then it was not there.

He sat in the white chair with his eyes closed. Beside him, the King said nothing at all. After a long time, Aurelian reached over, very gently, and dipped the white cup in the light, and put it into Edric's hand, and folded his fingers round it.

"It helps," said the King. "I promise. It's what it's for."

Edric drank.

It helped.

---

At the dark of the moon they went to the Teeth together, in the King's own ship, with the Hundred, and the King opened the sea-gate with a word and took him into the temple, where the chains went down into the black water, blazing. Edric had last seen it from a ledge high up the wall, with an old man beside him weeping. He looked up and saw the rope still hanging from the hole in the roof, plaited from two prisoners' blankets, rotted now to a few grey threads.

"Is that where you came in?" said the King, following his gaze. "The old man's tunnel? How extraordinary. Six years he must have dug." He shook his head. "He was very stubborn. You'd have liked each other."

"We did," said Edric.

He knelt at the edge of the pool, where the King told him to kneel, and the King knelt behind him and put both golden hands flat between his shoulder blades. And the Hundred raised their hands on the ledges all round the cave, a hundred small suns, and Aurelian, Eternal, made the eighth chain.

It came out of Edric's heart. That was how it felt. A line of gold light, running out of his chest and down into the black water, down and down, three hundred miles, to the place at the bottom of the world where something vast lay bound in the dark. He felt it go in. He felt it take hold.

And at the end of it, for one last moment, he felt the Deep. Not calling now. Not anything. Only an eye, very large and very dim, opening slowly in the dark, and looking up the new chain at the man on the end of it. Looking for a long time.

It did not say *interesting*.

It closed.

---

Hayda came to Solace in the spring.

She came alone, by the coast road, in plain Sardhi travelling clothes, with Bastien's long knife at her belt, and she stood at the foot of the Sunspire for three days, in the sun and the rain, until the door that only a Radiant's hand could open opened, and Edric came out of it.

He had not aged. He would never age now. But he had changed. His hair, which had been dark going grey, was gold at the roots. His skin was warm and smooth, and the white scar through his eyebrow had faded almost to nothing. The Tidemark was gone. His eyes were not grey any more. They were the colour of light through honey.

She looked at him for a long time.

"Cristal is gone," she said. "The library. The citadel. The terraces. They burned the books on the quay, in a pile, for a day and a night. Tam stood in front of them, and they burned him too. They let the rest of us go. We are in Corrow now, mostly. Jac has taken the *Slip* and gone south." She did not move. "Lyse Valtor died on the fortieth day, in the library, in Jac's arms. Before the fleet came. Maxim was on the quay, watching for you. He watched for three days after. Then he walked into the sea."

"I know," said Edric. "I felt it."

"You felt it," said Hayda bint Tariq.

"I'm sorry."

"Yes. I can see that you are." Her desert-well eyes moved over his golden face, slowly, the way she had once looked at a room in Solace to learn where everything was kept. "I came to ask you one thing. You told me, on the deck of the *Slip*, in Mazrah, that I was free. That I was no one's. You gave me a paper." She took it out of her coat: old, folded, soft at the creases. "I have kept it. I wanted to know, before I go home to the Sardh. Whose are you?"

He did not answer.

She waited. Then she put the paper back in her coat, and turned, and walked away down the white street towards the coast road and the east, and did not look back. He stood at the foot of the tower and watched her go until he could not see her. Then he went back inside, and the door forgot it was a door.

---

They called them the Twin Kings, afterwards, in Valemont. Aurelian and the Heir. The old gold and the new.

He was good at it. He found that out in the first year and went on finding it out for many years after. He took the Three Hands' empty places one by one, and filled them better than they had ever been filled: the Treasury, where he could feel the money moving like a tide; the courts, where he could feel a lie the way a man feels a draught; the Order, which was frightened of him, and obeyed. He abolished the Sun Warrants. He opened the King's granaries in the bad winters. He hanged no one who did not deserve it, and many who did he let go. In Solace they said the Heir was merciful. They said it the way people say it of a storm that passes to the north.

The fish grew thinner. Two drank where one had drunk, and drank carefully, and still the fish grew thinner, and the weed died along the coast, and the blighted things came ashore more often, and the beacons burned all night. He knew. He felt it, every time he dipped the cup: the faint flinch, very far away, at the bottom of the world. He had stopped hearing anything else.

He thought about Mira sometimes. Not often. In the early years he would feel her, now and then, through the sea: the faintest flicker on the line, the way a fisherman feels a fish brush the bait without taking it. A woman on the end of a walkway in the Weir, with her feet in the grey water, listening. He never went to Saltmere. He did not want her to see him either.

He felt her die.

It was a spring tide, a full moon, forty-one years after he had first sat in the white chair. He was sitting in it now, in the gold light, with the King asleep in the chair beside his, the way the old one slept these days, with his eyes open. And very far away, through the eighth chain, down into the sea and up again, he felt something touch the water at the end of a walkway in the Weir. A circle of woven sea-lavender, dry as dust, threaded with small white shells, laid on the grey surface by an old woman's hand. Floating. Asking.

He did not hear the song. He could not hear songs any more.

He put down the cup and sat very still for a long time, with the light coming up out of the well, and tried to remember the colour of her eyes. Green. He knew they had been green. He knew it the way he knew the sum of a column of figures. He could not see them.

Then the King stirred in his sleep, and murmured something in the old tongue, and Edric picked up the cup again.

---

The girl came up the shaft on a bright cold noon in autumn, in the sixty-third year of the Twin Kings.

She was perhaps twenty. She had been caught in the archives under the Sunspire at night, three days before, with a candle and a stolen key and a copy, in her own hand, of a page from a book that no longer existed. She was from Corrow, she said. Her grandmother had been a girl on Cristal, once, before the fire. Her grandmother had learned her letters from a schoolmaster called Tam, who had died in front of the books.

She came up the shaft in the burning gold, and it hurt her, Edric could see that it hurt her, and she set her teeth and did not cry out. And she stepped off onto the white floor of the great round room, and looked at the two golden men in their white chairs beside the well, and the seven chains, and the eighth, and did not lower her eyes.

The old King did not wake. He hardly woke at all now.

So Edric stood up, and smiled at her.

"You must be the one who's been in my archives," he said. "Do sit down. Would you like some tea?"

She did not sit. She looked past him at the chains coming up out of the well, taut and gold and shuddering, very faintly, every few heartbeats. Weaker than they had been, once. Much weaker.

"I'm going to break those," she said.

Edric laughed.

It was a beautiful laugh. He heard it go round the room of glass like music, and heard, somewhere very deep under it, something else, small and far away and old, like a man calling out in his sleep. He did not know what it was. He had not known for a long time.

"Oh," he said, wiping his eyes. "Oh, forgive me. It's only that you all say that. Every one." He sat down again in his white chair, and picked up the white cup, and dipped it, gently, into the light. "I'm not a tyrant, you know. I'm a dam. Let me tell you what's on the other side."

He drank.

Three hundred miles away, at the bottom of the world, something vast flinched in the dark.

And Edric Vane, who had once laughed at a heron from a landing stage in the Weir, and had not been surprised by anything in sixty years, looked at the girl with the grey eyes standing very straight in front of him, and found that he was bored.

*THE END*
