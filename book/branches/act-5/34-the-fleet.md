# Event 34: The Fleet

## Lift the fleet and keep the flagship's mirrors

*Heart − · Tide 0*

It carried them. Twelve white ships, riding the back of the sea like leaves on a river in flood, out past the reefs, out past the harbour mouth, out into the dark. He felt them go. He felt the men on their decks clinging to the rails, to the masts, to each other. He felt the great mirrors on their swivels, swinging, tilting, unbalanced, and he reached up with the sea to fill them with salt and crack them, every one.

And stopped, with the water already curling over the bows.

He could feel the flagship's mirror. It was the biggest of them: twenty feet across, not twelve, a great shallow bowl of silvered bronze on a swivel of brass as thick as a man's waist. It had been drinking the sun since dawn. It had burned the harbour tower and the shore walls and the Weir-folk's stilt-houses, and at an hour past noon it had swung round to find the citadel and found the third terrace in its way. It was dark now. But it was not empty. He could feel it, even through the sea: a faint golden warmth still lying in the curve of the bronze, like the heat in a stone after sunset. The last of the day's light, gathered and held. Waiting for someone to pour it out.

A weapon. The only weapon in the world, perhaps, that had ever been made to carry the King's own fire across a distance and put it where you wanted it.

He thought about the Teeth. A gate of sunfire across a cave mouth. A temple full of light. A golden man at the edge of a pool, with a hundred old knights around him, and the Deep at the bottom of the chains. He thought about how empty he was, how empty he would be by the time he got there. He thought about sunfire against sunfire.

The sea came over the bows of eleven white ships in long green walls of water, and struck their mirrors, and cracked them, and filled them with salt, and they went dark forever.

The twelfth, it did not touch.

---

He set eleven of them down on the Long Bank, as gently as he could, on the soft pale sand, and let the swell sink away beneath them, and they settled in the dark with their masts at angles and their keels in the sand and two thousand men on their decks, wet and terrified and alive.

The flagship he did not set down. He carried it on, on the back of the sea, round the end of the Long Bank and back towards the island, into the harbour mouth, between the reefs, a great white ship riding a swell that came from nowhere, with two hundred knights clinging to her rails and her mirror swinging on its brass swivel, dark and whole. He brought her in past the place where the harbour tower had been and set her down on the mud at the inner end of the hook, among the blackened stumps of the hundred stilt-houses she had burned, and let her lean there, aground, with her keel in the shallows.

Then he let go.

The sea went out of him, and he heard it go, and heard it speak to him in the vast slow voice as it went: *You could have drowned them. They would have drowned you.* And then, after a long silence, something else. *You kept one.*

*Yes.*

*Why?*

He did not answer. He was lying on the cold stone at the end of the broken quay, and could not stand, and from the citadel high above came the sound of four thousand people cheering, and from the mud at the inner end of the harbour, quite close, the sound of two hundred knights in white and gold climbing down off the side of a stranded ship into the shallows, and finding Jac Teague's watch waiting for them on the shingle with spears and nets, and laying down their swords.

---

He woke on the third day, not the seventh. He had not given everything away, this time. He had kept something back.

Cassia Vell was sitting by the fire in the library, in a plain grey coat that Tam had found for her, without her armour, without her sword. She had come ashore at dawn on the first day under a white flag, Jac told him, and knelt on the shingle and surrendered the fleet, and then asked to see him, and been told he might sleep for a week, and said she would wait. She had waited. She had sat by the fire in the library for two days and two nights with her big scarred hands in her lap and watched him sleep.

She did not get up when he opened his eyes.

"You kept the *Dawnbringer*," she said.

He did not pretend not to understand. "The flagship?"

"Her mirror." Cassia looked into the fire. "Eleven ships on the Long Bank with their mirrors smashed full of salt, so that they'll never take the sun again. And the flagship aground in your harbour with hers untouched. Whole. And still warm, Warden. I went down and laid my hand on the bronze yesterday. There's half a noon still in her, from the Long Day. She was holding it for the citadel." She looked at him. "I don't think you did that by accident. I don't think you do anything by accident."

"No," said Edric.

"Then tell me why."

He sat up, slowly. Every part of him ached. "You told Jac the King's gone to the Teeth. To the Lock. To drink the sea dry. With the Hundred around him." He looked at her. "By the time I get there, I'll have almost nothing. He'll have everything. I need something to put between me and his fire, Cassia. Something that can answer it. And the only thing in the world that can answer sunfire is sunfire."

"Then you need someone who can pour it." She did not take her eyes from his face. "That mirror doesn't answer to you. It doesn't answer to anyone touched by the sea. It answers to a Radiant's hand on the bronze, and the Radiant's own fire, calling the light out of it. A trained knight. Three, for a mirror that size." She was quiet a moment. "You know that. You've had two days to think, lying there with your eyes shut. You kept the ship, and you kept her crew. Two hundred knights of the Order, sitting under guard in your warehouses, waiting to hear what you'll do with them."

"I won't make you do it," said Edric. "You've given enough."

"I know you won't make me," said Cassia Vell. "That's not what I'm afraid of."

---

He went down to the warehouses behind the burned quays that afternoon, leaning on Bastien, and stood in the door of the largest of them, where the *Dawnbringer*'s knights sat on the floor in rows in their white and gold, under Jac's guard, with their swords piled in a corner.

They were young, most of them. Younger than he had thought. Novices, half of them, a year or two out of the Order's schools, with the histories of the Drying still fresh in their heads, in the words the High Radiant had taught them. They looked up when he came in, and he felt the fear go through them, all at once, like wind through a field of barley. *The Warden.* The sea-demon's priest. The man who had lifted their fleet out of the sea in the dark and put it down on a sandbank like a cat with a kitten.

He picked three. He did it with Undertow, without letting them see; he went along the rows and felt the water in them, the fear and the faith and the doubt, and found the three in whom the doubt was strongest, and the fire. A grey-bearded knight called Ser Aubin, who had served thirty years and been passed over for the Hundred twice. A woman of about thirty called Ser Maud, whose brother had died on the stair on the Long Day. And a boy of nineteen called Anselm, with a scar through one eyebrow, white and new, who had been the *Dawnbringer*'s third mirror-hand, and had poured the fire that struck the temple on the third terrace.

He told them what he wanted. He told them it would be against the King.

Ser Aubin spat on the floor.

"You'll have to break my hands," he said, "or my head. I won't turn the Order's fire on the Sun's anointed for a drowner. Not for my life."

"I'm not asking for your life," said Edric. He looked along the rows of young frightened faces. "I'm telling you what happens to the others. You came here to burn this island. Four thousand people. You burned their houses and their harbour and a man singing in his temple. They want you hanged, the people of Cristal. All two hundred. I'm the only thing standing between you and the terraces." He let that sit. "Help me, and every one of you goes home. Refuse, and I'll stop standing there."

He did not mean it. He thought he did not mean it. He kept his face very still, so that it showed nothing but what they expected, and what they expected, from the sea-demon's priest, was that he meant it.

Ser Maud looked at him for a long time.

Then she looked at the boy beside her, Anselm, who had not lifted his head since Edric came in.

"I'll do it," she said. "Sun forgive me. I'll do it." She put her hand on the boy's shoulder. "And so will he."

---

Cassia Vell was waiting for him at the top of the processional stair, in the place where the temple had been. She had heard. Everyone on Cristal had heard by nightfall.

"You told me you wouldn't make anyone," she said.

"I didn't make them. I asked them."

"You asked them with two hundred lives behind the question." Her pale eyes held his. "I've done that, Warden. In the King's name. I know what it is. I've stood in front of a village in the south and said, *give me the man who's hiding the Tidewarden books, and none of the rest of you will burn.* I never once lit a fire, after I said it. I never had to." She looked down at the smoking hollow at her feet, and the lump of half-melted brass lying in the rubble, that had once been a sun over an altar. "That's how he works. He never has to."

He did not answer.

"You'll have your mirror," said Cassia Vell. "And they'll pour it for you, when the time comes, and it'll cost them. That much fire, from that boy's hands, against the King's? It'll burn him. You know it will." She turned away. "I came to Cristal to tell a dead man I'd tried to undo what I'd done. I'll still go with you to the Teeth. But I'll go knowing you're the kind of man who'd keep the mirror."

---

They sailed on the fourth day: the *Faro* and the *Slip*, and behind them, under a jury rig and a crew of Jac's smugglers, a great white ship with her golden suns painted over in black tar, and on her deck, on a swivel of brass as thick as a man's waist, a twenty-foot mirror, turning, all day, every day, to follow the sun. Drinking.

Three young knights stood by it, under guard, in plain grey coats. They did not speak to Edric, all the way east. Ser Maud would not look at him. The boy Anselm, with the new white scar through his eyebrow, looked at him all the time.

### Later

The *Dawnbringer* lay off the south face of the Teeth in the dark of the moon, with her bow towards the sea-gate, and her great mirror tilted on its swivel to look straight into the arch, between the burning bars, down the hundred yards of black tunnel beyond. Ten days of sun lay in the curve of the bronze. Edric could feel it from the longboat, the way you feel a banked fire across a room.

"When you see the lantern," he had told Ser Maud, "pour all of it."

He saw his chance on the lowest ledge, in the temple, with his chest on fire and his face against the salt-crusted stone, when the King turned his hand towards Cassia Vell and the sunfire took her. Hayda saw it too. She lifted the lantern from the longboat's bow and swung it, three times, towards the tunnel mouth.

Out on the dead grey sea, three people laid their hands on the bronze.

The light came down the tunnel like the sun coming up. It came through the bars of the sea-gate as if they were not there, sunfire passing sunfire, and down the black throat of the rock in a single blinding line, and burst into the temple and struck the golden figure on the far ledge full in the chest. Aurelian staggered. His fire, pouring into Cassia, broke off. For a moment, a long moment, the King of Valemont stood blazing at the edge of the pool with his own stored noon pressing him back, his beautiful face twisted, pushing against it with both hands, and on the ledges all round the cave, the Hundred turned to stare at the tunnel mouth, where the light was coming from.

It was the moment Edric needed. He crawled to the edge of the ledge, and put his hand into the dead grey water.

And out on the sea, the King pushed back.

They found the boy Anselm afterwards, at dawn, when the *Faro*'s crew rowed over to the *Dawnbringer* to take her in tow. He was lying on the deck beside the mirror, with his hands still on the bronze, burned to the wrists. Ser Maud was sitting beside him with his head in her lap. Ser Aubin was standing at the rail with his back to them, looking at the place where the Teeth had been.

The mirror had cracked across, from rim to rim. It would never take the sun again.

"He asked to be the one at the centre," said Ser Maud, without looking up. "He said he'd poured the fire on the man in the temple, so he ought to pour this. He said it would even it out." She smoothed the boy's hair back from the white scar through his eyebrow. "Does it, Warden? Does it even out?"

Edric stood on the deck of the white ship in the first light, with the sea blue around him for the first time in three hundred years, and the whales blowing a mile off, and did not know what to say.
