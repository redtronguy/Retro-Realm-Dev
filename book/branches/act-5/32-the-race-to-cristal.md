## Send the waking instructions to a Cristal child

*Heart + · Tide 0*

He went forward, to the bow, alone. He stood there with the spray coming over him, and the whole of the western sea in front of him, grey and endless, and closed his eyes, and breathed.

He was weak. The King was right; he had felt it, coming down the shaft, and he felt it now. Half of what he had been. Less. He reached for the sea beneath the *Faro*'s keel, and it came, as it always came: eager, cold, waiting. He could take hold of it. He could push, the way he had pushed on the night of the squall, and feel the ship leap, and hold her running at the edge of what her timbers could bear for ten days and nine nights, until the sea had poured back into him every drop he had given away under the Pale Cliffs and more. He could feel how it would go. He could feel the Deep feel it, at the bottom of the world, and wait.

*Ten days,* he thought. *And on the eleventh, Lyse wakes or never wakes.*

He did not push.

He stood at the bow with his hands on the rail and thought, instead, about a library. A bed in the corner by the fire. Jac in a chair beside it, with a mirror she had been told not to use. And on the floor by the hearth, where she was not supposed to be, a small girl with a book open on her knees.

Nessa Bettan was nine. She was the youngest of Old Bettan's granddaughters, the last of the swarm of children the old woman had brought west in a fishing boat in the second year, and she was the worst pupil in Tam's school, Tam said, because she would not sit still, and the best, because she read everything. She had taught herself the Weir-tongue letters out of the old books in the library before Tam had finished teaching her the common ones. She had come up the citadel stair one winter night with a lamp, two years ago, and found the Warden alone in the library, and stood in the doorway, and asked him why the sea in the cave under the island was crying.

Not singing, as it had sung when they first came. Crying. She could hear it, she said. At night, mostly, with her feet in the water. Her great-grandmother had heard it too, Gran said, and her great-great-grandmother, back and back, all the way to the old days. It was in the Weir blood, a little, in some of them. Like an ear for music.

He had told her it was only the tide in the rocks. She had looked at him with her small brown Weir-folk face and said, quite politely, that he was lying, and gone back down the stair.

He had not forgotten it.

*A drop of water off a stone,* Faro had said. *That's all any of us could do, with the Deep chained. That's all Tidecraft is, now. A drop, and an ear to hear it.*

He let go of the rail.

"Rashid," he called, over his shoulder. "Sail her. Every stitch she'll carry. Do your best."

The Sardhi looked at him from the wheel. "My lord, you said ten days."

"I know what I said. Eleven, if the gods love us. That's what you told me." He turned back to the sea. "Let's find out if they do."

---

He waited until dark.

Then he went down to the captain's cabin, and filled a bowl from the water cask, and added seawater from a bucket over the side, half and half, the way the books said, and set it on the table, and sat in front of it with his hands flat on either side.

He had scried before. He had watched Dorran Quell sit on the edge of his bed in the white marble house, and Corvan Valtor in his study, and the money of Solace turning in the dark like a shoal. But that was looking. That was Still Water: making himself a surface and letting the world fall into it. This was the other way round. He had never tried to make himself heard.

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. He went down into the stillness, past the cold, past the mud and stones and weed of what was left of him, and reached out along the thread beside his heart. The thread that went three hundred miles west across the sea to a girl on a bed in a library. He followed it, as a man follows a rope in the dark, hand over hand. And at the far end of it, beside the bed, by the fire, he felt the water in the room. The water in Jac, tired and fierce and frightened. And the water in the jug on the table, and in the wet stone of the hearth where somebody had spilled it, and in the small body on the floor with a book on its knees.

He did not push. He did not have enough to push, and if he had, it would have been the wrong thing. A tide-taken voice at full strength, coming out of the water at a child of nine, would have been a storm in her head. She would have run screaming. Or worse. He did not want to be a storm. He wanted to be what Faro had been, through the wall of a cell, on the first day: a scratching. Small and patient and persistent, so that you had to listen to hear it at all.

He made himself very small.

*Nessa,* he said, into the water.

On Cristal, three hundred miles away, by the fire in the library, a little girl looked up from her book.

---

It took him three nights to teach her to hear him properly, and four more to teach her to breathe.

She was frightened on the first night. She went and sat in Jac's lap, which she had never done before, and would not say why. On the second night she went down to the harbour after dark with a lamp, alone, to the inner end of the hook where her family's stilt-house stood in the shallow water, and sat on the edge of the landing stage with her feet in the sea, the oldest way of sitting, and listened. And on the third night, she said, out loud, to the dark water, in a small cross voice: "If it's you, Warden, then stop mumbling."

He laughed, in the cabin of the *Faro*, three hundred miles away, with his hands on either side of the bowl. Rashid, passing the door, stopped, and listened, and went on, shaking his head.

After that it was easier. He did not tell her much, at first. He did not tell her about the King, or the fleet, or the white cup. He told her what Faro had told him, in the same order, because it was the only order he knew. *Sit. Close your eyes. Breathe in, for a count of eight. Hold it for eight. Out for eight. Hold for eight. No, slower. Slower than that. Slower than you think you can.*

"It's boring," said Nessa, on the fourth night.

*It's very boring,* he agreed. *I did it for three years.*

"Three *years*?"

*I was slower than you.*

She was. Much faster. He had known she would be; she had been born with the ear, and he had had to dig his out of himself in the dark with a chisel. By the sixth night she could find the stillness and hold it for a count of a hundred, and when she did he could feel it, three hundred miles away: a small clear pool, in the middle of a noisy child, perfectly still. Like a well in a courtyard. And at the bottom of it, so bright it startled him, something warm.

On the seventh night, he told her about Lyse.

---

He told her the truth, because she would know if he did not. That the lady on the bed in the library was not dead. That he had put her to sleep, with Still Water, to save her from people who wanted to hurt her, and that she would sleep for forty days and then never wake unless someone woke her. That he had meant to be there to do it, and could not be. That the ship was too slow, and he could not make it faster without hurting himself in a way that would be very hard to undo.

"The sea," said Nessa. She was sitting on the floor of the library, by the bed, with her back against its leg. Jac had gone to sleep in the chair. "You mean the sea would get in you. More."

He was quiet a moment, in the cabin of the *Faro*.

*Yes,* he said. *How did you know that?*

"You're cold," said Nessa simply. "When you talk. Your voice is cold. Gran says the Warden goes about cold as a fish and nobody's to say anything about it." She pulled her knees up. "What do I have to do?"

*There's a book on the third shelf from the window, near the top. Green leather. Tam won't let you have it. Get it anyway.*

She got it. She climbed the shelves, the way she had been told not to a hundred times, and brought it down, and sat by the fire with it open on her knees, and found the page, and read it out loud to him, slowly, in the Weir-tongue letters that she had taught herself out of these same books. *On the Discipline of Still Water. The Waking.*

"*As the Warden stilled the waters by his own stillness,*" read Nessa Bettan, "*so he must wake them by his own motion. Let the Warden be the tide that comes in. Let him move, and let the other's waters remember how to move with him.*" She looked up. "What does that mean?"

He told her. He told her about the small warm thing at the bottom of the well, under the stillness, the thing that was not the sea and not anything anyone had given you. The thing that was only yours. That you had to find it, and take hold of it, and move. In and out, like the tide. Slowly. And keep moving, however long it took, until the other person's waters remembered how to move with you.

"What if I can't find it?"

*You found it on the sixth night,* he said. *I felt you. It's very bright.*

She was quiet for a long time.

"What if I do it wrong," she said, in a much smaller voice, "and she doesn't wake up?"

He sat in the cabin with his hands on either side of the bowl and the ship running west under him, not fast enough, and thought about lying.

*Then it won't be your fault,* he said. *It'll be mine. I'm the one who put her to sleep. I'm the one who should be there. And I'm not.* He breathed. *But I don't think you'll do it wrong. I think you've got a better heart for it than I have. I'll be there, as much as I can. On the thread. I'll be with you the whole time.*

---

On the fortieth day, the wind failed.

It went at noon, off a reef with no name a hundred miles east of the Reach, and left the *Faro* lying on a flat grey sea with her sails hanging like washing, and Rashid cursing at the wheel in three languages, and the whole crew looking forward to the bow, where Edric stood with his hands on the rail. Waiting for him to do something.

He did not do anything.

He felt the sea under the keel, waiting. He felt how easy it would be. He could have them moving again in a heartbeat. He could have them at Cristal by morning, if he poured enough of himself into it; he would arrive emptied out and filled up again with the Deep to the brim, and kneel by the bed, and reach, and perhaps there would be nothing left of him that knew how. He did not know. That was the trouble with the sea. You never knew until it was in you.

He went below. He filled the bowl. And he sat in front of it all afternoon, while the ship lay becalmed and the sun went down red into the west, and held the thread.

At dusk, three hundred miles away, a small girl climbed onto the end of a bed in a library, and sat cross-legged on the furs at Lyse Valtor's feet, and put her two hands flat on the cold white ankles because she could not reach the forehead without lying on top of her, and closed her eyes.

He felt her breathe. In for eight. Out for eight. He felt her go down into the stillness, faster than he ever had, and find the bottom, and the warm bright thing there.

He felt her take hold of it.

And he felt her move.

It was very small. A child's tide, coming in over a child's beach. He felt it go out of her hands into the cold still waters of the girl on the bed, and the waters not answer. He felt her move again. Again. In, and out. The thread beside his heart, so faint now that he could barely feel it at all, did not stir.

*Keep going,* he said into the bowl. *Keep going. Don't stop.*

She did not stop. He felt her get tired. He felt her get frightened, and the stillness wobble, and steady again. He felt her start to cry and not stop moving. In, and out. In, and out. An hour. More. He felt Jac wake in the chair and see what she was doing and come to the bed and not touch her, not say anything, only kneel beside it with her face in her one good hand.

And then the thread moved.

Not because of him. He had not done anything. It moved the way a weed moves on the bottom of a harbour when the first of the flood comes in over it, faint and slow, a little this way and a little that. He felt the cold waters in Lyse Valtor feel a tide that was not his, and remember.

Her heart, beating. Once.

A long pause. Twice.

He sat in the cabin of the becalmed ship with his hands flat on the table on either side of a bowl of water, and found that his face was wet, and did not know when that had happened.

On Cristal, in the library, a girl of nineteen took a long, shuddering, gasping breath, like a swimmer coming up from very deep water, and opened her eyes, and saw sitting on the end of her bed a small, furious, tear-streaked child with her hands on her ankles, and said, in a whisper, "Who are you?"

"Nessa," said Nessa Bettan, and burst into tears, and fell asleep, all at once, on the furs at her feet.

---

The wind came back at midnight, out of the east.

The *Faro* raised Cristal at sunset on the eleventh day. Edric saw it from the bow, with his own eyes, clearly: the island rising out of the sea, the cliffs, the terraces, the citadel with its broken dome. And smoke. A great black column of it, leaning on the wind, and under it the harbour in ruins, and the twelve white ships lying in a curve across its mouth, with their mirrors going dark as the sun went down.

He had missed the Long Day. Whatever had happened on Cristal since dawn had happened without him. He could feel it from here, the way you feel a bruise: the burned quays, the melted stone, the hundred stilt-houses gone, the places on the terraces where people had fallen. He had not been there.

But he was standing on his own two feet. He could see. And when he reached down for the sea, it came, cold and slow and faint, but his. He had not driven the ship. He had not spent himself. He had ten days of stillness in him, and the Tidemark still stopped at his throat.

"Bring her in by the sea-cave," he said to Rashid. "And then leave the rest to me."

### Later

Lyse was sitting up in the bed in the library when he came up the citadel stair, with a blanket round her shoulders and a cup of something hot in her hands, and a sleeping child curled against her side like a cat.

He stopped in the doorway. Behind him, on the stair, Maxim Moren stopped too, and made a sound that was not a word.

Edric stood aside, and let him past, and went out, and sat on the top step outside the door in the dark, the way he would have done anyway. He sat there for a long time, listening, while the fire crackled, and two people breathed, and the smoke drifted in through the broken dome. Then he went down to the harbour, at midnight, and lifted twelve white ships out of the sea and set them down on the Long Bank, and was asleep on the floor of the library by the fire for only two days afterwards, not a week, because he had not come to Cristal empty.

Nessa Bettan was there when he woke. She was sitting by the fire with the green book on her knees, reading it again. She looked up at him with her small brown face and said, accusingly, that she had been tired for three days and Gran had made her drink fish broth, and that it was all his fault.

"Yes," said Edric. "It is."

She considered this. Then she put the book down.

"It's not crying so much," she said. "The sea. Since. Did you notice? It's still crying. But it stopped for a bit, when she woke up. Like it was listening." She frowned. "Why would it listen to me?"

He did not know. He thought about it for a long time afterwards: on the voyage east, and at Saltmere, and in a longboat in the dark of the moon, rowing towards the Teeth. He thought he knew, a little, when he put his hand into the dead grey water at the foot of the chains, and the Deep opened its clouded eye, and before it said anything else, it said: *There was a child. On your island. Who woke the sleeper with her own small tide, and took nothing for it. I heard.*

*I know,* he said.

*Is she yours?*

*No,* said Edric Vane. *She's her own. That was the point.*

And there were children on Cristal, in the years after, who could hear the sea, and raise a drop of water off a stone without it hurting them. Three of them, then five, then seven. Tam taught them to read first. Then arithmetic. Then history. And the first of them, the one the others went to when they could not find the stillness, was a Weir-folk girl with a cross voice who would not sit still, and who never in her life took anything from the sea that she could not give back by morning.
