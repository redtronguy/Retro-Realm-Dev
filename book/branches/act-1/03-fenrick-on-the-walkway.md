# Event 03: Fenrick on the Walkway

*Branches from Chapter 3, "The Weir": the moment on the narrow walkway when Fenrick Morrow steps closer with his hand resting on his sword hilt and asks, "Is it, Captain?"*

*The canon path, in which Edric invites Fenrick to the wedding, continues in [Chapter 3](../../03-the-weir.md).*

## Humiliate him in front of the Weir

*Heart − · Tide 0*

"Yes," said Edric. "It is."

He had meant to say something else. He had a gentler thing ready, somewhere at the back of his mind: *she chose, Fenrick, I'm sorry it wasn't you*. But it had been the longest day of his life, and he had held his father's starved body in his arms that afternoon, and he had walked down to the Weir with his heart so full it ached, and here was this drunk in a blue coat standing in his road with his hand on a sword, telling Mira what she would become. He found that he had no gentleness left for Fenrick Morrow. Not one drop.

"It's enough," he said. "It's more than enough. You've been standing in doorways looking at her for three years, cousin, like a dog outside a butcher's. Everybody in the Weir knows it. Everybody in the Weir is watching you do it now." He did not raise his voice. He did not need to; on the water, at night, a quiet voice carries further than a shout. "She said no. She said no when she was sixteen and she'd say it again tonight, and the next night, and every night until you're old. Go back to your garrison. Find a girl who'll have you. And take your hand off that sword, before you embarrass yourself."

Fenrick's face went perfectly white.

Then his hand closed on the hilt, and the blade came a hand's breadth out of the scabbard, and Mira cried out.

Edric had grown up on the quays of Saltmere, where men settled things with their hands and anyone who reached for steel was a fool, because steel took time and fists did not. He stepped in before the sword was clear. He caught Fenrick's wrist in his left hand and turned it, hard, the way the old bosun on the *Heron* had taught him to turn a drunk sailor's wrist, and felt the bones grind, and felt the hilt come loose. He took the sword out of Fenrick's fingers as easily as taking a cup from a child, and turned, and tossed it off the walkway.

It went into the marsh with a small, flat, ridiculous sound, like a fish jumping. The black water closed over it.

Fenrick lunged at him. And Edric, who had spent five years in the rigging and could keep his feet on a yardarm in a gale, simply stepped aside on the yard-wide planks and put out his foot.

The soldier went over the rope rail and down into the water with a splash that sent a heron screaming up out of the reeds.

---

For a moment there was no sound at all except the slap of the disturbed water against the stilts.

Then, from a dark window across the channel, an old woman cackled.

It was a high, cracked, delighted sound, and it was answered at once from another window, and another, and then a man's deep laugh, and a child's giggle quickly hushed, and someone banging a pot, until the whole dark web of the Weir was laughing, house to house, walkway to walkway, the way the Weir laughed at a wedding when the groom fell in. It was the oldest joke in the marshes. Men fell off walkways every night of the year.

Fenrick came up gasping. The water there was no more than chest deep, but the bottom was soft black mud that took a man to the knees, and he floundered, and went under again, and came up spitting, with weed in his cropped hair and his fine blue coat gone black with marsh-water. He grabbed at the stilts of the walkway and could not get a grip on the slime.

Edric crouched on the planks above him and looked down.

"Go home and sober up, cousin," he said. "You can come back and fish for your sword in the morning, if the eels haven't had it. And you can come to the wedding, if you like. Bring a towel."

The laughter rose again from the windows, louder.

Fenrick looked up at him from the black water. His face, in the swinging lamplight, was not angry. That was what Edric would remember, years later, when he could no longer remember Mira's face. It was not angry at all. It was naked, and stripped, and utterly still, like the face of a man who has been struck so hard he has not yet begun to feel it.

He did not say anything. He waded, slowly, to the next landing stage, and pulled himself out on his belly, and stood up with the water pouring off him, and walked away into the dark without looking back. All the way along the walkway, as he passed, the dark windows laughed at him.

---

"You shouldn't have done that," said Mira.

They were standing outside her mother's door. Her hand was shaking in his, and she was half laughing herself, the way people laugh after a fright, and half something else.

"He drew on me, Mira."

"I know. I know he did. I saw." She looked back along the walkway, where Fenrick had gone. "But not in front of the Weir. Not like that. Not with them laughing." She shivered. "He'd have got over the sword. Men get over swords. He'll never get over the laughing. You don't know him. You don't know what it is, to be laughed at by your own."

"He'll be gone back to his garrison in three days," said Edric. "Let him hate me from there."

She did not answer. Behind them, the last of the windows had stopped laughing, and gone dark, and the Weir was very quiet.

---

He went to the Three Anchors dripping.

He did not decide to. His feet took him there, as they had taken him there a hundred times, along the quays in the dark with the marsh-water squelching in his boots, and the yellow windows of the tavern rose up in front of him, and he went in.

The noise faltered when they saw him. Somebody snorted. Somebody else said, not quite loudly enough, *fell in, did you, corporal?*, and the whole room laughed.

Except for one man. At a corner table by the fire, a pale, neat, clerkish man with a mug of small beer in front of him looked up, and saw the soaked blue coat and the empty scabbard and the face above them, and did not laugh. He raised a hand instead, and nudged the chair across from him with his foot.

"Corporal Morrow, isn't it?" said Dorran Quell. "Sit by the fire. You look like a man who could do with a drink."

Afterwards, Dorran would think that it had been the easiest quarter of an hour of his life. He had expected to have to lead the soldier, a little at a time, the way you lead a nervous horse. He had a lie ready, about the boy wanting to get the marsh out of the girl. He never needed it. Fenrick drank the black rum as if it were water and said nothing at all until the drunk tailor came and sat down and began to talk about a letter for Solace. And when Dorran passed the pen into his left hand and began, as if in play, to write, the soldier watched every word go onto the paper with eyes like two holes in ice.

When Dorran crumpled the finished letter and tossed it, carelessly, into the sawdust behind the settle, Fenrick did not wait for the tailor to fall asleep. He did not wait for anything. He got up, in front of both of them, and walked to the corner, and knelt in the wet sawdust, and picked up the ball of paper, and smoothed it flat against his thigh, and put it inside his soaked coat.

Then he walked out.

Cade Rusk stared after him with his mouth open. Dorran sat very still, and after a while, he found that he was smiling. It was not, quite, what he had planned. It was better.

---

The letter was in the magistrate's box an hour after midnight. The sergeant of the night watch found it at dusk the next day, and the deputy Crown Magistrate read it at his desk by lamplight, a little before midnight, and signed the warrant.

So they came for Edric Vane a night early.

There was no Feast of Lamps. There was no crowd at the Three Anchors, no crew in their shore clothes, no Master Moren red-faced and blustering, no crown half-unwrapped in an old woman's hands. There was only the Solens' house on its stilts, with the lamps for tomorrow half-hung along the eaves, and Mira on a stool with a ribbon in her teeth, and her mother coughing by the stove, and Edric on a ladder with a hammer, laughing at something; and then the walkway shaking under the measured tread of six men in white and gold, and every window along the channel going dark at once.

He went with them without a fight. He told Mira it was a mistake. He told her he would be back for the tide.

At the corner by the net-sheds, as the soldiers walked him away, he looked back once, and saw a man standing in the shadow of a doorway three houses along: tall, black-haired, in a blue coat that had been brushed but still showed the tidemark of the marsh at the hem.

Fenrick Morrow was not white this time. He was not sweating, or frightened, or waiting by any door. He stood with his arms folded and watched them take Edric past, and his face was quite calm, and as the lantern swung by, Edric saw that he was smiling.

### Later

The Countess Morrow came to the house with the blue door at night, alone, in a dark cloak, and stood in the Count's study with her back to the fire and told him a thing she had never told anyone.

"The night Aren was born," Mira said. "Fenrick came home drunk. He'd been drinking since noon, waiting. He sat on the end of my bed with the baby in his arms, and he cried, and then he told me." She was not looking at him. "About the Anchors. About the purser and the pen. He thought I knew. He thought the whole Weir knew, and had always known, and had forgiven him because I married him. He wanted me to say it didn't matter."

Edric said nothing.

"He said he'd have let you be," she went on, very evenly. "He said it over and over. He'd have stood at the wedding and drunk to us and gone back to his garrison and hated you quietly for the rest of his life, and that would have been all. Only you put him in the water. In front of the Weir. And they laughed." Her hands were clenched in the folds of the cloak. "He said he could still hear them. Every night. In the Sardh, he said, when the Emir's people laughed at a soldier from the marshes who didn't know which fork to use, he heard the Weir laughing. He said he'd make the whole world stop laughing at him if it took him the rest of his life."

The fire cracked. Outside, a carriage went by on the Street of Lanterns, and its lamps ran across the ceiling and were gone.

"Emir Tariq laughed at him once," said Mira. "At a feast. Over nothing. A joke about marsh-frogs. Two years later, Fenrick opened the gates of Qasr."

She turned at last and looked at him, and he saw that she knew exactly what she was doing, and that it cost her.

"I'm not telling you this so you'll forgive him," she said. "I'm telling you so you'll know your share. You always thought you'd done nothing. That you were nothing but a sailor and a son and a man in love." Her voice cracked, very slightly, and steadied. "You were a man in love who put another man in the water for the pleasure of hearing them laugh. I saw your face, Edric. You enjoyed it."

When she had gone, he sat for a long time with his hand over the cold place on his chest, and could not, however hard he tried, remember his own face that night on the walkway. He could only remember the laughter, rolling from window to window across the dark marsh, and how sweet it had sounded.

## Tell Mira's mother he threatened you

*Heart 0 · Tide 0*

Edric did not move. He did not put his hand to his own belt. He did not say anything at all.

He looked at the hand resting on the sword hilt, and then at the face above it, and then, quite deliberately, at the dark windows of the houses on either side of the channel, where he knew the Weir was watching. Then he took Mira's hand and turned, and walked the long way round, back along the walkway and over the bridge by the eel-traps, so that he did not have to pass within reach of Fenrick Morrow at all.

"Edric," said Mira, low. "Edric, what are you—"

"Not here," he said. "Not with him. I'll not brawl with your cousin on a plank over the water three days before our wedding, and I'll not beg him to come to it either."

Behind them, Fenrick laughed, the short ugly bark with nothing in it. "That's right, Captain. Walk away. That's what hill-boys do." But he did not follow. Edric felt his eyes on his back all the way to the eel-traps, like a knife between his shoulders.

---

Mira's mother had heard. Of course she had heard; in the Weir, everyone heard everything. She was sitting up by the stove in her three shawls when they came in, with her sharp chin and her daughter's dark green eyes in a face gone hollow with coughing, and she looked at the two of them and said in the Weir-tongue, "Well? Who was it?"

Mira said, "Nobody, Ma. It's nothing."

Edric said, "It was Fenrick."

Mira turned on him. He did not look away from her mother.

"He was waiting for us on the walkway by the net-sheds, Mistress Solen," he said. "Drunk. He told Mira she'd be ashamed of her own kin inside five years. And when I told him that was enough, he put his hand on his sword and asked me if it was." He paused. "He didn't draw. But he wanted to. Every window on the channel saw it."

The old woman by the stove went very still. In the corner, where she sat on her stool with her eyes like black beads, Old Ysolde set down the shell she had been threading and turned her head.

"On the walkway," said Mira's mother. "On the eve of a wedding. With his hand on steel."

"Ma," said Mira. "Ma, please. He's your brother's son. He's family. He was drunk, he didn't mean—"

"He meant it. Men always mean it, when they're drunk. That's what drink is for." Mira's mother put her hands on the arms of her chair and pushed herself up. It took a long time, and she was coughing before she was straight, but she stood. "You didn't grow up on the walkways, Edric, not properly. You won't know. A blade on the walkways is the one thing we don't forgive. Not since my grandmother's grandmother's day, when the King's men came into the Weir with swords and the walkways ran red. Nobody draws on the water. Not a Weir man. Not ever." She reached for her cloak on its peg. "And not on my daughter's wedding road."

"Where are you going?" said Mira. "Ma, it's the middle of the night. Your chest."

"To my brother's," said her mother. "And then to Bettan's, and to old Corran's, and to the net-mother. They'll want to be told before morning." She looked at Edric for a long moment, and there was something in her face that he could not read: approval, perhaps, and something sadder underneath it. "You did right to tell me. You did right not to fight him. A hill-boy fighting a Weir-boy on the water would have been the Weir's shame and yours. This way it's only his."

When she had gone out into the dark, Mira sat down on the floor by the stove and put her face in her hands.

"You didn't have to," she said through her fingers. "He'd have gone back to his garrison. It would have been over."

"He put his hand on a sword, Mira. Next time he might not stop at the hand."

"I know." She lifted her face. It was wet. "I know. And you were right, and Ma's right, and he'll never come home again now, and I used to ride on his shoulders at the Midsummer lanterns." She wiped her eyes on her sleeve. "Gods. I hate it when everyone's right."

In the corner, Old Ysolde picked up her shell again, and threaded it, and said to nobody, in the old tongue: "The sea hears everything. It hears this too."

---

By morning, the whole Weir knew.

Fenrick woke late, in his father's house, with a head like a split cask and a taste in his mouth of black rum and something worse, and a crumpled, sawdust-stained paper in the inside pocket of his coat that he did not, for a moment, remember picking up. Then he remembered. He sat on the edge of the bed for a long time with it in his hand.

When he came out onto the walkway, his father was sitting on the landing stage with his back to the door. He did not turn round.

"Father?"

His father said nothing. He got up and went past Fenrick into the house without looking at him, and Fenrick saw on the planks beside where he had been sitting the knife Fenrick had brought him from the garrison last spring, a good knife with a horn handle, laid there with its blade pointing towards the water. In the Weir, that meant a gift returned.

He walked the length of the walkway to the net-sheds, and every door along the way was shut. Not closed: shut, with the bar down, in the middle of the morning, with the families behind them. He could hear them moving. A child laughed and was hushed. At Bettan's house an old woman opened the shutter as he passed and spat into the channel, and closed it again.

At the Solens' landing stage, the boat with no oars sat waiting for the spring tide, already garlanded with sea-lavender. Nobody was there.

He stood on the walkway in the bright cold morning and understood that he had been born here and would never come home again.

And he understood something else. He understood that the boy had done this. Not with a fist, not with a sword, nothing a man could answer. He had walked away, and gone to Fenrick's aunt like a child running to its mother, and let the Weir do it for him. He had been so careful to keep his own hands clean.

Fenrick took the crumpled paper out of his pocket and smoothed it flat against the rail. He read it again, every blotted word.

*Act swiftly, for he means to leave for the capital within days.*

He put it back in his coat. Then he walked up out of the Weir for the last time, to the Coastal Guard barracks on the harbour road, and asked the duty officer for pen and paper, and wrote out in his own hand a request for transfer to the eastern frontier garrisons, as far from the sea as the King's army could send a man. He signed it and dated it. The duty officer, who had heard the gossip like everyone else, looked at the date and then at Fenrick's face and said nothing.

That night he walked up the hill alone in the dark, towards the magistrate's house and the box beside its door, and his hand in his pocket was perfectly steady.

And on the next night, the night of the Feast of Lamps, Fenrick Morrow did not come to the Three Anchors to apologise. He was not standing by the door, white and wet and waiting. He was ten miles up the king's road with his kit on his back, walking east, and he did not once look round at the lights of Saltmere behind him.

### Later

Bastien laid the papers on the desk in the house with the blue door the way a cat lays down a bird: proudly, and with a certain contempt for the bird.

"The Coastal Guard keeps everything," he said. "Every roster, every complaint, every leave chit, for a hundred years, in a cellar at the Saltmere barracks that nobody has opened since the old King's jubilee. My man paid the clerk four marks. He'd have taken two."

There were three sheets. The first was a complaint to the captain of the Saltmere garrison from the elders of the Weir, dated three days before the night Edric Vane was taken, written in a clerk's careful hand and marked at the bottom with eleven crosses and one signature: *Corporal F. Morrow did on the third night before the spring tide stand upon the walkway of the Weir in drink and lay his hand on his sword against Captain E. Vane of the* Gull's Promise, *betrothed of our kinswoman, and threaten him before witnesses. We ask that he be forbidden the Weir.* The second was Fenrick's request for transfer east, in his own hand, dated the next morning.

The third was a note in the margin of the garrison daybook for the night before the Feast of Lamps, in the same hand as the duty sergeant's: *Cpl. Morrow seen going up Magistrate's Hill after midnight. Did not return to barracks till dawn.*

Edric read them twice. Then he sat back.

"The Lord Marshal of Valemont," he said, "threatened a man before the whole Weir, was shunned by his own people for it, asked the next morning to be sent as far from the sea as the army goes, and that same night walked up to the magistrate's door." He touched the third sheet. "And a sergeant wrote it down because a corporal came in late."

"The Chamber will like the dates," said Bastien. "Lords always like dates. They can count them on their fingers."

"Hayda's testimony is about Qasr," said Edric slowly. "This is about Saltmere. The one is the betrayal of a prince. The other is a boy in a blue coat walking up a hill." He folded the sheets very carefully. "They'll be harder to forgive together than apart."

He thought of a woman in three shawls pushing herself up out of her chair by the stove, coughing, reaching for her cloak in the middle of the night. *They'll want to be told before morning.* He had not known, then, that she was making a record. She had not known it either. She had only known that a blade on the water was the one thing the Weir did not forgive, and that what is not forgiven must be remembered.

He sat with the papers until the candle burned down. The Weir had remembered for him for nineteen years. It was time he gave it back its due.
