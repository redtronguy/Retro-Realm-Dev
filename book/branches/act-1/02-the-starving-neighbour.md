# Event 02: The Starving Neighbour

*Branches from Chapter 2, "Father and Son": the night of the homecoming, as Edric leaves his father's rooms for the Weir and his footsteps go "down the stairs, past his door", where Cade Rusk sits listening in the dark.*

## Give it

*Heart + · Tide 0*

The door on the landing below opened before he reached it.

Cade Rusk stood in the gap with a candle in his hand and no coat on, in a shirt that had been white once, and the candlelight came up under his soft face and made it look like something that had been left too long in water. He had been waiting. Edric knew it the moment he saw him. A man does not stand fully dressed behind a door at that hour by chance.

"Captain," said Cade. "Captain Vane. A word. Only a word. I won't keep you."

"I'm going down to the Weir, neighbour."

"I know. I know you are. And she'll be waiting, and you'll want to be off. I won't keep you." He did not move out of the doorway. Behind him, in the dark of the two rooms, a woman coughed, a thin dry sound like paper tearing, and stopped. "It's only. I wouldn't ask. I'd not ask on a night like this, with your father so happy, and you a captain, and the whole town drinking your health. Only I've nobody else to ask."

Edric waited.

"The rent," said Cade. He said it to the candle. "On the shop. Three quarters behind. The landlord's man came today, before your ship was even in. He says the bailiffs Thursday. They'll take the stock, what there is of it, and the shears, and the table, and then where am I? A tailor without shears." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "And Agna's chest. You'll have heard her. The healer wants paying before he'll come again."

"How much?"

Cade looked up at him then, and his small wet eyes were frightened, and greedy, and ashamed, all at once, so that it was impossible to say which was the true one. Perhaps all of them were.

"Eleven marks," he said. "Eleven would clear the landlord and the healer both. I'd pay it back. Every copper. I'd pay it back by midsummer, I swear to you, on my mother's grave."

Edric stood on the dark stair and thought about it.

He thought about the forty marks he had left his father eight months ago, and where they had gone. He thought about the thirty-eight marks of a winter long ago, the physician and the medicines and the burying, and about a sick woman upstairs and a sick woman downstairs, and about his father's face this afternoon saying *I paid it all, the week you sailed*. He thought that Cade Rusk had sat under that floor all winter with his ear to the boards and listened to an old man pick oakum for coppers and go to bed hungry, and had not once carried up a bowl of anything that was not offered for show. He was almost sure of that. Almost.

And he thought about the purse inside his coat. His pay for the voyage, paid off that afternoon in Moren's office: twenty-six marks and some silver. He had meant it for the healer who came to Mira's mother. He had meant to put it in Mira's hand tomorrow morning and watch her refuse it, and argue, and take it at last because her mother's cough was worse than her pride.

Eleven marks was nearly half.

"You knew my father was hungry," he said quietly. "All winter."

Cade's face crumpled. It did not crumple beautifully, the way a face does in a play. It simply fell in, like a pudding cut too soon, and the candle shook in his hand.

"I knew," he whispered. "Gods help me. I knew. I'd hear him walking about up there at night because he couldn't sleep for the hunger, and I'd lie there and think, I ought to take him something, and then I'd think, but then he'll know I know, and he's proud, and he'll hate me for it, and then I'd think, and what have I got to take him, anyway? Agna's soup is water. And then it'd be morning." He wiped his eyes with the back of the candle hand and nearly set his hair alight. "I'm not a good man, Captain. I never said I was. I'm only frightened. I've been frightened all my life."

Edric looked at him for a long moment.

Then he took out the purse, and counted eleven marks into Cade Rusk's palm, one at a time, in the candlelight, so that both of them could see each one go.

"Don't pay me back," he said. "Pay the landlord, and pay the healer. And the next time there's a hungry man over your head, take him the soup, even if it's water. That's what it costs."

Cade stared at the coins. His fingers closed on them very slowly, as if he expected them to vanish.

"Why?" he said. It came out hoarse.

"Because my mother was ill once," said Edric, "and you lent my father the money for the physician. He told me today. I'd forgotten anyone in this house ever did a kind thing for nothing." He buttoned his coat. "Goodnight, neighbour."

He went down the rest of the stairs and out into the street, and the night air was cold and sweet off the harbour, and he found that he felt lighter, though his purse was lighter too, and that he did not regret it. Mira would scold him. Mira would say he had been robbed. Mira would be right, perhaps. But she would kiss him afterwards, all the same.

---

Cade Rusk stood on the landing with eleven marks in his fist for a long time after the street door had banged.

Then he went back into his rooms and shut the door and sat down at the table in the dark, and opened his hand, and looked at the coins as if they were a letter in a language he could not read.

He had meant to ask for five. He had asked for eleven because a voice at the back of his head had said *he's flush, he'll not miss it, ask for more*. He had been ready to be refused. He had even, if he was honest, half wanted to be refused, so that he could go down to the Anchors and tell anyone who would listen how the young captain had grown too grand for his neighbours overnight. He had the speech ready. *A captain one day, and too proud to lend a few marks to the man who buried his mother.*

And the boy had given him the money. Given it. *Don't pay me back.* And looked at him as if he were still a man.

"Cade?" said Agna from the other room. "Was that him? Did he give it?"

"He gave it."

There was a silence. Then his wife laughed, a short dry bark that turned into a cough. "Well. More fool him."

Cade sat in the dark and did not answer her. He sat for a long time, with the coins in his hand and the ceiling creaking above him where the old man was moving about, happy, putting the dishes away, humming something under his breath.

After a while the ceiling went quiet.

He would pay the landlord in the morning. And the healer. He would. And then he thought about the Anchors, where there would be men drinking the young captain's health tonight, and where a man with eleven marks in his pocket might stand a round for once instead of cadging one, and be looked at, for once, as if he were somebody.

He told himself he would only stay for one.

---

He stayed for more than one. Of course he did. He always did. And at the corner table by the fire there was a soldier and a purser and a jug of black rum, and they poured for him without being asked, and the rum went down, and the boy's coins sat warm and heavy in his pocket, and somewhere around the third cup he heard his own voice saying *between ourselves*.

He heard himself tell them about the letter for Solace. He heard it come out of him the way it always did, the way water comes out of a cracked jug, because he had never in his life been able to hold anything in.

And then he stopped.

He stopped in the middle of a sentence, with *that kind of letter gets men hanged* still in his mouth, and put his hand over his face, and said, "No. No. I shouldn't have said. Forget I said. He's a good lad. He's a good lad, he gave me—" And he could not finish, because the purser was looking at him with an expression of the most perfect interest, and pouring him another cup, and saying gently, "Of course, neighbour. Of course he is. Drink up."

When the paper was written, Cade read it, and went white, and said what he said in every version of that night that has ever been told: that it was a hanging, that it was the Keep, that the boy was his neighbour. But this time he said one thing more, quietly, to the table, so quietly that only the soldier heard it.

"He gave me eleven marks tonight. He told me not to pay it back."

The soldier looked at him for a moment with eyes like black water. Then he looked away, at the corner behind the settle.

And at midnight, when Cade woke with his head on the table and saw the corner empty, he got as far as the door. He got as far as the street, with the cold on his face, and stood there for the length of a long breath, looking up the hill towards the magistrate's house, with eleven marks of the boy's money in his pocket like eleven small stones.

Then he went home to bed. But he did not sleep, and he did not pay the landlord in the morning, and he never spent a single one of the coins as long as he lived.

### Later

The diamond lay in Cade Rusk's palm and broke the firelight of the Dry Well into a hundred small rainbows, and the priest closed the fat, shaking fingers over it and said, "It's yours. All of it. Do some good with it, friend."

Cade looked at his own fist for a long time.

Then, slowly, he opened it again.

"I can't," he said.

In the doorway to the back room, Agna made a sound like a cat that has been stepped on.

"I can't take it, Brother." Cade's voice was thick. "Not his. Not from him." He got up, clumsily, and went behind the bar, and knelt, and came back with something wrapped in a rag gone black with years of handling. He unfolded it on the table between them. Eleven silver marks, of the old coinage, with the head of the Sun King worn smooth on every one. "He gave me these. The night he came home. The night before it all. I asked him for a loan and he gave it me and said not to pay it back. Said to take soup up to the next hungry man instead." He laughed, wetly. "I never spent them. I couldn't. Fourteen years, and the shop gone, and the inn going, and Agna sick, and some nights not a crust in the house, and I never spent them. I'd take them out and look at them."

He pushed the diamond back across the table, past the coins, until it touched the priest's hand.

"Give it to someone he'd have wanted to have it," he said. "Not me. I've had my share of his kindness. More than my share."

The priest sat very still. For a moment, only a moment, the mild forgettable face seemed to blur at its edges, as if a breath of wind had touched still water.

"There's a jeweller in Fendle—" Agna began, from the doorway, in a voice like a knife being sharpened.

"No," said Cade, without turning round. "No, there isn't. Not for us."

The Sardhi jeweller came through on the market road the next week, as he did every month, and stopped at the Crossed Keys in Fendle as he always did, and slept in his own bed, and rode on south in the morning with his throat whole. And on a bench in the yard of the Dry Well, eleven old silver marks went at last to a carter's boy with no shoes and the blight-cough, for the healer, and a bowl of soup that was not water.

## Refuse

*Heart 0 · Tide 0*

The door on the landing below opened before he reached it.

Cade Rusk stood in the gap with a candle in his hand and no coat on, and the candlelight came up under his soft face and made it look like something left too long in water.

"Captain," he said. "Captain Vane. A word. I won't keep you."

He told it the way he had plainly rehearsed it: the rent on the shop three quarters behind, the landlord's man, the bailiffs on Thursday, the shears and the table, Agna's chest and the healer who wanted paying first. Behind him in the dark, as if on cue, a woman coughed.

"Eleven marks," said Cade. "Eleven would clear it all. I'd pay it back by midsummer. On my mother's grave."

Edric stood on the dark stair and looked at him.

He was tired. It had been the longest day of his life: the narrows at dawn, Moren's hand on the quay, the captain's empty cabin, his father so light in his arms that it had frightened him. And under all of it, all evening, as he made the stew and fed the old man and listened to him talk, there had been a slow, cold anger that he had kept pushed down at the bottom of himself because it was his homecoming. It came up now, at the sight of the candle and the soft wet face, and he did not push it down.

"You knew he was starving," he said.

Cade's eyes slid sideways. "Now, lad, we've been over that. I offered. I told you I offered."

"You lived under his floor all winter. You'd have heard him walking at night because he couldn't sleep for hunger. And you never once went up those stairs with so much as a heel of bread that wasn't for show." Edric heard his own voice, very quiet, very level, and did not quite recognise it. "And tonight you sat under the same floor and listened to me promise him a house on the hill. And you thought: *the boy's flush. The boy will pay.*"

"That's not fair." Cade's face had gone a dull, mottled red. "That's not fair, that's not *fair*. I lent your father thirty-eight marks when your mother—"

"And he paid you back every one of them, out of my money, the week I sailed, and then he picked oakum for eight months and ate oats. You've had your thirty-eight marks, neighbour. You've had them twice over." Edric took a breath. "I've twenty-six marks in my pocket and every one of them is going to a sick woman in the Weir whose daughter is about to be my wife. I haven't eleven for you. Ask the Anchors. You're there often enough."

He went down past him. He did not look back. He heard the door close behind him on the landing, softly, without a bang, which was somehow worse.

Out in the street the night was cold and sweet off the harbour, and he stood for a moment and breathed it, and felt the anger go out of him with the breath, and felt in its place something small and sour that might have been shame.

Then he thought of his father's empty bread crock, and the shame went too, and he walked down the hill towards the lamps of the Weir and did not think of Cade Rusk again that night, or for a great many nights after.

---

Cade stood behind his closed door with the candle guttering and listened to the young captain's footsteps go away down the street.

*Ask the Anchors. You're there often enough.*

He found that he was shaking. Not with shame. Shame was an old acquaintance; it came and went like the damp and he had long since stopped noticing it. This was something else. This was hot and bright and it went right through him, from his belly to the backs of his eyes, and it felt, for the first time in years, almost like strength.

*Every one of them is going to a sick woman in the Weir.* To the marsh-girl's mother. Not to a neighbour of fifteen years who had held the boy on his knee. Not to a man who had lent his family money for a burying when no one else on Ropewalk Lane would. To the *Weir*.

"Cade?" said Agna from the other room. "Did he give it?"

"No."

Silence. Then a short, dry laugh. "Course he didn't. What did you expect? He's gentry now. He'll be crossing the street to keep his boots out of our gutter by midsummer."

Cade said nothing. He put on his coat. His hands were steady, buttoning it, and that surprised him.

"Where are you going?"

"Out."

---

He did not wait to be asked to the corner table at the Three Anchors. He saw the jug of black rum and the soldier and the purser and went straight to them, and sat, and when the purser poured him a cup he drank it down in one and held the cup out for another, and when the purser said, mildly, "You look like a man with something on his mind, neighbour," Cade Rusk laughed out loud.

"Something on my mind," he said. "Oh, aye. Something on my mind. Our young captain. Captain *Vane*." He drank. "Too grand for his neighbours now. Too grand by half. Turned me away on the stair tonight like a beggar. Me, that lent his father the money to bury his mother."

He talked. He had always talked; it was the one thing he had ever been good at, after silence. But tonight there was no stopping him and no wanting to stop. He told them about the ham and the cheese and the two kinds of bread. He told them about the house on the hill with a garden. And then, leaning in, delighted, he told them about the letter for Solace, and the dead captain, and the old judge with the blue door, and the old man saying *that kind of letter gets men hanged*. He did not lower his voice to what he believed was a whisper. He did not stop and look uneasy. He embellished. He said the boy had gone white when his father asked about it. He said the boy had been to Solace before, on the quiet, he'd swear to it. He said you could always tell, with that sort, the ones who were too good, too lucky.

"There's always something under it," he said, and tapped the side of his nose. "Always."

The purser watched him with an expression of the most perfect interest, and refilled his cup, and said nothing at all. He did not need to. When the pen and paper came and the pen went into the left hand, Cade Rusk read the finished letter, moving his lips, and went a little pale under his flush, and said what a man says, *here now, that's a hanging*. But he said it more quietly than he might have. And when the purser crumpled the paper and tossed it into the sawdust behind the settle, Cade's eyes went to the corner and stayed there a moment, and then came back to the jug.

At midnight he woke with his head on the table and the corner empty.

He looked at it for a long time. *You could go after him,* said the small voice at the bottom of his mind. *You could go up the hill. You could knock on the boy's door.*

And another voice answered it, a new one, hot and bright, that he had found on the landing tonight and did not mean to give back. *Knock on his door? He'd not open it. He'd not have eleven marks for you, nor a word neither. Ask the Anchors. You're there often enough.*

Cade Rusk got up, and put on his coat, and went home to bed, and slept, for once, quite soundly.

### Later

There was a jeweller in Fendle. Agna had said so on the night the priest came, kneeling by his chair with her thin yellow hand over his fat one, and Cade had said nothing, and the saying nothing had been a kind of yes.

The jeweller came the week after, as she had said he would, and looked at the stone through a glass for a long time, and offered forty-five thousand marks. Cade took it. And in the night he woke to screaming, and went along the passage in his nightshirt with a candle, and found the jeweller dead on the floor of the best room and his wife on her knees beside him, wet to the elbows, counting money out of a strongbox by the light of the overturned lamp.

"Ninety thousand more," said Agna, without looking up. She was very calm. "He had ninety thousand more. Help me, Cade."

He did not help her. He stood in the doorway and shook. And then, because a voice at the bottom of him said *run*, and because he had never in his life been any good at anything but getting out of the way, he snatched up the strongbox in both arms and ran: out of the back door, across the yard in the rain, along the old Sallow dyke in the dark, with the box banging against his belly and his bare feet slipping in the mud.

And all the way, gasping, he told himself the story he had been telling himself for fourteen years, the one that had started on a dark stair on Ropewalk Lane, the one that made everything easier. *He turned me away. Him with his ham and his two kinds of bread and his house on the hill. Eleven marks, and he turned me away like a beggar, and said ask the Anchors. Well. I asked. And nobody's ever paid Cade Rusk what he's owed.*

The diamond had been the boy's. The priest had said so. *Do some good with it.* But the boy had never done any good to him. The boy had looked at him on the stair as if he were dirt, and gone down to the Weir with his purse full for the marsh-girl's mother, and if the boy had died in the Keep, then the world had only done to him what he had done to Cade, and turnabout was fair. It wasn't Cade who had held the knife. It wasn't Cade who had written the letter. It was never Cade.

He found that, if he told it to himself enough times, it almost held.

They found him at first light, two miles off, asleep in a ditch with the strongbox in his arms. He woke to a constable's boot in his ribs and lay looking up at the grey sky, and for a moment, before he remembered, he thought he was back on the landing on Ropewalk Lane, with a candle in his hand, waiting for a young man's footsteps on the stair, rehearsing what he would say if he were refused.
