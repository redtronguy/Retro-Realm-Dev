# Event 01: The Pepper Casks

*Branches from Chapter 1, "The Gull's Promise": the night on the voyage home when Edric goes down alone into the aft hold, "the night before we made Port Jasmine", and finds sand where the pepper should be.*

*The canon path, in which Edric says nothing and decides the Ember Coast merchants must have cheated the ship, continues in [Chapter 1](../../01-the-gulls-promise.md).*

## Confront Dorran privately

*Heart + · Tide 0*

The lantern swung on its hook and the shadows in the aft hold swung with it, so that the stacked casks seemed to lean towards him and away, towards him and away, like men listening.

Edric knelt with the lid of the cask in one hand and looked at what was inside. He had come down because the smell was wrong. Pepper had a smell you could taste from the hatch, hot and dusty and sweet, a smell that got into your clothes and your hair and stayed there for a month after you came ashore. The smell in this corner of the hold was damp sacking and bilge. He had told himself it was the damp. He had told himself that all the way down the ladder.

He pushed his hand in, past a layer of good black peppercorns no deeper than his wrist, and his fingers met wet cloth, and under the cloth, sand. Cold, grey, heavy beach sand, packed down hard.

He tried the next cask. The same. And the next. He counted along the row with the lantern held high: eleven casks branded with Moren's mark and the purser's tally, every one of them light by a few pounds where it should not have been, every one of them topped with a fist of pepper and filled with the shore of some beach on the Ember Coast.

He sat back on his heels.

He was nineteen and he was first mate and he had never in his life had to decide what to do about another man's sin. On every ship he had sailed, someone else had decided. He thought of Captain Halloran, asleep in the stern cabin with a headache he had been complaining of since noon. He thought of Moren, square and careful on the quay at Saltmere, whose house would stand or fall on what was in this hold. And he thought, because he could not help it, of Dorran Quell bent over his ledgers by the light of a stub of candle, neat and pale and quiet, never once late with a sum in eight years.

Eleven casks. A tenth of the pepper.

He put the lids back. He did it carefully, tapping each one home with the heel of his hand, and took the lantern, and went up the ladder.

---

The purser's berth was a cupboard aft of the bread room, with a bunk and a fold-down desk and a shelf of ledgers held in by a bar against the roll. Dorran was at the desk when Edric knocked. He looked up with his small, polite smile, and saw Edric's face, and the smile stayed where it was a moment too long.

"Mister Vane. You're up late."

"I've been in the aft hold," said Edric, and shut the door behind him.

He watched it happen. He had not known he could see a thing like that in a man's face, but he saw it now: the stillness first, and then a kind of quick, cold reckoning behind the eyes, like a clerk running his finger down a column, and only after that, last of all, the fear.

"The damp," said Dorran. "Yes. I've marked it. A tenth of the pepper, I'm afraid. It's in the book." He turned a page with one finger. "Off the Ember Coast. You'll remember the rain."

"I remember the rain," said Edric. "Rain doesn't turn pepper into sand."

The ship leaned, and the bar across the shelf creaked, and the candle-flame lay down and stood up again.

"I don't know what you mean," said Dorran.

"Yes, you do."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then Dorran Quell put his pen down very precisely beside the ledger, squared to its edge, and Edric saw that his hand was shaking, and that he was ashamed of it, and that the shame was worse to him than the fear.

"Who else knows?" Dorran said. His voice had gone quite flat.

"Nobody."

"The captain?"

"He's asleep. He's not well." Edric leaned his back against the door. "I haven't told anyone, Dorran. I came to you."

"Why?"

It was not the question Edric had expected. He had expected excuses, or anger, or denial. He had not expected to be asked why he had been decent, as if decency were a move in a game whose rules Dorran could not quite make out.

"Because you've sailed with me eight months," he said, "and you never cheated a man of his ration or his pay, and you sat up with old Wim three nights when he had the flux, and I don't think you're a thief. I think you did a stupid thing. I want to know why."

Dorran laughed. It was not much of a laugh.

"Every purser does it," he said. "Every one, on every ship on this coast. A little here and a little there. It never hurts anyone. The owners write off a tenth for damp every voyage; they expect it; they'd think you were lying if you didn't. Halloran never once looked in the stores. Nobody looks." He was talking faster now, to the ledger, not to Edric. "You don't know what it is to be a clerk, Mister Vane. You climb the rigging and the crew sing songs about you. I count. I've counted every biscuit and every barrel and every copper on this ship for eight years, and I'll count them for eight more, and when I'm forty Moren will give me a stool in the back of the office and call it a promotion. My father was a cooper on Tanner's Row. He drank. I learned to count by counting the coppers he spent on it. I swore I'd never be poor the way he was poor." He stopped. "There. That's why. Is that what you wanted?"

Edric looked at him for a long time.

"Here's what we'll do," he said. "You'll put it back. Out of your share, this voyage and the next, however long it takes. You'll write it in the book as a debt to the house, in your own hand, so it's there in black and white if anyone ever looks. And I won't say a word. Not to the captain. Not to Moren. Not to anyone. Not ever."

Dorran looked up.

"That's all?"

"That's all."

"You'd do that." It was not quite a question.

"It's eleven casks of pepper, Dorran. It isn't worth a man's life." Edric found that he was smiling, a little, because the thing was done and it had been easier than he had feared. "Put it right, and it never happened. You have my word."

He held out his hand. After a moment, Dorran took it. His fingers were cold and damp, and they held on, briefly, harder than Edric expected, the way a man holds a rope.

"Thank you," said Dorran Quell. "I won't forget it."

---

He did not sleep that night.

He lay in his bunk in the cupboard aft of the bread room with the candle out and the ship working around him, and went over it, the way he went over a column that would not balance.

*You have my word.*

He had believed it, in the moment. The boy's face had done that thing it did, opening like a window, and for the space of a handshake Dorran had felt something he did not have a name for, something warm and humiliating, like being forgiven by a child.

But the moment had passed, and the boy had gone back on deck, and the ledger was still open on the desk, and the numbers were the numbers.

The boy knew. That was the sum of it. Whatever else was true, the boy knew, and would always know, and could say so whenever he liked. Tomorrow. Next year. In ten years, over a bottle, to a friend. To Moren, when Moren asked who ought to be the next purser of the *Gull's Promise*. To a magistrate. To a girl, in bed.

A debt in his own hand, in black and white, in the book. *So it's there if anyone ever looks.* The boy had made him write his own confession, and called it mercy, and gone up on deck whistling.

A man who forgives you, Dorran thought, owns you. He holds the note. He can call it in whenever he chooses, and you will never, ever know when that will be.

He did not believe in soft-hearted fools. He had told himself so for twenty-six years. But lying in the dark, with the hull groaning around him, he found that he believed in this one a little, and that it frightened him far more than an enemy would have. An enemy you could understand. An enemy wanted something. What did a man want, who let you go?

He did not know. And not knowing was a hook in his chest.

---

Three days later the captain's headache became a fever. Six weeks after that, the *Gull's Promise* came round the harbour light at Saltmere with her flag at half-mast and a nineteen-year-old at the wheel.

Dorran stood at the rail and watched Moren shake the boy's hand on the quay. He watched the boy's face light up. He did not need to hear the words.

And then the boy turned, and looked up, and found him at the rail, and smiled at him: not the open, grinning wave of a shipmate, but something smaller and kinder and private, a quick tilt of the head that said, as plainly as words, *it's all right. I haven't forgotten. Neither of us will ever say.*

Dorran raised a hand, and smiled back, and went cold to the bottom of his soul.

That night, in the corner of the Three Anchors, when the soldier came in from the dark with murder in his face and the drunken tailor sat down and began to talk about a letter for Solace, Dorran Quell called for pen and paper and passed the pen into his left hand. And his left hand shook, so that the blotted letters came out worse than he meant them to, and he had to hold his wrist with his right to steady it. He told himself it was the rum. He had drunk none.

### Later

The bank of Quell had been dead for a day and a night, and the Lord Treasurer had not been home.

He stood in his counting-house a little after midnight with the shutters closed and the crowd gone from the street at last, and the saddlebags packed on the floor at his feet, heavy as two dead dogs. Two million marks. Everything that was left of the Blight Fund. The horse was waiting in the yard. The north gate would be open until two.

He had his hand on the latch of the door when he stopped.

He did not know why. He stood there for a long time, a thin grey man in a brown cloak, with the latch cold under his fingers, and found that he was thinking of a cupboard aft of a bread room, and a candle lying down and standing up again with the roll of a ship, and a boy's voice saying *it isn't worth a man's life*.

Nineteen years, and he had never once let himself think of it. He had thought instead of the hook, and the note held, and the debt the boy might call in. The boy had never called it in. The boy had gone to the Keep with it still in his pocket, unsaid. Dorran had made very sure of that.

He went back to the desk. He opened the black ledger at the page where the Blight Fund began, eleven years of it, every mark taken and every beacon left dark, and he took a slip of paper and wrote on it, in his own neat copperplate, for the first time in his life with his own name under it:

*North gate. The king's road. The Greyhills, then the border. The towns are owed every mark of it. I was always going to put it back. That is a lie. D.Q.*

He laid it in the ledger and left the ledger open on the desk where the auditors could not miss it, and went out to the horse.

Bastien brought the ledger to the house with the blue door at dawn. Edric read the slip twice, standing at the window, and felt something under his ribs that was not triumph, and was not quite pity, and was closer than either to an old, faint, foolish hope.

He did not wait for a rider this time. He rode north himself that same morning, and came up the ravine to Rook's monastery on the fourth day instead of the eighth, and found Dorran Quell hungry and frightened and very much alive, sitting on the straw with his back to the wall of the cave, as if he had been expecting someone.

## Report him to Moren

*Heart 0 · Tide 0*

The lantern swung on its hook, and Edric knelt in the aft hold with his hand in a cask of sand, and knew exactly what he ought to do, because it was what Halloran had taught him.

*A ship is a trust, boy. Every man aboard holds a piece of it. The owner trusts the captain with his fortune. The captain trusts the mate with his ship. The mate trusts the crew with their lives. And when one man breaks his piece, every man aboard is the poorer, whether he knows it or not.*

Eleven casks. A tenth of Moren's pepper, on the voyage that would decide whether Moren's house lived or died.

He put the lids back, and went up the ladder, and went aft to tell the captain.

Halloran was in his bunk with a wet cloth over his eyes. He lifted one corner of it and squinted at Edric with a face gone grey and shiny.

"Not tonight, boy. Head like a cracked bell. Whatever it is, tell me in the morning."

In the morning the fever had come, and after that there was no telling him anything at all.

---

So Edric carried it home. He carried it for six weeks, around the Cape of Storms and up the long coast, folded small and kept at the back of his mind with the oilskin packet and the old man's last words. He did not speak to Dorran about it. He told himself it was not his place; that it was the owner's cargo and the owner's purser, and the owner's business to judge. He did not quite admit to himself that he could not think what he would say to Dorran's face.

And on the quay at Saltmere, in the shade of the harbour master's office, with Moren's hand still warm in his and the word *captain* still ringing in his ears, he heard Moren say, "Was there anything else? Anything the captain left?"

He thought of the packet, and kept that.

He thought of the sand, and did not.

"There's one thing, sir," he said. "I'm sorry to bring it to you today of all days. But you'll see the books tomorrow and you ought to hear it from me first. The tenth of the pepper we lost to damp. It wasn't damp."

He told it plainly, the way he told everything. The smell from the hatch. The eleven casks. The fist of pepper on top and the wet sacking and the grey sand below. The purser's tally on every one.

Moren listened without moving. When it was done, he sat a long while on his coil of rope, looking at the water.

"Eight years," he said at last. "Eight years he's kept my books. Never late. Never a mark wrong." He rubbed his face with both hands. "That's how they do it, you know. They're never wrong in the little things, so you never look at the big ones."

"I don't think he meant real harm, sir. I think every purser—"

"Every purser does it. Yes. I know. I've heard it." Moren stood up. His knees, Edric noticed, held. "And every owner who lets it pass ends up with a fleet of pursers who do a little more each voyage, until one year it isn't a tenth, it's half, and the owner's house is sold for the timber." He looked up at the ship. "Is he aboard?"

"Yes, sir."

"Fetch him down."

---

It was done on the quay, in front of the harbour master's office, with half the crew still coiling lines a dozen yards away and pretending not to listen.

Moren did not shout. He was not a shouting man. He laid it out for Dorran Quell exactly as Edric had laid it out for him, and asked whether any of it was false. Dorran stood very straight in his neat dark coat with his hands at his sides, and his eyes went once to Edric's face, and stayed there, and did not move for the rest of it.

"No, sir," he said. "None of it is false."

"Then you're dismissed from my service as of this hour. You'll be paid your wages to today, less the value of the pepper. You'll have no reference from my house." Moren's voice was quite even. "I'm sorry for it, Quell. You were a good clerk. You might have been a great deal more."

Dorran said nothing. He was still looking at Edric.

Edric made himself meet it. He had thought he would feel righteous, or at least clean. He felt neither. He felt as if he had kicked a dog that had not yet bitten anyone.

"I'm sorry, Dorran," he said. "I truly am. It wasn't mine to keep."

Dorran Quell smiled at him then: a small, polite smile that went no further than his teeth.

"No," he said. "Of course it wasn't, Captain. You did your duty. I'd expect nothing less." He made Moren a short bow, and turned, and walked away down the long quay with his back very straight, and did not look round, and Harl the bosun spat into the harbour and said to nobody in particular that it was about time.

---

He walked to Chandler's Row and sat in his narrow rented room with his ledgers, which were no longer his ledgers, and did not light the candle.

Through the thin wall he could hear his landlady's canary chirping in its cage, and her voice in the passage telling someone that the purser on the second floor had been let go off the *Gull*, she'd had it from the fishwife, and wasn't it a shame, such a quiet young man, and would he be able to pay to the end of the month, did they think. By evening the whole of Chandler's Row would know. By tomorrow, the whole coast. There was not a counting-house between Saltmere and the Cape that would put him on a stool now.

He had been afraid for six weeks. He had lain awake every night of the voyage wondering what the boy knew and what the boy would do. And now he knew, and the fear was gone, and in its place there was something he had never felt before in his careful life: something hot and clean and almost sweet, like the first mouthful of strong drink to a man who has never touched it.

*It wasn't mine to keep.*

No reference. No reference in Saltmere meant no work on the coast. Eight years, and the boy had ended them in a quarter of an hour, on the quay, in front of the crew, on the very morning he was made captain. And then said he was *sorry*.

Dorran sat in the dark and found that he was not afraid of Edric Vane any more. He was not afraid of anything. He only wanted, quite calmly, with a clarity that astonished him, to see the boy lose everything he had just been given, piece by piece, while he watched.

That night he went to the Three Anchors. Not to hide, this time, behind a mug of small beer he did not drink. He went to find someone. And when the soldier came in from the dark with that look on his face, Dorran did not wait for chance. He raised a hand at once.

When the tailor came and talked about the letter for Solace, Dorran wrote the denunciation in a quarter of an hour, with his left hand, and his left hand did not shake at all.

### Later

The Count of Cristal dined at the house of Quell on the Street of Lanterns in his second month in Solace: white marble, forty servants, a long table under a ceiling painted with the Sun King's triumph over the Drowned Heresy. The Baroness was charming and nervous. The daughter played the harp after dinner, beautifully, with an expression that said she would rather be anywhere else on earth.

And the Lord Treasurer, over the port, told a story.

"I was a ship's clerk, once," he said. "Did you know that, my lord Count? Nobody believes it now. Nineteen years ago, in a fishing town in the south. And I made one small mistake, the kind every clerk on that coast made, and a boy informed on me for it. A deckhand Moren had just made captain, too green to know how the world works. Had me thrown off the quay in front of the whole crew." He turned his glass so that the candlelight ran in it like blood. "It was the best thing that ever happened to me. It taught me what a man is worth."

"And what is he worth, my lord?" said the Count.

"Exactly what he can be made to lose." Dorran smiled his small smile. "The boy came to a bad end, I'm told. They usually do."

Under the table, Edric's hand lay flat on his knee, and he reached, very lightly, the way Faro had taught him, for the currents in the man across from him. He had expected fear. In every other powerful man he had touched in Solace there had been fear at the bottom, old and cold and settled, like silt.

In Dorran Quell there was none. There was only a long, slow, satisfied current, warm as blood, going round and round in the dark like a fish in a bowl, and it had been going round for nineteen years.

That week the Lord Treasurer suspended the Blight Fund payments to the fishing towns of the Saltmere coast "pending review", and had the clerk who drew up the papers dismissed without a reference for a blotted line. The Count read of both in the *Courant*, and folded the paper very carefully along its creases, and sat for a long time at the window, tasting salt.
