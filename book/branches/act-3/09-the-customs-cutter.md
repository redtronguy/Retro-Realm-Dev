# Event 09: The Customs Cutter

## Outrun it honestly

*Heart 0 · Tide 0*

The cutter came out of the haze off Harnmouth at the turn of the forenoon watch, and by noon she was two miles astern and closing.

She was a King's ship, and she looked it: long and lean and low, painted white, with a gold sun on her pennant and a great spread of new canvas, and a brass bow-chaser that winked in the sunlight every time she rose to a swell. Pike, at the rail with Jac's glass, said she was the *Diligence*, out of Saltmere, and said it the way another man might name a disease. He had been six years in the King's galleys. He had been put there by a cutter very like this one.

"She's faster than us," said Jac, "in open water, in a wind like this." She said it quite calmly. She was standing by the wheel with her arms folded and her red hair blowing, and if you had not known her, you would have thought she was bored. "And we've forty casks of brandy in the hold and two hundred sacks of salt, and the salt alone is enough to hang the lot of us. So. Ideas, gentlemen. I'm open to ideas."

Faro was at the wheel. He had been aboard five weeks. His hands had only just stopped bleeding from the ratlines, and his skin had only just stopped peeling, and he still wore Jac's old straw hat pulled low against the glare. Nobody had asked him for ideas. But he had them, because the sea was giving them to him.

He could feel it. He could feel all of it: the long swell running in from the south-west, the set of the current along the coast, the reef line of the Hound's Teeth three miles to starboard, a jagged line of black rocks and white water running out from the shore like the jaw of a dog. And he could feel something else. Under the water, off the seaward end of the Teeth, a long low bank of sand, lying a fathom down, shifting very slightly in the current. Shifting towards the cutter's course. As if it were listening to him. As if it were waiting for him to want something.

He knew what would happen if he did. He could see it as clearly as if it had already happened: the *Diligence* running down on them with every sail drawing, and the sand coming up under her, a fathom, half a fathom, a foot, and the long white hull grinding to a stop in the middle of the open sea with her masts whipping forward and her crew thrown flat on her deck. It would be so easy. He would not even have to reach. He would only have to stop holding on.

He thought of the eye in the dark. *Every time you ask, a little more of me will come into you.*

He held on.

In for eight. Out for eight. He found the stillness the old man had taught him, in a cell, a lifetime ago, and he put the sea away from him, gently, the way you put a dog out of a room. He felt the sandbank settle back. He felt the water go quiet and ordinary under the keel. And he was alone at the wheel, with his own two hands and his own eyes and fourteen years of nothing, and a King's cutter two miles astern.

"The Teeth," he said.

Jac looked at him. "What about them?"

"There's a gap. Halfway along. The fishermen out of Saltmere call it the Needle." He could see it in his mind: not from the sea, but from a boy's memory, a boy in a borrowed skiff with Halloran's old chart on his knees, sixteen years old and showing off. "At the top of the tide there's a fathom and a half in it. Maybe less. The *Slip* draws a fathom and a quarter, loaded." He looked at the sun. "It's the top of the tide in an hour."

"A fathom and a quarter," said Jac, "*loaded*."

"Yes."

She understood. He watched her understand. He watched her face, which never showed anything, show it: the forty casks of the best northern brandy, that she had paid for with every mark she had and a loan from a man in Corrow she did not like, that was going to clear her debts and buy the *Slip* new sails and pay the crew's shares for the winter.

"No," she said.

"The salt can stay. It's lighter than you'd think, packed dry. But the brandy has to go. All of it. Or she won't float through, and they'll take us against the rocks."

"I've never lost a cargo," said Jac Teague. "Never. Not once, in eleven years. Ask anyone in the Reach."

"I know."

Astern, the bow-chaser on the *Diligence* banged, and a white plume of water went up a cable's length behind the *Slip*'s wake. A ranging shot. The next one would not fall short.

Jac looked at the cutter, and then at Faro, for a long moment. There was something in her face he could not read. As if she had been expecting him to say something else. As if she had been waiting, without knowing what for, and was disappointed, and relieved, both at once.

"Pike," she said. "Olaf. Get the hatch off. Every cask. Over the side." She turned back to Faro. "And if you put her on the rocks, Shipwreck, I'll drown you myself."

---

They went over one by one, the casks, each of them a month's wages in Saltmere, bobbing away astern in a long brown line like ducks, and Olaf wept as he heaved them and Pike swore at every one, and the *Slip* came up in the water, lighter, livelier, and leaned over, and began to run for the Teeth.

The *Diligence* came after them. She was gaining, even so. The bow-chaser banged again, and this time the shot went through the main topsail with a sound like tearing silk and left a hole you could have put your head through. Somebody shouted. Tam, in the rigging, was clinging to the yard with his eyes shut.

Faro did not look back. He was watching the Teeth.

The reef came up out of the glare like the jaw of a beast: black rock, and white water breaking over it, and here and there a ledge awash with weed. And halfway along, where the boy in the skiff had found it seventeen years ago, there was a place where the white water was a little less white. A gap no wider than a church door. A line of darker water running through it, smooth and green and fast.

He put the wheel over.

The *Slip* went for the gap like a thread for a needle. He felt her lift on the swell, and drop, and lift. He felt the rocks go by on either side, close enough to touch, close enough that Pike, in the bow, could have spat on them. He felt the keel touch, once, a long soft grinding kiss of sand along her belly that went through every timber and every man aboard, and he thought, *this is it, this is the end of it*, and he did not reach. He wanted to. Every part of him wanted to. The sea was right there, under the keel, cold and patient and eager, and it would have lifted her the way a mother lifts a child over a puddle.

He kept his hands on the wheel, and his eyes on the water, and his mind on the old man in the cell.

And the *Slip* came off the sand on the next swell, and shot through the Needle into the calm water on the landward side, and was through.

Behind them, the *Diligence* came up to the gap, and her captain looked at it, and looked at the white water breaking on either side, and thought about the King's ship under his feet and his own neck, and put his helm over, and stood away along the outside of the reef, where there was no way through for ten miles. By the time she had gone round, the *Slip* was hull-down in the haze to the north, and gone.

---

Nobody said anything for a long time. Olaf was sitting on the empty hatch with his head in his hands. Tam came down out of the rigging and was sick over the lee rail.

Jac came and stood beside the wheel. She looked at the hole in the main topsail, and the scraped paint along the waterline, and the empty hold, and then at him.

"Forty casks," she said.

"I know. I'm sorry."

"I'll be paying that off till midwinter. So will you. Out of your share." She was quiet a moment. Then, oddly: "You didn't do anything. Did you?"

He looked at her.

"I've been at sea all my life," said Jac Teague. "I know what luck feels like. Since we found you on that reef, we've had nothing but. Fair winds. Easy seas. Olaf catching fish every night like they're queuing up to be caught." She tilted her head. "And just now, with that cutter on our tail, I thought, *here it comes, here's the luck.* I could almost feel it coming. And it didn't." She looked at him narrowly. "You threw my brandy over the side and went through the Needle on a fathom and a quarter with your own two hands. Didn't you?"

He did not know what to say. So he said the truth.

"Yes."

She looked at him for a long time. Then she laughed, short and rough, and slapped his shoulder hard enough to hurt.

"That," she said, "was the best bit of sailing I ever saw. And the most expensive." And she went off forward, bellowing for the sailmaker's palm and needle, and did not mention the brandy again.

But that night, in the Reach, she changed her boast. *Never lost a ship,* she said, in the tavern in Corrow, to anyone who would listen. *Never lost a passenger. Lost a cargo once. Best day's work I ever did.* And when people asked her why, she only grinned.

### Later

In the nineteenth year, in Solace, at a reception at the Ministry of the Treasury, a grey-haired, weather-beaten man in the plain blue coat of a harbour commissioner was introduced to the Count of Cristal, and stayed talking to him by the window for most of an hour.

His name was Captain Aldous Fenn. He had commanded the King's customs cutter *Diligence* on the Saltmere station for eleven years, and had been promoted ashore, he said, for catching more smugglers than any captain on the coast. "All but one," he said, and laughed. "There was a black cutter, years ago, out of the Reach. No name on her stern. I chased her down the coast off the Hound's Teeth one summer with a fair wind and a clear sky, and I had her, my lord. I had her cold. And her master put her through the Needle at the top of the tide. Threw his whole cargo over the side to do it, brandy, I could smell it on the water for a mile. Went through a gap I wouldn't have put a rowing boat through, on a keel's breadth of water, and never touched the rocks." He shook his head, smiling. "Best piece of seamanship I ever saw. I always wanted to meet the man who did it. Shake his hand. Then hang him, of course."

"Of course," said the Count.

"I put it in my report as luck," said Captain Fenn. "The Radiants came round asking, a few years back. Wanted every report of strange doings in the Reach: ships sunk in a calm, sandbanks that weren't on the charts. Sea-witchery, they called it." He snorted. "I told them there was nothing strange about it. Just a damned good sailor." He raised his glass. "Wherever he is."

The Count raised his own, and drank, and said nothing, and when Captain Fenn had gone, he stood at the window for a long while, looking down at the harbour, with something in his still face that his steward, watching from across the room, had not seen there in a long time, and thought might have been pride.

## Sink it

*Heart − · Tide ++*

The cutter came out of the haze off Harnmouth at the turn of the forenoon watch, and by noon she was a mile astern and closing, and her bow-chaser had already spoken twice.

The second shot had not fallen short. It had come in low across the water, skipping, and hit the *Slip* in the quarter, just above the waterline, with a crack like a bone breaking, and gone through the planking into the after hold in a shower of splinters. Pike had been standing at the rail there with Jac's glass. He was sitting on the deck now, with his back against the rail and his hands pressed to his side, and a long sliver of oak as thick as a man's thumb was standing out from between his fingers, and the blood was coming through them, slow and dark.

"I'm all right," he kept saying. "I'm all right. Leave it. Leave it in." His one eye found Faro at the wheel. "Don't let them take us, lad. You hear me? Not the galleys. I did six years. I'll not do it again. I'll go over the side first."

The *Diligence* was close enough now that Faro could see her people. Men in blue coats at her rail, with muskets. A man in her bow, sighting along the brass barrel of the chaser. An officer on her quarterdeck in a white coat with gold at the cuffs, with a speaking trumpet in his hand, calling across the water for them to heave to in the King's name. And over all of it, at her masthead, a gold sun on a white pennant, cracking in the wind.

He had seen that sun before. He had seen it on the sleeve of the soldiers who had come for him at the Feast of Lamps. He had seen it over the gate of the Drowned Keep, every day, for thirteen years. He had seen it, in a memory that was not his, on the sails of a hundred white ships in a burning harbour, and on the breasts of the men who stood in them with fire pouring from their hands.

*In the King's name.*

Under the water, off the seaward end of the Hound's Teeth, a long bank of sand lay a fathom down, shifting very slightly in the current. He could feel it waiting. He could feel the whole sea waiting, the swell and the current and the deep cold water off the reef, all of it, turned towards him, the way a dog turns towards its master's hand. He could ground her. He knew it. He could lift the sand under her keel and leave her stranded in the open sea, with her masts whipping and her crew thrown flat, and sail away while they cursed. It would be enough. It would be more than enough.

Pike coughed, and there was blood on his lips.

*Enough,* said something in Faro, very calmly. *Enough to let them limp home, and refit, and come out again next week. And catch another ship, and put another old man in the galleys for six years. And another. And another. For three hundred years.*

He reached.

He did not reach for the sand. He reached past it, down, into the deep cold water off the end of the reef, where the swell came in from the open sea, and he took it in his hands, and he lifted.

---

It began as a swell. A long, low, glassy hump of water rising out of the sea behind the *Diligence*, smooth as a sheet of green bottle-glass, no higher than a man. Then higher. Then higher still. He felt it come up out of the deep, and felt the cold come up into him with it, through the deck, through the wheel, through the palms of his hands, so cold that it burned, and lit the lines on his chest under his shirt like fuses, and he did not let go. He took more. He took more than he had taken under the Teeth, on the night he came up out of the dark. He took it until his teeth were bared and his eyes were streaming and the salt in his mouth was so strong he could not breathe.

The officer on the *Diligence*'s quarterdeck turned round.

Faro saw him turn. He saw the speaking trumpet fall out of his hand. He saw the men at the rail turn too, one after another, and their faces change.

The wave came up behind the cutter like a wall. Green and smooth and enormous, with the sun shining through it, so that for a moment, at its crest, it was full of light. It picked her up. It picked her up as if she weighed nothing, a hundred feet of King's ship with her guns and her masts and her forty men, and carried her, sideways, stern first, across a quarter of a mile of open sea, and threw her down on the Hound's Teeth.

He heard her break. Even over the roar of the water, he heard it. A long, splintering, grinding scream of timber, like a tree coming down in a forest, and then another, and then the wave fell back off the reef in a vast white smother of foam, and when it cleared, there was no ship. There were spars, and planks, and a tangle of rigging draped across the black rocks like weed. A white pennant with a gold sun on it, floating.

And there were men in the water.

He could feel them. Thrashing among the rocks, in the white water, crying out. Some of them were reaching the reef. Some of them were dragging themselves up onto the ledges, among the weed. He felt them clinging there, gasping. Alive.

And he felt the sea under them still heaving. Still wanting.

*They would hang you,* said the voice in him. It sounded like his own. It sounded very reasonable. *They would hang Jac, and the boy, and put the old man back in the galleys to die. They will go home, if you let them, and tell their captains what they saw. A black cutter, and a wave out of a calm sea. And men will come looking. Men in white, with fire in their hands.*

He did not lift another wave. He would tell himself that afterwards. He only let the one he had raised fall back the way it wanted, across the reef, once more, and did not hold it.

When it had gone, the ledges were bare.

---

He let go.

He let go, and the cold went out of him all at once, and he fell. He fell against the wheel and slid down it onto the deck, and lay there on the wet planks with the sky going round above him, and the taste of salt and blood in his mouth, and memories that were not his pouring through him like water through a broken dam. A harbour. White ships. A wave that rose to save the people in the water and could not rise high enough. And this time, at last, it could. This time, it had. He felt the sea's satisfaction go through him like warmth, like wine, like the sun on his back at the end of a long day, and he lay on the deck of the *Slip* and shook, and could not tell whether he was weeping or laughing.

When he could see again, Jac was crouching over him.

She had a pistol in her belt and a cutlass on her hip and Pike's blood on her shirt, and she was staring at him the way you might stare at a lit fuse. Behind her, the crew of the *Slip* stood in a half-circle on the deck, saying nothing. Olaf had his hand on the little brass sun he wore round his neck, and was not singing. Tam's face was white as paper. The elder of the Sardhi brothers made a sign with his fingers, the ward against the evil eye, and then, slowly, after a long moment, did not make the second one.

"Forty men," said Jac Teague, very quietly. "That was forty men, Faro."

"They'd have hanged you."

"Yes," said Jac. "They would." She looked down at his chest. His shirt had come open. The Tidemark was blazing in the noon light, bright enough to see even in the sun, and it had spread: past his elbows now, nearly to his wrists, and up his throat to the hinge of the jaw. "And I'd have taken my chances with the rope over that. I think. I don't know." She stood up. "I don't know."

She went to see to Pike, and did not come back.

Pike lived. They got the splinter out that night, in Jac's cabin, with brandy and a pair of pliers and Olaf holding him down, and he lived. He came on deck a week later, grey and stiff and strapped round the middle with sailcloth, and he came straight to the wheel where Faro was steering, and stood there for a long time without speaking.

"I said I'd go over the side," the old bosun said at last, "sooner than go back to the galleys." His one eye was on the sea. "I meant it. I'd have done it. And now I'm alive, and they're not, and I've been lying in that bunk for a week trying to work out if I'm glad." He was silent. "I'm not, lad. That's the thing. I thought I would be. And I'm not."

He went forward. After that, he still saved Faro the best fish. But he did not stand near the wheel again, on the night watch, unless he had to.

### Later

In the nineteenth year, in Solace, the Count of Cristal attended a service of the Radiant Order in the great Temple of the Noon, because it was the kind of thing the Count of Cristal was seen to do, and afterwards, in the cloister, a woman in widow's black came up to him and put a paper in his hand.

It was a petition. There were a great many of them, in the cloister, every holy day; widows and orphans and beggars pressing them on any rich man who passed. This one was to the High Radiant herself. It asked, in a careful clerk's hand, for the Order to reopen its inquiry into the loss of the King's cutter *Diligence*, wrecked with all forty hands on the Hound's Teeth, in a calm sea, on a clear day, fourteen summers ago. It said that a fisherman of Harnmouth had seen it happen, and had sworn before a magistrate that the sea had stood up like a wall behind her, and that a black cutter with no name had been running ahead of her, and had sailed on west, into the Reach. It said that there were other stories, from the Reach, since then. It asked the High Radiant to find out what was out there, in the west. It was signed by thirty-one women and eleven children, with their marks.

"My husband was her bosun," said the widow. "Will you sign it, my lord? They say you came from the west. They say you know the Reach."

The Count looked at the paper for a long moment.

"I'm sorry for your loss," he said, and gave it back to her unsigned, with a purse, and walked on.

The High Radiant Cassia Vell read the petition that evening, in her rooms in the Sunspire, by the last of the light. She had read it before. It came to her every year. This year, for the first time, she did not put it in the drawer with the others. She sat for a long time with it on her knee, looking west out of her window, towards the sea, and then she sent for the charts of the Shattered Reach.
