# Event 17: Bastien's Vendetta

*Branches from Chapter 18, "Building on Ruins": the night in the harbour tower in the fourth year, when Bastien Corso finishes his story of the box under the tree at Larkmere and Faro sits "with that for a long time" before answering a man who has come to ask for help to kill the High Justicar.*

*The canon path, forbidding it and offering Bastien a stewardship instead, continues in [Chapter 18](../../18-building-on-ruins.md).*

## Allow it

*Heart − · Tide 0*

Faro sat with it for a long time.

The gale howled round the harbour tower. The fire spat. Across the table, Bastien Corso sat with his scarred throat and his black eyes and his big hands round the glass of brandy, and waited, the way a man waits who has been waiting for twenty-five years and can wait a few minutes more.

Faro thought about Corvan Valtor.

He thought about a warm study in Saltmere, and a fire, and a pale, precise young man reading a letter by lamplight, and the moment his face had changed. *I've done you a kindness.* The letter curling in the flames. The handshake. He thought about the Keep, and the dark, and the water coming down the walls, and fourteen years. He thought about a boy hanged on a bridge in front of a crowd because somebody had to be, and a baby in a box under a tree.

He had a plan for Valtor. It was not finished. It had not been finished for four years. He had the shape of it, in the library at the top of the citadel, in notes and letters and the reports of agents: the Valtor household, the paralysed father, the second wife, the daughter, the son. It was a slow plan, and patient, and cold, and at the end of it, in some room in Solace, Corvan Valtor would look up into the face of the man he had buried, and know him. He had dreamed of that face for eighteen years. Not Valtor's face. His own, in Valtor's eyes.

And here was a man who had a better claim.

That was the thing he could not get past. He sat and looked at it and could not get past it. Bastien Corso's brother had died on the Bridge of Lamps twenty-five years ago, when Edric Vane was a boy of thirteen running barefoot on the quays of Saltmere. Bastien had sworn his vendetta before Faro had ever heard the name Valtor. Bastien had climbed the wall at Larkmere and put his knife in the man and dug the child out of the earth with his hands. He had been carrying it longer, and further, and it had cost him more. What right had Edric Vane to tell him to wait, so that the Warden of Cristal could have his reckoning first?

*None,* said the cold. *And yet you want it. Admit that you want it.*

He did. He wanted it very much. He made himself look at how much.

"You'd do it quietly," he said at last.

Bastien's eyes did not change. But something in the big body across the table went still, the way a dog goes still when it hears its name.

"Quietly," said Bastien. "No one would know. Not who did it. Not who sent me."

"No one sends you," said Faro. "That's the point. If I help you, I'm not sending you. I'm only giving you a ship, and money, and papers that say you're a wine merchant from the north coast, and a way into Solace and out again. What you do there is your business. What you swore is your business." He looked down at his own hands, flat on the table. "I won't stop you. I don't think I have the right."

Bastien looked at him for a long time.

"You've a reason of your own," he said slowly. "For hating him. I can see it."

"I have."

"But you'd let me have him."

"He was yours first."

Bastien Corso stood up. He was not a man who made speeches; he stood there by the table in the firelight with his face like a closed door and his hands at his sides, and for a moment Faro thought he was going to say nothing at all. Then he held out his hand across the table, and Faro took it, and Bastien's grip was so hard it hurt.

"I'll not forget it," he said. "Whatever comes after. I'll not forget it, Warden."

He sailed on the morning tide, in a fast lugger from the Cristal fleet with a crew of four who asked no questions, and a hold of good northern wine, and a set of papers in the name of Tobin Ashe, vintner, of Harrowgate. Faro stood on the harbour wall and watched him go. Jac, beside him, said nothing at all, which was not like her.

"You think I'm wrong," said Faro.

"I think," said Jac Teague, "that you've just handed somebody else the one thing you've been living on for four years. And I think you'll find out what that feels like." She turned away. "I've been wrong before."

---

It was six weeks before he looked.

He told himself he would not. He told himself, every night, that it was Bastien's business and not his, that he had given it away and given it away meant gone. And every night he went up to the library, and sat at the table under the lamp, and did not look at the bowl of still water that stood on the shelf by the window.

On the forty-third night, he took it down.

He had not done this before, not with a man so far away. The book said it could be done. *On the Discipline of Still Water: the Seeing.* *All water is one water. The pool in thy bowl and the pool in a far city's fountain are one pool, and a still mind may look through the one into the other, as through a window.* He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. He found the stillness and went down into it, and down, and laid his hands on either side of the bowl, and looked into the water.

For a long time, only his own face, blurred, and the lamp.

Then the cold came, sharper than he had known it, and the salt so strong his eyes watered, and a woman's face that was not his memory, an old Warden on a white terrace weeping, and gone. And the water in the bowl was not his water any more. It was dark, and it moved, and there were lamps in it.

A bridge.

He knew it from the books and the agents' letters before he knew it with his eyes: a high stone span across a ravine in the heart of Solace, with the lower city on one side and the terraces on the other, and iron lamp-posts every twenty paces along its length, each with a lantern of gold glass. The Bridge of Lamps. It was late; the bridge was nearly empty. A link-boy going home. A pair of Radiant priests in white, far off, walking away. And coming across from the terrace side, alone, with his hands clasped behind his back and his head a little bowed, a thin man in a black robe.

He was older. Of course he was older. He was forty-six. His hair had gone grey at the temples and his face had sharpened, grown finer and harder, like a blade that has been honed for twenty years. But he walked the same way, precise and unhurried, every step placed. The High Justicar of Valemont, walking home from the Hall of Justice across the bridge where he had hanged a man's brother, as he did every night, the agents' letters had said, because he liked to walk, and because he was not afraid of anyone in Solace, and had not been for a very long time.

Faro watched him come, and felt the water in the bowl begin to tremble.

Halfway across, under the seventh lamp, a man stepped out of the shadow of the parapet.

Valtor stopped. He did not start, or cry out. He stood quite still and looked at the thick-set, black-browed man in the wine merchant's coat, and Faro saw him look, and saw him not know. Twenty-five years. A face in a crowd outside a court; a shape in the dark of a garden.

"Corvan Valtor," said Bastien Corso.

"Yes," said the High Justicar. His voice came up through the water very faint, very clear, every word placed like a stone. "Do I know you?"

"You hanged my brother. On this bridge. Twenty-five years ago this spring. Matteo Corso. For a Radiant knifed in an alley by the docks. There were six men who saw him in a tavern across the city that night. You didn't call them."

Valtor looked at him for a moment longer. Faro watched his face, and saw something move in it, very slight: not fear. A kind of weariness.

"I've hanged a great many men's brothers," he said. "I don't remember all of them. I'm sorry. Is that what you want me to say?"

"No," said Bastien. "I want you to remember Larkmere."

And that went in. Faro saw it go in. He saw the High Justicar's face, which had held its stillness for twenty years in courtrooms and council chambers and the presence of the King, break across, like ice when a stone hits it, and something look out through the crack: a young man in a garden at midnight with a spade and a lantern and a little box. He saw Valtor's lips move. He could not hear what they said.

Bastien stepped in close, the way a man steps in to embrace a friend, and put his arm round the High Justicar's shoulders.

It was very quick. It was very quiet. Valtor made a small sound, almost surprised, and his knees went, and Bastien held him up, and stood there with him under the seventh lamp for a long moment, holding him, like two men leaning on the parapet to look at the view. Then he lifted him, and tipped him over.

The black robe went down into the dark of the ravine without a sound. Faro did not see it land.

Bastien stood at the parapet for a while longer, looking down. Then he wiped his knife on his sleeve, and put it away, and turned, and walked off the Bridge of Lamps towards the lower city, not fast, with his hands in his pockets, under the golden lanterns, until the dark took him.

---

The water in the bowl was only water. Faro sat in the library at Cristal with his hands on either side of it and the lamp burning low, and found that he was shaking, and that his face was wet, and that he did not know why.

He waited for the sea to rise. He had braced himself for it the whole six weeks: the sweetness, the cold eager surge of the thing that had enjoyed it on the beach. It came. He felt it come up in him, a long slow swell of satisfaction, deep and huge and not his. *Yes. One. One of them, into the dark.*

But under it, in himself, there was something else, and it was not satisfaction at all.

Corvan Valtor had died on a bridge thinking of a box in a garden. He had died knowing Bastien's face, and Matteo Corso's name, and Larkmere. He had not once thought of a warm study in Saltmere, and a boy across the desk, and a letter in the fire. He had not known, at the end, that Edric Vane was alive. He never would.

Faro had given it away. He had said *he was yours first*, and meant it, and it had been the right thing to say; he still thought so. And it had gone out of him across the sea with Bastien in a fast lugger with a hold of wine, and he would never have it back.

*Admit that you wanted it,* said the cold.

"I wanted it," he said aloud, to the empty library, to the bowl, to no one. "I wanted it, and he had a better claim, and I gave it to him." He put his hands over his face. "I'd do it again."

He was not sure, sitting there in the dark, that it was true.

---

Bastien came home a month later, on a grey day, in the lugger, and walked up from the quay to the harbour tower, and stood in front of Faro's table, and said: "It's done."

"I know."

Bastien looked at him, and then at the bowl of still water on the shelf, and understood, and did not ask.

"He didn't know me," he said after a while. "Not at first. Twenty-five years, and I'd thought of him every day, and he didn't know my face." He was quiet. "He asked who sent me. At the end. I said nobody." He looked at Faro with his black eyes. "Nobody did."

"Nobody did," Faro agreed.

There was a silence.

"I thought I'd feel it," said Bastien Corso. He said it quite flatly, without self-pity, like a man reporting the weather. "Twenty-five years. I thought it'd be like putting down a weight." He looked at his own hands. "It's not. It's like putting down a weight and finding you can't stand up straight without it." And then, after a moment: "There's a Radiant inquiry. The King's ordered it. They're looking for a vintner from Harrowgate called Tobin Ashe, who left Solace by sea the morning after." He met Faro's eyes. "They'll not find him. But they'll look."

### Later

There were Two Hands, when the Count of Cristal came to Solace in the spring. Everyone still called them the Three, from habit, and then remembered, and looked uncomfortable. The High Justicar's chain had gone to his deputy, a dry, careful, colourless man named Ormond Treece who had no enemies and no ideas, and the Radiant inquiry into the death on the Bridge of Lamps had found nothing, and stopped finding it, and moved its attention instead, slowly and coldly, westward, towards the smugglers of the Reach and the island they said had a harbour now.

The great grey house on the Street of Lanterns was quiet. Edric went to dine there in the summer, as he had always meant to. The widow was there: small, plump, fair, charming, in half-mourning, with quick pale-blue eyes that missed nothing. But her husband's fortune had gone where the law said it must, to his daughter, at once, in trust under the old Marquis Aubray, and the girl had gone north to her mother's people, and Heloise Valtor had her widow's portion and the house and her son, and that was all, and there was nothing she could do about it. She kept a stillroom at the top of the house. She made cordials for headaches. She asked the Count, over dessert, whether it was true the Sardhi had drugs that brought deep sleep; and he looked at her, and at the fat solemn little boy of nine beside her, and said that he had never heard of any.

The boy asked him if it was true that he had killed a sea-monster with his bare hands. Edric said it was not quite true. He did not show him anything.

Lyse Valtor married Maxim Moren that autumn, in the north, with her grandfather's blessing blinked out letter by letter on a board. Nobody needed to fake her death. Nobody needed to die in a stillroom. And there was no trial in the Great Court, and no handsome prisoner in the dock telling the court where he was born; Edric found Andric anyway, in the gambling den by the docks, and looked at him for an hour across a card table, and could think of nothing to use him for, and paid him to leave Solace. He went, smiling. Edric did not know where.

Some nights he sat in the study of the house with the blue door and thought about the stillroom he had never stood in, and the boy he had never seen on the floor, and the old man who had never had cause to blink *enough*. And in the autumn, when word came from the Greyhills that the Lord Treasurer had paid the last of what he had stolen for a heel of bread, and had been sitting in a cave without food for two days, he read it at his desk, and felt nothing at all, and put it down, and picked up his pen to write that Rook should wait another day.

It was Hayda who took the pen out of his hand. She did not say anything. She only looked at him, until he looked away, and got up, and called for his horse.

## Promise him Valtor's ruin instead

*Heart − · Tide 0*

Faro sat with it for a long time.

"Don't kill him," he said at last.

Bastien's face did not change. But Faro felt him close, like a door swinging shut in a wind.

"Hear me out," said Faro. "Then go, if you like. I'll not stop you." He leaned forward across the table, into the firelight. "Killing is quick. You climb a wall, you wait in the dark, you put your knife in him, and it's over in a breath. He doesn't suffer. He doesn't lose anything. He's the High Justicar of Valemont one moment and a dead man the next, and they'll bury him in the Temple of the Noon with the King's own priests singing over him and a stone with his name in gold. That's what you'd give him. A quick death and a fine funeral."

Bastien said nothing. But he was listening.

"I can give you something better," said Faro. "I can give you everything he has. His name. His office. His house. His wife, and his children, and the love of every one of them. The good opinion of every lord in Solace. The ear of the King. Every single thing that he has built for twenty-five years on your brother's body and that child's grave. I can take it all from him, piece by piece, slowly, so that he feels every piece go. And at the end, when there's nothing left, when he's standing in the ruins of all of it with nothing in his hands, you'll be there." He held the black eyes. "You'll be the one who tells him why."

The fire crackled. The gale howled round the tower.

"Can you do that?" said Bastien Corso.

"Yes."

He did not know, when he said it, whether it was true. He knew a great deal about Corvan Valtor, from four years of agents and letters and gold, and nothing at all about how to do what he had just promised. But he felt the sea in him rise as he said it, cold and interested and very sure, and he felt, for a moment, that there was nothing he could not do.

Bastien looked at him for a long time.

"You've a reason of your own," he said slowly. "For hating him."

"I have."

"And you'd share it with me."

"I'd give you the last of it," said Faro. "The end. The part I've been saving." He was quiet a moment. "That's a promise. Before the end, you'll see him fall. And you'll be the one to tell him who pushed him, and why."

He heard himself say it, and knew, as he said it, that he had gone further than he meant. He had meant to offer the man a place, and a purpose, and a reason to put the knife away. He had meant to say *I can't promise you'll be the one to push him*. And instead he had reached into the man with Undertow, without quite meaning to, and found the thing that pulled hardest in him, the vendetta, twenty-five years deep and polished like a blade, and he had taken hold of it. And promised it everything it wanted.

Bastien Corso stood up.

He drew his knife. Faro did not move. He watched the big scarred hand draw the long blade from the belt, the knife his family had used for three hundred years, and lay it flat across his own left palm, and close the fingers over it, and draw it out. Blood ran down his wrist and dripped on the table.

"In my family," said Bastien, "a vendetta is sworn in blood and salt. It doesn't end until it's done. Not for death, not for marriage, not for the Sun himself." He held out his bleeding hand across the table, palm up. There was a little dish of salt by the brandy, for the fish they had eaten earlier; he took a pinch of it and dropped it into the cut, and did not flinch. "If you'll take this, you take my vendetta. It's yours, and mine. And I'm yours, Warden, until it's done. My knife is yours. Not the King's, not my own. Yours."

*An oath,* said the sea, very deep, very far. *Blood and salt. I know oaths, little one. I have heard every one that was ever sworn over water. Take it.*

Faro looked at the hand.

He thought: *I should say no. I should say I want a steward, not a sworn knife.* He thought of Faro in the cell, saying *the moment it becomes sweet*. He thought of Corvan Valtor holding a letter over a fire.

He took the knife from the table, and cut his own palm, and put salt in it, and took Bastien Corso's hand.

---

He found out, in the months that followed, what he had bought.

Bastien was the best steward a man ever had. He knew Solace: every street, every lord, every clerk who could be paid and every one who could not. He knew Larkmere. He went to the mainland as Faro's agent and came back with things nobody else could have found: the name of the midwife who had attended a married lady at Larkmere twenty-three years ago, the name of the married lady, the house in Solace where she lived now as somebody else's wife. He found the gambling den by the docks where a handsome young man with a southern accent had been fleecing merchants' sons, and stood outside in the alley, and did not go in, and came back to Cristal and sat in the harbour tower for a whole night without speaking. He worked harder than anyone on the island. He never asked for anything. And he never, ever, let Faro forget.

*When?* he would say, sometimes, in the library, very quietly, when the others had gone. Not impatient. Only reminding. *When, Warden?*

*In the spring.*

In the autumn of the fourth year, a man came to Cristal on a coasting trader and took a room at the harbour tavern and asked questions. He was a pleasant, harmless, talkative man, a cloth-merchant, he said, from Solace, and he asked about the Warden. What he looked like. Where he came from. Whether it was true he had money, a great deal of it, from somewhere, and whether anyone knew where. He asked a good many people. He asked Big Olaf, who told him it was the Sun.

Three days later, the Bettan boys found him in the harbour at dawn, floating face down under the stilt-houses.

Nobody saw how he had got there. The council was told he had been drunk, and fallen off the quay in the dark, and nobody disputed it. But Faro went down to the harbour tower that morning and found Bastien sitting by the cold fire, cleaning his knife, and stood in the door, and looked at him.

"He was the High Justicar's man," said Bastien, without looking up. "I knew his face. He's been in Valtor's pay for ten years, in the provinces. Asking questions for him." He turned the blade to the window. "If he'd gone home with your name, Warden, there'd have been no spring. Valtor would have had you in the Keep again before you ever set foot in Solace. And the promise would have been broken."

He looked up then. His black eyes were quite calm.

"I'll not let anything break it," he said. "Not anything. You've my oath."

Faro stood in the doorway for a long moment. He thought of a dozen things to say, and every one of them was a thing he had no right to say to a man he had cut his own hand to swear with.

He went back up to the library and sat at the table with his palm open in front of him, looking at the thin pink scar across it, and understood for the first time what he had made, and that it pointed exactly where he had pointed it, and would not stop.

### Later

Corvan Valtor did not die. He went mad, in the end, on a wet afternoon after the storm, on his knees in the little grey walled garden behind his house, digging in the earth with his bare hands for the children he had put in the dark. And Edric, kneeling in the broken glass of the stillroom upstairs beside a dead boy with a wooden horse in his hand, heard an old man's eyes say *enough*, and believed them.

Bastien did not.

Edric found him in the garden an hour after midnight, in the rain, standing over the madman with his knife drawn. Valtor did not look up. He was still digging, in the mud under the bare little tree, muttering, with his black robe soaked through and his fingernails torn.

"Bastien," said Edric. "It's done."

"It's not done." Bastien did not turn round. "You promised me. Blood and salt. I'm to tell him why. That was the promise." His voice was very low and very steady. "He doesn't know. He doesn't know who dug the boy up. He doesn't know whose brother hanged. He's down there in the mud thinking it was all *him*, his own doing, his own sin, the poor bastard, like a tragedy in a play. It wasn't. It was me. It was you and me. And he's going to know it before I let him go."

"Look at him," said Edric. "He can't know anything. There's nothing left to tell."

"Then I'll tell what's left." Bastien turned at last. His face in the rain was like a shut door, and behind the door, for the first time since the harbour tower, Edric felt something that frightened him. "You don't get to stop, Warden. Not because the child died. Not because you've had enough. You took my vendetta. It doesn't end for death, or marriage, or the Sun himself. You don't get to put it down because it went sour in your hand."

They stood in the rain in the little garden for a long time, with the madman digging between them.

In the end it was not Edric who stopped it. It was Valtor, who stopped digging, and looked up at Bastien Corso with his wild red eyes, and held up his muddy hands, empty, like a child, and said, quite clearly, in his old precise voice: "Have you seen my son? I put him here. I've looked everywhere."

Bastien stood very still. Then he put his knife away, slowly, and knelt down in the mud beside the man who had hanged his brother, and said: "Yes. I found him. A long time ago. He's all right." And stayed there, kneeling, in the rain, until the madman went back to digging.

When he stood up he walked past Edric without looking at him. At the garden gate he stopped.

"There," he said. "That's the end of it. I told him." He did not turn round. "Don't ever promise me anything again, Warden."

He was at the Keep, at the end, at the oar beside Aren Morrow, and did not miss a stroke. But he never called Edric *Warden* again.
