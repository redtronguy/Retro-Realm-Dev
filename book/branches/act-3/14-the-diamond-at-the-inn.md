## Give Cade the diamond, and pay a guard to watch him

*Heart + · Tide 0*

He got as far as the Sallow dyke.

The rain had thickened while he sat by the fire, and the road ran ahead of him into a grey nothing of reeds and water, and the brass lantern bumped against his chest at every step. He did not look back at the Dry Well. He had put the stone into Cade Rusk's hand and let it find whatever was in the man, as the sea finds the weak plank in a hull. That had been the whole idea. A test. If there was anything in Cade worth saving, the diamond would find it. If there was not, the diamond would find that too.

At the crown of the dyke, where the old sluice-gate stood rotting in the reeds, he stopped.

He had not meant to reach. But the habit was in him now, the way the habit of reading the sky is in a sailor, and he felt the inn behind him without trying. Three hundred yards back, he felt the people.

Cade was a muddle. Fear and want and shame went round and round in him like scum in an eddy, and none of them pulled very hard in any one direction. Cade had never pulled hard in any direction in his life.

The woman was different.

Agna Rusk pulled like a drain: a single cold, steady, purposeful draw, all the water in the room bending towards it. There was no muddle in her. She wanted one thing. She had wanted it for a very long time, through years of coughing and flea-ridden beds and a husband who drank the stock. Now there was a stone the size of a quail's egg in her husband's fist, and a jeweller in Fendle who did not ask questions, and a week.

*Let it find what is in them,* said the sea, very far down. It was not quite the Deep's voice; only the shape the cold made in his thoughts when he stood still. *That was the bargain you made with yourself. A test. Let it run.*

He stood on the dyke in the rain and thought about the test.

He had told himself he was offering Cade Rusk the choice nobody had offered Edric Vane: here is the means to do good, do some. But a test is only a test if a man can pass it. He thought about the yellow eyes in the doorway, and a jeweller coming up the market road next week with his strongbox, a man who had done nothing to Edric Vane and never would. He thought about what fifty thousand marks does in a house where a woman has been counting other people's purses for twenty years.

It was not a test. It was a trap with a stone for bait. And he had baited it, and walked away so he would not have to see it close.

*He watched them write it,* said the cold. *He went home to bed.*

"Yes," said the priest aloud, to the rain. "And I'm not him."

He turned round, and walked back past the Dry Well on the far side of the road, with his head down, without stopping, and went on north the five miles to Fendle.

---

The Crossed Keys at Fendle was everything the Dry Well was not: warm, loud, lit, full of carters steaming by the fire and a landlady who put a bowl of mutton in front of a Brother of the Lantern without asking whether he could pay. He ate it, and sat with his back to the wall and his face held soft and dull, and watched the room, and reached.

It took him an hour to find the right man.

He was sitting alone at the end of the long table: a broad, heavy, grey-whiskered man of fifty or so, with a stiff leg stuck out straight in front of him and a quarterstaff leaning against the bench. The landlady called him Sergeant. He had been a sergeant of the marsh-road watch until a drover's bull broke his knee, and now the stallholders at Fendle market paid him to keep the peace on market days, and he had never once been known to take a bribe to look the other way.

The priest felt him, and found him the way you find good timber. There was a debt in him, a small, nagging one: a daughter in Harnmouth, a dowry he could not make up. There was a weariness, the stiff knee aching in the wet. And under it, plain and solid as a keel, nothing much else. No rot. No drain. A man who would do what he said he would do.

The priest went and sat down across from him.

"Sergeant," he said. "My name is Brother Faro. I'd like to hire you."

The sergeant looked at him, at the grey robe and the bare muddy feet and the brass lantern, and his eyebrows went up. "Hire me, Brother? With what? Prayers?"

The priest put a small, heavy purse on the table. The sergeant opened it a crack, and his face did not change at all, which the priest liked.

"That's a year's wages," said the sergeant quietly. "Two."

"There's a Sardhi jeweller," said the priest, "who comes up the market road from the south every month, and buys stones, and doesn't ask where they came from. He'll be through next week. When he stops at the Dry Well on the Sallow dyke, and he will stop there, I want you to stop there too. Take a room. Drink their sour beer. And watch the landlord."

"Cade Rusk?" The sergeant snorted. "Rusk couldn't hurt a fly if it sat on his nose and insulted his mother."

"Then watch whoever can. If anyone in that house lifts a hand against anyone, I want you to stop it, and send for the magistrate's men here in Fendle, and let the law have them." The priest paused. "Whoever it is."

The sergeant's small shrewd eyes tried to get hold of the priest's face, and slid off.

"Why?" said the sergeant. "What's a Brother of the Lantern want with Cade Rusk and a Sardhi jeweller?"

"I gave a man something," said the priest, "that he isn't strong enough to carry. I can't take it back. I can only see that it doesn't fall on anyone else."

The sergeant was quiet a long moment. Then he put the purse inside his coat.

"Bram Tolly," he said, and held out his hand, a big square hand, hard as a plank. "I'll watch him for you, Brother. And whoever else needs watching." He paused. "You'll want to know how it goes?"

"I'll know," said the priest.

---

It cost him a week.

He had not planned for a week. There were bills of exchange in his case, drawn on the house of Moren, and a man in an office on Harbour Street who had perhaps two months left before the first of them fell due, and every day the priest spent on the marsh road was a day he did not spend on that. He slept in the hayloft of a farm two miles from the Dry Well, eating bread he begged at back doors to keep the face honest, holding the stillness hour after hour until the cold went into his bones and stayed there.

On the seventh night, the jeweller came.

The priest felt him before he saw him: a small dry careful current coming up the road from the south in a hired gig, with a heavy iron box under the seat and a man's fear wrapped around it like a blanket. He stopped at the Dry Well because it was raining again, and the Crossed Keys was five miles further.

An hour after him, a heavy man with a stiff leg limped in out of the rain and asked for a bed.

The priest went and stood in the reeds on the far side of the Sallow dyke, across the road from the inn, in the dark, with the rain running down his shaved head, and waited.

He felt it all. He did not need to see. The jeweller and Cade in the taproom, the scales on the table, the stone under the lamp; the long silence, the offer, the jeweller's dry voice saying a number, *forty-five thousand*, and the great slow lurch of want in Cade as he heard it, like a ship heeling. The money counted out; the strongbox closed. The house going to bed. The jeweller's room at the top of the stair, the bolt shot on the door, which was a poor bolt in a poor door in a poor house. Tolly's room across the passage. Cade, drunk on his own beer and his own luck, asleep within minutes, snoring.

And Agna Rusk, lying awake beside him in the dark, with her heart going like a bird's.

At a little after one, she got up.

He felt her go down to the kitchen. He felt her stand there a long time, at the table, by the knife block. He felt her want gather itself, and steady, and turn hard and cold and simple, the way his own had turned a week ago in the taproom, listening to Cade: *it would be easy, it would be nothing.* He felt her take the long knife.

He felt something else, too. In himself. Rising.

*Let her,* said the cold. *Let it close. You did not make her. You did not put the knife in her hand. She will go up those stairs, and the jeweller will die, and they will take Cade Rusk to the galleys or the rope, and he will have earned it fourteen years ago in the Three Anchors. Is that not justice? Is that not what you came out of the sea for? Stand still, little one. Only stand still. You need do nothing at all.*

He stood very still in the reeds.

*You need do nothing at all.*

He thought of a man with his head on a table in the Three Anchors, waking at midnight, seeing the corner empty, and going home to bed.

He did not reach. He did not need to: he had already done the one thing he could do, a week ago, in the Crossed Keys, with a purse of gold. Whatever happened now would happen without the sea in it. He stood in the reeds and held the stillness and let himself feel all of it, and did not let it become sweet.

Agna went up the stair.

She went slowly, on the outside edge of each tread, where the boards did not creak. She went past her own door. She reached the top. She put her thin yellow hand on the jeweller's door, and felt the bolt, and drew a breath.

And across the passage, another door opened, and a lantern came out of it, and a big square hand closed over her wrist.

---

He heard it from the road. Everyone within a mile must have heard it.

Agna screamed. It was not a frightened scream; it was rage, pure and high, like a gull's, and it went on and on. The jeweller's voice, shrill behind his door, crying out in Sardhi. Something falling. And Tolly's voice, very calm, very loud, the voice of a sergeant of the watch: *Drop it, mistress. Drop it now, or I'll break the arm and you'll drop it anyway.* The clatter of the knife on the boards.

Then a door, and stumbling feet, and Cade's voice, thick with sleep and beer: "What? What is it? Agna? What's—"

The priest crossed the road. He did not mean to. He stood in the yard of the Dry Well under the creaking sign, in the dark, and through the crack of the shutter he could see the stairhead in the lantern light: the jeweller in his nightshirt in his doorway, clutching his strongbox; Tolly, heavy and patient, with the woman's wrist pinned in one fist and the lantern in the other; Agna, twisting, her shawl fallen, her face barely a face at all. And Cade, at the bottom of the stairs, in a dirty nightshirt, with his mouth open.

"Cade," Agna was saying. "Cade, tell him. Tell him I was going to the privy. Tell him it's a mistake. Tell him, you fat useless—it's *ours*, Cade, ninety thousand in that box, *ours*, tell him—"

"Landlord," said Bram Tolly, without looking round. "Did you know of this?"

And Cade Rusk stood at the bottom of his own stairs, with the whole of his life behind him like a road he had walked with his eyes on his feet, and said nothing.

The silence went on, one breath, two, three, the old habit closing over Cade like water over a dropped stone. Fear of her. Fear of the sergeant. Fear of being seen, of being *involved*. He knew exactly what Cade would do. He had always known.

Cade opened his mouth.

"She told me," he said. His voice cracked. "Last week. After the priest came. She said the jeweller'd have more in his box than he paid for the stone. She said we could have it all. And I said no." He swallowed. "I said no, and then I went to sleep. I said no and I went to sleep." He looked up the stairs at his wife, and his soft wet face was crumpling. "She meant it, sergeant. She's been meaning it all week. Take her."

Agna stopped screaming.

She looked down at her husband from the top of the stairs as if she had never seen him before. Then she spat, very precisely, and it fell on the step at his feet.

Out in the yard, in the dark, the priest let out a breath he did not know he had been holding. In for eight. Out for eight. His hands, inside the sleeves of the grey robe, were shaking. He did not know why.

---

The magistrate's men came from Fendle at dawn with a cart, and took Agna Rusk away in it with her hands tied. The jeweller left an hour later, very fast, without paying for his room. Tolly limped out last, with his quarterstaff on his shoulder, and stopped in the road, and looked across at the reeds on the far side of the dyke for a while, and nodded once to nobody, and walked on north.

Cade Rusk sat on the step of the Dry Well in the grey light, in his nightshirt and a coat, with forty-five thousand marks in a leather bag between his feet that nobody had taken from him, and looked at the empty road.

Once, he looked up, and saw a figure on the crown of the dyke by the old sluice-gate, grey and still and blurred by the drizzle, so that he could not tell whether it was a man or a post.

When he looked again, there was nobody there.

### Later

Five years later, on an afternoon in Solace, a man knocked at the front door of the house with the blue door and asked to see the Count of Cristal.

Bastien nearly sent him away. He was fat, still, but less fat, and grey, and sober, in a decent brown coat that had been brushed for the occasion; he held his hat in both hands and would not give his name. He said he kept an inn on the marsh road. He said it was a matter of the Count's safety. And he said, when Bastien put a hand on the door, that he would wait on the step all night if he had to, because he had once failed to say a thing when it mattered and did not mean to do it twice.

Edric saw him in the study. He held the Count's face, still and foreign and courteous, and Cade Rusk stood on the carpet turning his hat round and round and told him what he had come to tell. That the Dry Well was a decent house now; the beds had no fleas and the stew had meat in it, and the carters stopped there again. That two men had drunk in his taproom a week ago, on their way north to Solace: one young and handsome with a southern voice, one older, with galley-scars on his wrists, who had got drunk and boasted. A house with a blue door. A strongroom behind the study. A fortune in gold, and a window by a fig tree, and a fine southern lord who would wait in the alley and help carry it off.

"I don't know who you are, my lord," said Cade, not looking at him. "It's not my business who you are. I only know a priest told me once to balance the account. I've been trying." He put on his hat. "I'm trying."

He went out by the front door. Edric stood at the window and watched him go down the Street of Lanterns, and did not call him back, and did not let his face change.

That night, when the galley-man came through the study window, puffing, he found a priest in the chair by the cold fireplace, and the door to the strongroom locked, and Bastien behind him; and he did not go out the way he had come. He stayed until morning and talked, a great deal, to save his neck. And in the alley under the fig tree, Lord Andric Cavell, who had waited three hours in the dark with a knife in his sleeve for a partner who never came down the wall, was taken by six men of the Crown Guard with the knife still in his hand.

They tried him for escape, and for the old Harrowgate sentence, and for the plot on the Count's strongroom, and the High Justicar prosecuted the case himself. And when the old judge asked the prisoner to state his parentage, the prisoner smiled his smile like the sun coming out, and told them.

No one died under the fig tree that night. Edric thought about that, afterwards, more often than he thought about the trial.
