# Event 11: Who May Live in Cristal?

*Branches from Chapter 18, "Building on Ruins": in the third year, when the council of the twelve terraces meets under the tree in the Court of Tides and, as it always does, "brought the ones they could not agree on to the Warden".*

## The applicants

They came on the same ship, which was nobody's fault, and on the same morning, which was Jac's.

"I could have brought them one at a time," she said, standing in the doorway of the harbour tower with the rain running off her hat. "Over a month. Let you get used to the idea. But I thought, no. I thought, he says he wants a city for the people nobody wants. Let's see what he does when nobody wants them."

The council met that afternoon in the Court of Tides, under the tree. It was an old holm oak, black and twisted, that had grown up through the paving in the three hundred years the citadel stood empty, and pushed the great flagstones aside like a man shrugging off a blanket, and nobody had had the heart to cut it down. The twelve councillors sat on a ring of stone benches in its shade: smugglers and fishermen and a Sardhi spice-trader and a former galley slave and old Mother Bettan of the Weir-folk, so bent now that she sat folded over her stick like a heron on one leg. The people of the terraces stood round the edges of the court, as many as could fit, which was a great many. Word had gone round.

Faro sat on the step of the old fountain, under the tree, where the council had put a chair for him that he never used. And the applicants came in one by one, and stood on the paving in front of him, and told him who they were.

---

The first was a man of about forty, lean and grey and quiet, with big scarred hands and pale eyes, and nothing with him but the clothes he stood in and a canvas bag. His name, he said, was Ansel Stroud. Half the smugglers in the court already knew it. Faro saw them know it, saw the ripple go round the edge of the crowd, and men step back.

"I've killed people," Stroud said, quite simply. He did not look at the crowd; he looked at Faro. "For money, mostly. In Corrow and Harnmouth and up the coast. Seven men and a woman. I can give you their names, if you want them. I remember all of them." His voice did not change. "I stopped two years ago. I don't know why. I woke up one morning and couldn't do it any more. And since then I've been trying to find somewhere I can stop being that man, and there isn't anywhere. Everyone knows my face. Somebody's always got a grudge, or a job, or a bounty." He looked down at his hands. "They say you take anyone. They say you don't ask what people were. I'd like to fish. That's all. I used to fish, when I was a boy. I'd like to do that again, until I die."

"And if someone comes for you here?" said one of the smugglers on the council. "With a grudge? With a bounty? You bring that to our door."

Stroud looked at him with his pale eyes. "Yes," he said. "I do."

---

The second was a family: a man and a woman and two children, a boy of ten and a girl of six, all four of them in plain grey travelling clothes, all four of them standing very close together. The man was tall and fair and broad-shouldered, and stood the way soldiers stand, and when he took off his hat, there was a pale band across his forehead where a helmet had kept the sun off for years.

"Corin Ashe," he said. "Once a knight of the Radiant Order. Lately of the beacon at Fallow Point, on the south coast." He did not say it proudly. "This is my wife, Mareth. My son, Aldo. My daughter, Wren."

The court went silent. Faro felt it go silent. He felt, too, something he had not expected, faintly, like heat on his face from an open oven door: a warmth coming off the man, the ghost of the sunfire he had carried. It did not hurt, quite. But it was there, and the Tidemark on his chest under his shirt prickled with it, the way skin prickles near a fire.

"Last spring," said Corin Ashe, "I was ordered to burn a village. A fishing village, on the coast near Fallow, that had stopped paying the beacon-tithe because it had no fish to pay it with. There was an old woman there who could make stone sweat. Just that. She could put her hand on a wall and make water run down it. The village had kept her for sixty years, and said nothing, and in the spring someone told the Order." He was quiet a moment. "I went with my lance. I saw the old woman. She was sitting in her doorway, waiting. She'd been waiting her whole life. And I found that I couldn't." He looked at his children. "So I came home, and took them, and ran. They hang deserters. They hang their families too, sometimes, to make a point. We've been running since the spring."

From the bench where she sat folded over her stick, old Mother Bettan of the Weir-folk spat on the paving.

"They burned my grandmother's sister," she said, in the Weir-tongue, to nobody, to everybody. "Men in white with fire in their hands. I was nine."

Corin Ashe did not understand the words. But he understood the spitting. He bowed his head, and did not argue, and his small daughter put her hand into his.

---

The third was a clerk. There was no other word for him. A small, thin, ink-stained man of fifty with spectacles and a frayed black coat and a satchel that he held against his chest with both arms, as if someone might snatch it, and he stood on the paving in front of the Warden and shook.

"Merrit Fell," he said. "Of Saltmere. A clerk. In the counting-house of Abernay and Sons, on Harbour Street, for twenty-six years." He swallowed. "My wife had the blight-cough. Four years ago. I borrowed for the physicians. Two hundred marks, from Abernay's, at a fair rate, and I was paying it. And then she died anyway, and Abernay's went bankrupt, and the debt was sold." His hands tightened on the satchel. "To the house of Quell. In Solace."

Faro did not move. But under his shirt, over his heart, the cold turned over once, like a fish in deep water.

"They bought every debt on the coast that year," said Merrit Fell. "Every one. For a copper in the mark. And then they changed the terms, which they can do, the law says they can do, and two hundred marks became two thousand, and then four, and then they sent men. They put my neighbour in the debtors' prison at Saltmere. He died there in the winter." The clerk's spectacles were misting. "I ran. I've nothing. I can keep accounts. I can keep them very well. I know how the house of Quell does it, sir, I've seen the papers, I've copied them; I've copies here." He held up the satchel. "Please. They'll find me anywhere on the mainland. Baron Quell has agents in every port."

"He's right," said Jac, from the edge of the court. "They will. Quell's men have been asking after him in Corrow. A clerk with spectacles. They'll come here next. And they won't come asking."

---

The council argued until dark.

They argued about the murderer, and whether a man could stop being what he had been, and who would pay if he couldn't. They argued about the Radiant, and whether a man who had carried sunfire could ever be trusted on an island where the Warden's power came from the sea, and whether he was a spy, and whether his children were. They argued about the clerk, and Baron Quell's agents, and whether the island could afford to make an enemy of the richest banker in Valemont before it had finished building its walls. Mother Bettan said nothing more at all. She sat on her bench folded over her stick and looked at Corin Ashe's little girl with eyes like black stones.

When the lamps were lit in the Court of Tides, the council fell silent, one by one, and looked at the man on the step of the fountain under the tree.

They had not agreed. They never did, on the ones that mattered. That was what he was for.

He stood up.

"All three," he said.

There was a noise from the crowd round the edges of the court. Not quite a protest. Something lower, and more uneasy, like the sound a ship makes when the wind shifts.

"All three," said Faro again, and let his voice carry, the way he had learned to let it carry on a deck in a gale. "Every one of you here came to this island because there was nowhere else that would have you. I know. I've heard your stories. I've heard them in the harbour tower, every night, for three years. Runaways from the galleys. Thieves who wanted to stop being thieves. Debtors. Smugglers. Weir-folk turned out of the marsh." He looked at Mother Bettan. "Sardhi who prayed to the wrong god. If I'd asked every one of you, on the quay, what you'd been, and turned away the ones whose answers frightened me, half of you wouldn't be standing here."

"A murderer, Warden," said the smuggler on the council. "A *Radiant*."

"A man who stopped killing," said Faro, "and a man who wouldn't burn an old woman in her doorway. I don't know two better reasons to let a man in." He turned to Stroud. "You'll fish. Bettan's sons need a hand on the boats. If anyone comes for you with a grudge, you'll come to me before you do anything else. If you ever lift a hand to anyone on this island, you'll leave it the same day, and you won't come back." He turned to Corin Ashe. "You'll keep your hands in your pockets, and your fire with them. You'll live on the fourth terrace, at the top, away from the Weir, and your children will go to Tam's school with everyone else's." And to the clerk: "And you'll keep the island's accounts. God knows nobody else can."

Merrit Fell burst into tears. He stood on the paving of the Court of Tides with his satchel clutched to his chest and his spectacles in his hand and wept like a child, without any shame at all, and after a moment the Sardhi spice-trader on the council got up from his bench, and went to him, and put an arm round his shoulders, and led him to sit down.

Corin Ashe looked at him for a long moment, and then bowed, very low, the way knights bow to kings. Faro did not like it. He did not say so.

Ansel Stroud only nodded, once, and picked up his canvas bag.

---

Corin Ashe came to the harbour tower that night, alone, after his children were asleep in the empty house on the fourth terrace. He stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands, and did not come in until he was asked, and then sat on the very edge of the chair across the table, as if he expected to be told to leave it.

"I felt it," he said. "In the court. When I came near you." He looked at the Warden's collar, buttoned to the throat. "We're taught to. In the Order. From the first year. The sunfire knows what's touched by the sea. It's how we find them, the old women who make stone sweat." He was quiet a moment. "I've never felt anything like you. It was like standing next to the ocean in the dark."

"And yet you came," said Faro.

"And yet I came." Corin Ashe turned his hat round in his hands. "I wanted you to know that I know. So you wouldn't wonder. So you'd never have to wonder, every time I came up the stair, whether the Radiant had worked it out yet." He met Faro's eyes. "I'll never tell anyone. Not the Order, not my wife, not anyone. Whatever you are. Whatever happens."

"Why?"

The knight thought about it, honestly, for a long time.

"Because you could have said no," he said at last. "In front of all those people. It would have been easy. They wanted you to. I could feel that too." He stood up, and put on his hat. "Nobody's ever done anything for me that was hard, before. I find I don't know what to do with it except keep it."

He went out. Faro sat in the harbour tower for a long time after the door closed, with his hand flat on the table, and under his shirt, over his heart, the cold lay very still, as if it were listening.

---

It went wrong within the week, as he had known it would.

Somebody threw a stone through the window of the house on the fourth terrace on the third night. On the fifth, somebody painted a gold sun on the door, and a gallows under it. On the sixth, three of the Weir-folk's young men got drunk on Sardhi brandy and went up the stair after midnight with a can of lamp oil and a lantern, to give the Radiant, they said afterwards, a taste of his own.

Faro felt them go. He felt it the way he felt everything on the island now, through the water: the cisterns and the rain-butts and the damp in the stone, the sweat on the young men's skin, the oil sloshing in its can. He was in the library. He was on the fourth terrace before they were.

They stopped when they saw him, on the stair below the house, with the lantern light on his face. They stood there, three boys of eighteen, with the oil can and the lantern, swaying, and the eldest of them was a Bettan, one of the old woman's great-grandsons, and his face was wet.

"He's one of them," the boy said. "The ones who burned Gran's people. You let him in. You let one of them in, Warden. How could you?"

Behind Faro, the door of the house opened, and Corin Ashe stood in it, in his shirt, with his wife behind him and his children behind her. His hands were at his sides. Faro felt the warmth come off him, the sunfire, the ghost of it, rising like heat off a stone. He could have burned the stair, the boys, the oil, all of it, in a single breath. He did not move.

"Go home," said Faro to the boys. "Go home, and sleep, and come and see me in the morning, and we'll talk about it."

They went. The Bettan boy went last, looking back.

Corin Ashe stood in the doorway, watching them go, for a long time. Then he said, without turning round, "You'll lose them, for this. The Weir-folk. Some of them."

"Some of them," said Faro.

"Is it worth it? For four strangers?"

He did not answer. He did not know. He went back down the stair in the dark, and the next morning, the Bettan boy came to the harbour tower and sat across the table from him for an hour and said almost nothing, and in the end went away without being forgiven, because he had not asked to be.

That month, four Weir-folk families took their boats and went back east, to the marsh, to a Weir that had no fish. Mother Bettan did not go with them. But she did not speak to Faro again until the spring.

### Later

In the nineteenth year, when the King's fleet came out of the east with white sails and gold suns and the Radiants came ashore in boats with sunfire burning in their hands, a fair-haired man in a fisherman's smock was standing on the harbour wall of Cristal among Jac's watch, with a spear in his hand that he did not use.

He held up his other hand instead. And light came out of it.

The Radiants on the first boat saw it and faltered at their oars. They had never seen sunfire turned against them. They had not known it could be. Corin Ashe stood on the wall with his hand raised and the light pouring out of it, golden and terrible, and the light from the boats struck his and broke against it like waves against a rock, and he held it, for as long as a man can hold his breath, and then for longer, until the skin of his hands blistered and his eyes streamed and his son, thirteen years old now, came running along the wall to hold him up.

Merrit Fell was not on the wall. He was in Solace, in a room in the house on the Street of Lanterns, with every ledger of the house of Quell that he had copied over twenty-six years spread out on the table in front of him, showing the Count of Cristal exactly where a banker's money went when it did not want to be seen. Ansel Stroud was dead by then. He had died in the fourth winter, on the shore below the Weir, between a blighted thing and a Bettan child of five, with a fish-knife in his hand. He had not run. Mother Bettan had sat by his body until dawn, and in the morning she came to the harbour tower for the first time in a year, and sat down across the table from Faro, and said, in the Weir-tongue, "Well. You were right about that one."

## Turn them away

He sat on the step of the fountain for a long time, in the lamplight, under the tree, and looked at them.

He looked at the murderer, with his scarred hands and his canvas bag, who wanted to fish. He looked at the Radiant, with the ghost of the sunfire coming off him like heat from an oven, and his wife, and his son, and his daughter with her hand in his. He looked at the clerk, shaking, clutching his satchel of copied papers. And he thought about the island.

Four thousand people. A harbour, a market, a school. Walls that were not finished, and a watch of forty, and blighted things out of the sea every dark of the moon. A city that had existed for three years, on the edge of the world, by the grace of the fact that nobody in Valemont had yet thought it worth destroying. He thought about what would happen if a Radiant knight, with sunfire in his hands, walked up the processional stair one night when the Warden was away and turned out to have been sent. He thought about the murderer's grudges, and who would pay them. He thought about Baron Quell's agents, coming out of the east, not asking.

He thought about the name *Quell* most of all. About how much he wanted the clerk's satchel. About what it would cost to show his hand three years too soon.

*Not yet,* said the cold voice in him, quite reasonably. *Not now. Not for three strangers. You are building something. You must protect it.*

He stood up.

"No," he said. "None of them."

---

The noise that went round the court was not a protest. That was what he remembered afterwards. It was relief. He felt it go through the crowd like a wind going through barley: shoulders loosening, breath going out. The smuggler on the council nodded, slowly. Mother Bettan closed her eyes.

"Jac will take you back to Corrow on the morning tide," said Faro to the three of them. "I'll pay your passage, and a month's bread. Beyond that, I can't help you." He made himself look at each of them as he said it. "I'm sorry. This place is too young. It can't carry you yet."

Ansel Stroud only nodded, as if he had expected nothing else, and picked up his bag. Merrit Fell made a small sound, like a dog that has been kicked, and clutched his satchel tighter, and did not argue. He did not seem to have any arguments left.

Corin Ashe looked at him for a long time.

"Too young," he said. "I see." He put his hand on his son's shoulder. "I was told, all along the coast, that there was a man in the west who took in everyone the King had forgotten. That he didn't ask what you'd been. That he'd made a city for people who had nowhere else." His voice was very even. "I suppose I should have asked what he'd do with the ones the King hadn't forgotten."

"You'll be safe in Corrow," said Faro. "For a while. The Order doesn't go there."

"For a while," said Corin Ashe. And he turned, and took his wife's arm, and walked out of the Court of Tides with his family close round him, and his small daughter, at the arch, looked back over her shoulder at the man on the step of the fountain, as if she were trying to remember his face.

---

He went down to the quay once, that night, after the lamps were out. He told himself it was to see that they had bread.

They were sleeping in the empty warehouse at the end of the quay, the murderer and the clerk and the Radiant's family, on sacking, by the light of a single lamp. Stroud was sitting up against the wall with his eyes open, the way a man sleeps who has slept in dangerous places for twenty years. The Ashe children were curled together under their father's coat. And the clerk was awake, at the far end, hunched over his satchel by the lamp, and when he saw the Warden in the doorway, he scrambled to his feet.

"Sir," he said. "Sir. Please. A moment."

Faro went to him. The clerk was fumbling in the satchel with shaking fingers. He brought out a sheaf of papers, tied with a bit of string, and held it out.

"Take it," he whispered. "The copies. The house of Quell's terms, the debt-buying, all of it. Two years of it, in my own hand. I don't know what you are, sir, or why you looked like that when I said the name. But I saw your face." His spectacles were misting again. "They'll find me in Corrow. I know they will. And when they do, they'll take this, and burn it, and nobody will ever know what they did. Please. Let somebody know."

Faro looked at the papers for a long moment. He knew what they were. He knew what they were worth. The first thread into the house of the man who had written a letter, left-handed, half a lifetime ago, at a table in the Three Anchors.

He took them.

"Thank you," he said. And then, because he could not think of anything else, "I'm sorry."

Merrit Fell looked at the papers in the Warden's hand, and then up at the Warden's face, and something went out of his own, very quietly, like a lamp being turned down.

"Yes, sir," he said. "I expect you are."

They sailed on the morning tide. He did not go down to the quay to watch. He sat in the harbour tower with the shutters closed and a ledger open in front of him that he did not read.

Jac came in when she got back, three days later. She stood in the doorway for a while, saying nothing, with her hat in her hands.

"Stroud took a berth on a sealer going north," she said at last. "Didn't say where. The clerk's sleeping under the fish-market in Corrow. I gave him your month's bread. He won't last the winter, the state he's in. And Quell's men are still asking." She turned her hat round in her hands. "The Radiant's looking for passage to the Sardh. Says they'll go east, where the Order can't follow. Desert. With two children."

"I know."

"Do you?" said Jac Teague. She looked at him for a long moment. "I brought them in together, Faro. All three, on one morning. I told you why. I said, let's see what he does." She put her hat back on. "Well. Now I've seen."

She went out. The door closed.

The city was quieter, that month, than it had ever been. Nobody threw stones. Nobody painted anything on anybody's door. The council met under the tree and argued about drains and the price of fish. Four new Weir-folk families came from the east, having heard that Cristal was safe. The walls went up a little faster.

He walked the walls one evening at dusk, with the lamps being lit, and heard two of the watch talking at the corner tower, not knowing he was there.

"Did you hear about the Radiant?" said one. "The Warden sent him back."

"Good," said the other. "Can't trust them. Can't trust any of them."

"No," said the first. Then, after a while, more quietly: "Only it makes you think, doesn't it. If he'd send a man back for what he used to be." He was silent. "What I used to be's not so pretty either."

Faro walked on, along the wall, in the dark, before they turned and saw him.

### Later

In the nineteenth year, when the King's fleet came out of the east to burn Cristal, it did not have to feel its way in.

Every chart of the Reach in Valemont showed the island ringed with reefs, the harbour mouth a needle's eye between two towers, a channel no stranger could find. Faro had counted on that. He had counted on it for five years. It was the first wall of Cristal and the strongest, and he had built nothing to replace it.

The flagship came through the channel at the head of the fleet on the top of the tide, without a sounding, without a pause, as if her helmsman had been born there. And in her bows, beside the pilot's chair, in the white and gold of the Radiant Order, with a new captain's sash across his chest, stood a tall fair man with a pale band across his forehead where a helmet had kept the sun off, and his hand raised to point the way.

He had seen the channel once. Jac had brought him in through it, with his wife and his children, on a wet morning three years before, and taken him out again three days later. He had stood at the rail both times and watched, and remembered, because a man who has been turned away from the last door in the world remembers everything about it.

Afterwards, in the ruins of the first terrace, they found a letter, in a fair hand, addressed to the Warden. It said that the desert had taken Mareth in the first summer, and the boy in the second. It said that the Order had offered a pardon to any deserter who could bring them something worth having. It said that the girl was alive, and in Solace, and safe, and would be raised in the Order's house, and would never want for anything. It said: *You told me this place was too young to carry us. I hope it was old enough to carry this.*

It was not signed. It did not need to be.

He did not answer the council at once. He got up from the step of the fountain and walked across the paving, under the tree, in the lamplight, to where the three applicants stood. The court went quiet as he passed.

"I'm going to look at you," he said to them. "Each of you. It won't hurt. You won't feel it, or not much. But I'll see whether you've told the truth, and I'll see what you've left out. If you'd rather I didn't, you can go now, and Jac will take you back to Corrow, and I won't think less of you." He waited. "Well?"

The murderer looked at him with his pale eyes, and did not move. The clerk swallowed, and clutched his satchel, and did not move. Corin Ashe looked at his wife, and then at Faro, and something went across his face that might have been fear, and might have been recognition.

"I know what that is," he said quietly. "What you're going to do. The Order has a word for it."

"I'm sure it does," said Faro. "Will you let me?"

Corin Ashe looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded.

---

Still Water was the discipline he knew best, after Riptide. He had practised it every day for three years: the face that was not his face, the mask that let men who had known him for a year pass him in the street. But a mask was only the half of it. The other half was the looking.

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. He let his own face go still, as still as a pool on a windless day. And he looked at the murderer, and let himself see.

It was like looking down into water. Not the man's face: the man. The currents in him, the way Faro felt currents in the sea. He saw the years of killing, cold and grey and settled at the bottom of him like silt. He saw the morning two years ago when he had woken and could not do it any more, a single bright thread running through the grey. And he saw what was moving now, on the surface, in the lamplight, with the whole court watching: no lie. Only a great tiredness. And under it, very small, very far down, something the man had not said, because he had not known it was there. Hope. He had not hoped for anything in twenty years. He was hoping now, and it frightened him more than anything he had ever done.

Faro let go, and looked at the Radiant.

Corin Ashe's water was clear. Clearer than any man's he had looked into. It was full of light, the ghost of the sunfire, and it hurt to look at, like looking at a lake at noon. But there was no lie in it. A village burning, and an old woman in her doorway, and a lance that would not come down. A wife. Two children. A terror for them so deep and steady that it had become a kind of floor he stood on. And, at the very bottom, a thing Faro had not expected, and did not want to see: the man was afraid of him. Not of the Warden. Of what he was looking at, now, through the Warden's eyes. *I know what that is.*

He let go, and looked at the clerk.

And there it was.

The debt was real. The dead wife was real; he saw her, a small woman in a bed by a window, coughing. The neighbour in the debtors' prison was real, and the fear of the house of Quell was real, so real it shook him like a fever. But under the fear, under the truth, there was a thing the clerk had not said. A man in a good coat, in a back room in Saltmere, three months ago. A paper on a table. *The debt is forgiven, Master Fell, all of it, if you will go west and tell us where the cheap salt is coming from. Who runs the island. What he wants.* A purse. A promise. And the clerk, shaking, taking it, because the alternative was the prison where his neighbour had died.

He was not a spy. Not really. He was a frightened man who had been told to be one, and had come to the island meaning to confess it, and had stood on the paving in front of the Warden and found he did not have the courage.

Faro let go.

He was cold. Much colder than he had expected. Three looks, and the salt was in his mouth as if he had swallowed seawater, and the Tidemark was prickling up his throat under his collar, and for a moment, behind his eyes, he saw a white city by the sea, and a woman in a long robe standing on a balcony above a square full of people, looking down at them one by one, and every one of them going still as she looked.

He blinked it away.

---

"All three may stay," he said.

The court did not like it. He felt it not liking it. But it did not argue, not this time, because they had all seen what he had done, standing in the lamplight with his face gone still as glass, and none of them was quite sure what it was, and none of them wanted to ask.

"Ansel Stroud will fish with the Bettans," said Faro. "Corin Ashe will live on the fourth terrace, and keep his hands in his pockets. Merrit Fell will keep the island's accounts." He looked at the clerk. "And will come and see me in the harbour tower tonight, alone, and tell me about the man in the good coat in Saltmere."

Merrit Fell went the colour of paper. He opened his mouth, and shut it again, and nodded.

"And I'll be watching," said Faro, to all three of them, and to the court, and to the twelve on their benches. "All of you know what I mean by that now. If any of them lies to me, I'll know. If any of them means this island harm, I'll know it before they do." He let that settle. "That's the price. For them, and for everyone else who comes after them. Anyone may live in Cristal. But I'll look first."

There was no stone through anyone's window that week. Nobody painted anything on anybody's door. The Weir-folk boys did not go up the stair with lamp oil. Old Mother Bettan watched Corin Ashe's little daughter going down to the school every morning, holding her brother's hand, and said nothing at all. It was the quietest month the island had ever had.

He walked the walls one evening at dusk, and heard two of the watch talking at the corner tower.

"He looked at them," one said. "Right into them. Like a lamp. Bettan's boy says he can do it to anyone."

"Good," said the other. "Means nobody'll try anything."

"Mm." A pause. "Makes you think, though. Whether he's done it to you."

Faro walked on, along the wall, in the dark, and did not look at either of them. He did not need to. He could feel them both, the currents in them, without trying, the way he felt the sea. He had not been able to stop feeling them since the Court of Tides.

### Later

Merrit Fell wrote to the house of Quell every month, from that night until the spring the Count of Cristal sailed for Solace.

He wrote at the table in the harbour tower, with the Warden standing behind his chair, and every letter was a masterpiece: careful, frightened, full of detail, and false from beginning to end. The island was poor, he wrote. The Warden was a smuggler with a talent for weather, nothing more. The cheap salt came from a Sardhi syndicate in Mazrah. There was nothing in the west worth Baron Quell's attention. And the house of Quell, which trusted its ledgers above everything, read the letters and believed them, and did not look west again until it was much too late.

The cost came slowly, as the cost always did. The Tidemark climbed his face a year sooner than it would have. And Cristal learned to live with being looked at. It learned so well that by the fifth year, when a new family came off a ship onto the quay, the people of the terraces would stand back and wait for the Warden to come down and look at them, and would not speak to them until he had. Nobody thought this strange any more.

Hayda thought it strange. She said so, in her first month on the island, at dinner, in front of the council, in her clear cool carrying voice.

"In my father's court," she said, "there was a man who could tell if you were lying. A Sardhi truth-reader. My father kept him beside the throne." She looked at Faro across the table. "Everyone in Qasr loved my father. And nobody ever told him anything at all."
