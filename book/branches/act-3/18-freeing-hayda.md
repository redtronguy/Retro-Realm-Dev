## Buy her and keep her as a weapon, without telling her she is free

*Heart −− · Tide 0*

She stood on the deck of the *Slip* in the merchant's silks and jewels, very small and very straight, with her hands folded in front of her the way she had been taught, and her eyes on the planks a yard in front of his boots, and waited to be told what she was for.

He had the paper in the breast of his coat. He could feel it there, against the Tidemark: a single sheet of good Sardhi parchment, drawn up that morning by a notary in the old town of Mazrah while Jac paced the anteroom, sealed with the notary's seal and witnessed by two clerks, saying in High Sardhi and in the common tongue that the woman Hayda, daughter of Tariq, aged seventeen years, was from this day free, and no one's property, and had never lawfully been. There was a purse beside it, heavy with gold. He had meant to give her both. He had rehearsed what he would say, on the voyage east, standing at the rail at night: *You are free. You are no one's. You never were.*

He looked at her, and reached, as he reached for everyone now without trying.

He had expected grief. He found it, a long way down, under ice. He had expected fear; it was there, the steady low background fear of a slave who has been sold four times and does not know yet what the fifth master wants. But what he had not expected was the rest of it. Under the fear, and the grief, and the careful downcast stillness, there was something in her that pulled like a riptide: a single hard bright current of hate, eleven years deep, polished like a blade. He did not know yet whom it was for. He could guess.

And over all of it, like a skin of ice over a pool, the habit. Eleven years of doing what she was told, when she was told, because the ones who did not were beaten, or sold south, or worse. He felt it in her very clearly. It was the strongest thing about her, stronger even than the hate. It held everything else down.

*A free woman can choose,* said the cold, very calm, very reasonable, the voice he had come to think of as his own good sense. *And a woman who chooses may choose to go. To Port Jasmine, to the south, to some quiet life where no one knows her face. She would be right to. And then where is your witness? Where is the daughter of Tariq, standing up in the Chamber of Lords with her father's ring in her hand? You did not come all this way for a ward. You came for a weapon.*

*She has a right to choose.*

*She has had no rights for eleven years. Another year will not kill her. One year, and then the paper, and the purse, and anything she asks. It is not so much. It is a kindness, in the end. You will give her what she wants most in the world. You will give her Fenrick Morrow.*

He stood on the deck and looked at her, and the paper lay against his heart, and did not move.

"Look at me," he said in High Sardhi.

She looked up. He saw the language go into her: the shock of it, the court speech of Qasr, which she had not heard spoken properly since she was six. Her face did not change. But her eyes did. They were as dark and still as a desert well, and for a moment, very far down in them, something moved.

"You belong to the house of Cristal now," he said. "I have bought you, as others bought you. I won't pretend otherwise." He watched the words go in, and the ice close over them, smooth and familiar. "But I am not like the others. No one in my house will lay a hand on you. You will not be sold again, or shown, or made to sing for anyone. You will have rooms of your own, and books, and teachers, and anything you ask for that can be bought. I want one thing from you. One. And when it's done, you may ask me anything you like, and I will give it to you."

She was silent for a long moment.

"What thing?" said Hayda.

"To stand up in front of the man who sold you," said Faro, "and tell the world what he did."

He watched it go into her. He watched her face, so still, so proud, so careful, crack, just for a moment, like ice on a pond when a stone hits it. And through the crack, he saw the riptide.

"Morrow," said Hayda. "Fenrick Morrow. The pale captain." Her voice went lower, and colder, and harder, until it was like a blade being drawn. "The one who opened the gates. The one who killed my father in his bed, and sold my mother and me in the market at Qadir. I was there. I saw him take the money. I was six." She looked at him with her desert-well eyes. "You know him?"

"I know him."

"Where is he?"

"In Solace. He's the Lord Marshal of Valemont now. And in the spring of next year, I'm going to Solace, to see him."

Hayda looked at him for a long time. Then she lowered her eyes again to the planks a yard in front of his boots, and folded her hands, and bowed her head: the deep, practised, graceful bow of a well-trained slave to a new master.

"Then I am yours," she said, "until the spring."

---

Jac said nothing until they were clear of the harbour mouth. Then she gave the wheel to one of the Sardhi brothers and came and stood beside him at the rail, with her red hair whipping and her face like thunder.

"You had that paper drawn up this morning," she said, very low. "I sat in the notary's front room for an hour while you did it. I watched you pay him."

"I know."

"So why's it still in your coat?"

He did not look at her. He looked at the coast of the Sardh going down into the dusk behind them, the white walls of Mazrah turning pink and then grey.

"Because if I give it to her now, she may go," he said. "And if she goes, I lose the one witness in the world who can stand up in the Chamber of Lords and break Fenrick Morrow. I'll give it to her when it's done. A year. Less. She'll have everything she wants, and then she'll have that too."

Jac Teague looked at him for a long time.

"That's what they all say," she said. "Every slaver I ever met in this trade. *I'll free them when the debt's worked off. When they've earned it. When it's safe.*" She spat over the rail into the dark water. "I took you off a reef with your wrists still in irons, Faro. I took them off with a hacksaw on my own deck. I didn't ask you to work off the hacksaw first."

She went back to the wheel. She did not speak to him again until Cristal.

---

He kept his word, in everything but the one thing.

No one in Cristal laid a hand on her. She had two rooms in the citadel, with a window over the harbour, and a Weir-folk woman to see to her, and new clothes, plain ones, which she chose herself and wore without comment. She had the run of the library. She read everything in it in a month, and started again. She learned the Weir-tongue from the Bettan grandmothers and the old tongue from the books, and sailing from Jac, who was kind to her, gruffly, and never once mentioned the paper; and the knife from Bastien, who taught it to her the way his family had taught it for three hundred years, and said she was the best pupil he had ever had, and did not say it with any pleasure.

She learned, he thought, the way a blade is sharpened. Quickly, and completely, and without joy.

She did not argue with him at dinner. She did not ask questions in front of the council. When he spoke to her she answered, courteously, in a low clear voice, and looked at the table a yard in front of his plate. When he came into a room she stood up. When he left it, she bowed. He told her not to, more than once. She stopped standing. She never stopped bowing. It was such a small bow, by the end, that no one but he could see it.

And one night, at the end of the first winter, when he came up from the shore after a fight with a blighted thing, a big one, a shark that had come into the shallows and taken two of the watch before he could reach it, and he was so cold his hands would not stop shaking and the Tidemark was blazing on his throat above the collar of his coat, she was waiting for him in the library, by the fire.

She poured him a cup of the Sardhi coffee, thick and black and scalding, and put it into his shaking hands, as a servant puts a cup into her master's hands. And stood beside his chair, with her own hands folded, and her eyes on the floor.

"Is there anything else my lord requires?" she said.

He looked up at her. He thought, for a moment, that there was a question in her: something unasked, sitting behind the still face like a stone at the bottom of a well. He reached for it, and felt it there, and could not tell what it was.

"No," he said. "Thank you. Go to bed, Hayda."

She bowed, very slightly, and went.

He sat by the fire with the coffee going cold in his hands. And later, very late, lying awake in his own room down the passage, he heard her singing. Very low, through the wall. A song in High Sardhi, a pretty, light, intricate thing, the kind of song a spice merchant in Mazrah has a pretty girl taught so that she can sing it for buyers. She sang it all the way through, perfectly, and then she sang it again.

He lay in the dark and listened, and did not get up, and did not go to her door. The paper was in the locked drawer of the desk in the library, under the treatise on the Sardh. He had put it there himself, on the first night home.

He did not laugh that winter. Nobody in Cristal took him out in the small boat past the harbour mouth for no reason at all. When the Count of Cristal sailed for Solace in the spring, Jac said to Big Olaf, on the quay, watching him go up the gangway with the slight straight figure in grey a pace behind him, that she had seen a great many men go cold in her time, but she had never before watched one do it on purpose.

### Later

She testified. Of course she did.

She stood up in the second gallery of the Chamber of Lords, veiled, in grey, and came down into the well of the hall, and put back her veil, and laid her father's ring and her father's letter and the bill of sale on the clerk's table one by one, and pointed at Fenrick Morrow, and her hand did not waver. She was magnificent. He watched from the gallery and felt the Lord Marshal of Valemont break, and walk the length of the marble floor, and go out through the bronze doors for the last time.

In the carriage afterwards, with the curtains drawn, she sat very straight with the cedar box in her lap, and did not weep. She did not touch him. She looked at the curtain a yard in front of her face the whole way home.

That night, at the window of the study in the house with the blue door, when Bastien had brought the news of the sword and the study floor and gone out again, she came and stood beside him in the dark. Not close.

"It's done," she said.

"Yes."

"Then I'll ask now. You said I might ask anything." She held out her hand, palm up. "The paper, my lord."

He turned and looked at her.

"I found it in the first winter," said Hayda. "In the locked drawer of the desk in the library. Under the treatise on the Sardh. You know I read everything in that room. I read that too." Her voice did not rise at all. "Dated at Mazrah, the day you bought me. Sealed, witnessed. I was free before I set foot on your ship. You had it done, and put it in your coat, and looked at me, and decided I was more use to you as I was." She was quiet a moment. "Do you know who else did that? Who kept me, and fed me, and gave me fine rooms, and never once laid a hand on me, because I was worth more to him whole? The spice merchant. And the man before him. I know the kind of master who's kind because it pays. I've had four." She did not lower her hand. "You were the fifth. The paper, please."

He went to the desk, and unlocked the drawer where he had moved it, and gave it to her. She read it through once, by the window. Then she folded it, and put it in her sleeve, and bowed, very slightly, so slightly that only he could have seen it.

She was gone in the morning. Her rooms were empty. She had taken nothing he had bought her except the knife.

He did not see her again until the siege.

The white fleet came through the Reach in ten days, on soundings so exact that not one of the twelve ships touched a reef, by channels no King's ship had sailed in three hundred years. Afterwards, among the papers on a Radiant galley they took on the second night, Jac found the chart. It was drawn in a fine, small, beautiful hand, in a script he had seen every day for a year, in the margins of the books in the library at Cristal. And on the quarterdeck of the flagship, in the gold glare of the mirrors at noon, beside the High Radiant in her white and gold, there stood a slight, straight figure in grey, very still, with her chin up, watching the harbour tower run like wax into the sea.
