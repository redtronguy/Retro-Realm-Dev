# Event 08: Jac's Cargo

*Branches from Chapter 14, "Salt and Smugglers": in the month after the squall, when "he did not use the sea again for a month", the* Slip *takes on a cargo that is not brandy.*

## The cargo

They took the passengers aboard at night, in a cove on the far southern edge of the coast, where the last of Valemont's fishing villages gave way to salt flats and nobody's land.

There were twenty-three of them. They came down the beach in a long, quiet line, carrying everything they owned in bundles on their backs: men and women and a great many children, and one old woman so bent that two of the men took turns carrying her. Sardhi, all of them, from the wars in the east. They had walked, Jac said, for months, across the desert and then along the coast, sleeping in ditches and working for bread, and every village they passed through had moved them on. They were going west now, to Mazrah, on the Sardh coast, where there was work in the date groves and the salt pans, and a factor who had paid their passage.

The factor came with them. He was a soft-spoken, round-faced man in a good grey coat, with clean hands and a little ledger he kept in an inside pocket, and his name was Seref Bal. He shook Jac's hand on the beach, and counted out a hundred and fifty gold marks into her palm by lantern light, and promised the other hundred and fifty on delivery. The *Slip* was to carry them three days west, to a cluster of islets called the Kettles on the eastern edge of the Reach, and hand them over there to a brig out of Mazrah called the *Bountiful*. A clean job. No customs men out there. No questions.

"People who don't want to be found," Jac said to Faro, as the passengers filed down into the hold. "It's half our trade. Don't look like that, Shipwreck. It's honest money. Honester than brandy."

He did not know, then, why he was looking like anything.

He found out on the second night.

He was off watch, lying in his corner of the hold, and he could not sleep, because the hold was full of breathing. Twenty-three people, packed in among the casks, and every one of them breathing the way people breathe in a strange place, shallow and careful. A baby cried and was hushed. Somewhere in the dark, a boy of about eight was reciting something under his breath, over and over, in Sardhi. Faro listened. It was a list of names. Villages. Rivers. A grandfather. A dog. *So I won't forget,* the boy said, when Faro asked him, in Sardhi, very quietly. *My mother says when we get to the new place we must forget everything and work. So I'm saying it now. While I can.*

The boy's mother had a paper. They all had papers. She showed him hers, by the light of the shuttered lantern, unfolding it with great care, as if it were the most precious thing she owned. It was her contract, she said. Her work contract, for the date groves. She could not read it. None of them could. It was in the trade script of the Mazrah counting-houses, not the Sardhi of the villages. But the factor had read it to them, and they had all made their marks.

Faro had learned the trade script in the Keep, from an old man who had made him copy it on the wet stone floor until his fingers bled. He read the paper twice, slowly, by lantern light. Then he folded it up and gave it back to her, and told her it was a fine contract, and went up on deck, and stood at the rail in the dark until his hands stopped shaking.

It was not a contract. It was a debt-bond. It said that the bearer owed the holder, for passage and food and lodging and the factor's fees, a sum that a date-picker could not have earned in forty years, and that until the debt was paid, the bearer and the bearer's children were the property of whoever held the bond. In Mazrah, Faro knew, debt-bonds were sold in the market, on the steps of the counting-house, every morning. Like slaves. Exactly like slaves. The only difference was the paper, and the paper had their marks on it.

He went to Jac's cabin.

She was at the table with the chart and a cup of coffee, and she listened while he told her, and her face did not change at all. When he had finished, she picked up the coffee and drank it.

"I know," she said.

"You *know*?"

"I didn't read the paper. I don't read Mazrah script." She put the cup down. "But I've been in this trade eleven years, Faro, and I know what a factor with clean hands looks like, and I know what Mazrah is, and I'm not a fool." She met his eyes. "I said no questions. I meant it. It's how I've stayed alive. It's how the *Slip*'s stayed afloat, and the crew's stayed fed, and nobody's ever been able to say Jac Teague broke her word. A hundred and fifty marks is in my strongbox, and a hundred and fifty more is waiting at the Kettles, and the *Bountiful*'s master is a man called Hesk who works for the slave-houses in Mazrah, and they own half the warehouses in Corrow, and if I don't deliver, every one of those doors is shut to me for the rest of my life. If I'm lucky." She leaned back. "So. What do you want me to do?"

Below them, through the planks, he could hear the boy, still saying his list of names.

## Free them

"Don't deliver them," he said.

Jac laughed. It was not a pleasant laugh. "Just like that."

"Just like that."

"And the hundred and fifty in the strongbox? And the hundred and fifty at the Kettles? And Hesk, and the slave-houses, and Corrow? And the crew, who've each got a share coming?" She got up, and the cabin was too small for her, and she paced it anyway, two steps each way, like a cat in a box. "You've been aboard two months, Shipwreck. Two months. You've got no name and no money and no past, and a trick with the sea that frightens my crew half to death, and you want me to throw away eleven years for twenty-three strangers I'll never see again?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

He thought about how to answer. He thought about the Keep. About thirteen years in the dark, because a man with clean hands had written something on a piece of paper, and another man had read it, and decided. About his father, starving by a window. About how easy it was, how terribly easy, to do a thing like that to a person if you never had to look them in the face.

"Because I've been cargo," he said. "Someone signed a paper, once, that said I belonged to them. And nobody came." He held her eyes. "I can't make you, Jac. It's your ship. But I'm asking."

She stopped pacing. She stood with her back to him, looking out of the stern window at the black water and the white wake, for a long time.

"I hate you," she said at last, quite conversationally. "I want you to know that. I've never hated anyone so much in my life." She turned round. "Get Pike. And Olaf. And bring me the factor."

---

Seref Bal did not understand, at first. Then he understood, and his round face went quite white, and then quite red, and he began to talk. He talked about contracts, and about the law in Mazrah, and about Hesk, and about the slave-houses, and what they did to people who cheated them. He talked about the *Slip*, and how far her name would travel, and how fast. He talked about Jac's father. He did not shout. He was not that kind of man. He simply talked, softly and reasonably, with his clean hands folded on his little ledger, and every word was a knife.

Jac let him finish. Then she went to her strongbox, and unlocked it, and took out a leather purse, and put it in his hands.

"A hundred and fifty marks," she said. "Count it. I don't keep money for a job I didn't do."

"You'll regret this, Captain."

"I already do." She nodded to Big Olaf. "Put him in the cable tier. We'll set him ashore at Corrow, and he can tell Hesk anything he likes."

The crew took it badly, at first. Pike swore, long and inventively. The younger of the Sardhi brothers went white and said nothing. Tam asked what a debt-bond was, and when Faro told him, he went down into the hold and did not come up for an hour. But when Faro went down himself, later, to tell the passengers in their own tongue that they would not be going to Mazrah, he found the boy sitting among them, playing knucklebones with the child who said his list of names, and teaching him the words for the parts of a ship.

The passengers did not believe him. He had not expected them to. They had been lied to all the way across the desert. The boy's mother sat with her paper on her knees and stared at him, and the old woman in the corner, the one who had been carried down the beach, looked at him with eyes as black and hard as olives and said nothing at all.

So he took the paper, very gently, out of the young mother's hands, and read it aloud to them, in Sardhi, every word. And then he tore it in half, and gave her the halves.

There was a long silence in the hold.

Then the old woman spoke. She had a voice like a dry reed, and the accent of the old Sardhi courts, not the villages.

"Where will we go?"

"I don't know yet," said Faro. "But not there."

---

They put into Corrow four days later, with no fee and no cargo worth the name and twenty-three hungry passengers, and Seref Bal went ashore without looking back, and by evening every tavern on the quay knew that Jac Teague had broken a contract with the Mazrah houses. Two of the chandlers who sold her water would not sell to her. A man she had known for ten years crossed the street to avoid her. Somebody painted a word on the *Slip*'s hull in the night, in tar, and Olaf spent the next morning scraping it off and singing very loudly.

Jac said nothing about any of it. She found the passengers a place to sleep, in a half-empty warehouse she rented at the back of the harbour, and paid for their bread out of her own purse, and when Faro tried to thank her, she told him what he could do with his thanks, in some detail.

But that night, late, she came and found him on deck, and stood beside him at the rail with a bottle, and passed it to him without a word.

"Hesk will come after me," she said at last.

"I know."

"Then you'll have to stand next to me when he does." She took the bottle back. "That's the deal, Shipwreck. You cost me eleven years. You can spend the next eleven paying it off." She drank. Then, after a while, in a different voice: "That boy. The one with the names. He came up to me on the quay this afternoon, and kissed my hand. Like I was a queen." She looked at the bottle. "Nobody's ever done that."

He did not say anything. She did not seem to want him to.

"I'll tell you something," said Jac Teague. "I've never once in my life done a thing I couldn't afford. Not once. My father taught me that." She was quiet a long time. "I think I like it."

### Later

In the third year of Cristal, when there was a hospital and a school and a market in the first square, and the Sardhi families from the warehouse in Corrow had houses of their own on the second terrace with jasmine climbing the walls, the old woman with the voice like a dry reed sent for the Warden.

Her name was Nadira. She had lived in the second terrace for two years, the first settlers of Cristal, the first after the smugglers, and in all that time she had barely spoken to him. She sat in the doorway of her daughter-in-law's house every evening and watched him pass with her olive-black eyes, and nodded, and said nothing. Now she was dying, quite peacefully, of being very old, and she wanted him.

"I was a nurse," she said, when he sat down by her bed. "Before. In a great house. In Qasr." Her thin fingers moved on the blanket. "I nursed the Emir's daughter. A little girl with a long neck and a temper like a hawk. When the city fell, I was in the kitchen. I hid in the oven. I heard them take her. I heard the pale captain laughing in the courtyard." She turned her head on the pillow and looked at him. "You have been asking questions about Qasr. Your ships ask in every port. My grandsons hear things. I did not tell you before, because I did not know what you were. A man who tears up papers. A man the sea listens to. I did not know if you were better than the others, or only stronger."

"And now?"

"Now I am dying, and I have watched you for two years." She closed her eyes. "Her name is Hayda. She will be fifteen. She has a scar on her left hand, here, where she caught it on a bridle when she was four and would not cry. She sings in the old mode, the court mode, because I taught her. If she is alive, someone is making her sing."

Two years later, in a spice merchant's house in Mazrah, a girl with a long neck and a scar on her left hand sang for a foreign lord in the old court mode, and Faro stood at the back of the room and knew her at once.

## Let the deal go through

He did not say anything for a long time.

He thought about it. He made himself think about it, coldly, the way Faro had taught him to think about a problem: turn it over, look at every side. On one side, twenty-three people in the hold, and a paper with their marks on it. On the other, everything else.

He had no money. He had no name. He had a treasure on an island at the edge of the world, and he could not reach it without a ship, and the only ship he had was this one, and this woman's goodwill was the only thing between him and a rock in the Reach with seaweed to eat. If he asked her to break her word, she might do it. She might also put him ashore at Corrow and never speak to him again. And then there would be no Cristal. No harbour for the people nobody wanted. No city where the sea was listened to. No road to Solace. And the men who had done this to him, who had done this to Faro and to his father and to the sea itself, would go on living, and eating, and sleeping in their beds.

*You cannot save everyone,* said a voice in his head. It sounded like his own. It sounded very reasonable. *Not yet. Not with nothing. When you have the island, when you have the gold, you will save thousands. Tens of thousands. You will build a place where no one is ever sold again. But not if you throw it away now, for twenty-three.*

"Nothing," he said. "I don't want you to do anything. I only thought you should know."

Jac looked at him for a long moment. Something moved in her face that might have been relief, and might have been something else.

"Right," she said. "Well. Now I know." She picked up the coffee again. "Go and get some sleep, Faro. It's your watch at four."

---

He did not sleep. He lay in his corner of the hold and listened to the boy saying his list of names, until the boy fell asleep in the middle of a village, and then he listened to the breathing.

The next day, he stayed on deck. He did not go down into the hold. He did not speak Sardhi to anyone. When the passengers came up in twos and threes for air, at Jac's order, he kept to the wheel and looked at the sea, and when the boy came and stood near him, watching him steer with the frank curiosity of a child, he did not look down.

There was a girl among them, a little older than the boy, twelve perhaps, with a thin, sharp face and a way of standing very straight. She did not come near him. But she watched him. Every time he looked up from the wheel, she was watching him, from the forward hatch, with dark, steady, unblinking eyes, as if she were memorising his face.

On the third evening, in a flat calm under a copper sky, the *Bountiful* came up out of the west and hove to off the Kettles.

She was a broad, ugly, deep-hulled brig with a fresh coat of white paint and her name in gold on her stern, and she smelled, even from a cable's length, even through the clean salt air. Faro had been in the Keep for thirteen years. He knew what that smell was. Jac knew it too; he saw her nostrils flare. But she said nothing, and brought the *Slip* alongside with her usual neatness, and the brig's crew threw down a boarding ladder, and a lean man with a shaven head and a long Sardhi knife leaned over the rail and called down a greeting to Seref Bal in the trade tongue.

They went up the ladder one by one.

He made himself watch. He did not know why. It seemed to him that if he was going to let it happen, he owed them at least that. The old woman, carried. The young mothers with their babies. The boy, still clutching his knucklebones, looking back over his shoulder at the *Slip* as he climbed, at Tam, who had taught him the words for the parts of a ship and who was standing by the mainmast now with his fists clenched and his face like chalk.

The girl with the sharp face went last. At the top of the ladder she stopped, and turned, and looked down at the *Slip*'s deck. Not at Jac. Not at the factor. At Faro, at the wheel. She looked at him for a long moment, quite calmly, and then she said, in Sardhi, in a clear, carrying voice that reached every corner of the deck:

"I heard you. In the night. You read my aunt's paper. You know what it says."

Nobody on the *Slip* except the Sardhi brothers understood her. The elder brother looked at Faro, and then looked away, and made a small sign with his fingers that Faro had not seen before.

Then a hand came down and took the girl by the collar, and she was gone over the rail.

---

Jac paid him that night. She came to him at the stern, where he was standing alone, and put a little leather purse in his hand.

"Fifty marks," she said. "Your share. Everyone gets a share."

He looked at it. It was the first money he had held in fourteen years. It was heavier than he had expected.

"I'd throw it over the side," said Jac Teague, "if I were you. But you won't. And neither will I." She stood beside him at the rail for a while, not drinking, not talking. Then she said, without looking at him: "You could've asked me not to. Last night. I've been thinking about it. I'm not sure what I'd have said."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked at him then, sideways, in the dark. "I thought you were a man who'd have asked." She pushed herself off the rail. "Shows what I know."

He stood at the stern until the *Bountiful*'s lights had dropped below the horizon behind them. Then he went below, and lay down in his corner, in the empty hold, among the casks, and it was very quiet, and he could not sleep at all.

He spent the fifty marks in Cristal, the next summer, on chisels, for the master mason. They were good chisels. They cut the first stone of the new quay.

### Later

The spice merchant's house in Mazrah had a courtyard with a fountain, and the merchant had dressed his treasure in silk and jewels and stood her by it, in the evening light, for the foreign lord to see.

Faro had bought her before he saw her. He had brought the gold in a chest. He had his speech ready, in High Sardhi, the speech about freedom and choice, and he said it, on the deck of the *Slip*, with the purse and the paper in his hands, exactly as he had planned.

Hayda listened to all of it without moving. Then she said, very quietly, "I know you."

"You can't."

"There was a girl in this house," said Hayda. "For three years. She scrubbed the floors and carried the water and she died of a fever last winter, in the room under the stairs, with her hand in mine. She came from a village in the east. She came on a brig called the *Bountiful*, by way of a black cutter with no name, and a man at the wheel of the cutter who spoke Sardhi like a scholar and read her aunt's bond in the night, and knew what it was, and let them climb the ladder." Her eyes went over his face, feature by feature, the long jaw, the straight mouth, the grey eyes. "She told me about him every night. So that someone would remember. She said he had eyes like rain."

He stood on the deck with the purse in his hand and could not say anything at all.

"I will stay," said Hayda, "because you know where Morrow is. That is the only reason." She took the paper out of his hand, and not the purse. "Do not ever call me your ward."

She did stay. She learned the old tongue and the knife and the sea, and she sat at his table, and in all the time she was at Cristal, she never once asked him who he was when he wasn't angry. She did not seem to want to know.

## Sink the slaver's ship

"Deliver them," he said.

Jac's eyebrows went up.

"Take them to the Kettles," said Faro. "Let Hesk bring his brig. Let him come alongside." He was looking past her, out of the stern window, at the black water. "And then leave the rest to me."

She looked at him for a long moment. He saw her understand. He saw her remember the night of the squall, and the lines on his chest blazing in the lantern light, and the *Slip* rising off her beam ends on a hand of water that had come up out of nowhere.

"You can't," she said. "You said. Only when you have to."

"I have to."

"Faro, there are thirty men on that brig."

"Thirty men," said Faro, "who carry people in chains for money, and have done for years, and will do for years more, until somebody stops them." His voice was very quiet. "Nobody has ever stopped them, Jac. Nobody's going to. Except me."

---

The *Bountiful* came up out of the west on the third evening, in a flat calm under a copper sky, and hove to off the Kettles.

She was a broad, ugly, deep-hulled brig with a fresh coat of white paint and her name in gold on her stern, and the smell came across the water ahead of her, through the clean salt air. Faro stood at the *Slip*'s rail and smelled it, and knew it. Thirteen years in the Keep. The smell of people kept below, in the dark, for too long.

Jac brought them alongside. A boarding ladder came down. A lean man with a shaven head and a long Sardhi knife leaned over the brig's rail and called down to Seref Bal in the trade tongue, and laughed at something the factor answered.

"Get them back," Faro said to Jac, low. "All of them. Get them below. Now."

The first of the passengers already had a foot on the ladder. Jac stared at him, and then at the brig, and then she was moving, bellowing at the crew, and Olaf and Pike were catching the passengers by their arms and hustling them back across the deck to the hatch, and Seref Bal was crying out in protest, and up on the brig the lean man had stopped laughing.

Hesk understood faster than his factor. He was a slaver; he had been cheated before. He shouted, and men appeared at the brig's rail with crossbows, and somebody tore the cover off a swivel gun on her quarterdeck and swung its muzzle down until it was looking straight at the *Slip*'s open hatch, where the last of the children were scrambling below.

Faro reached.

It was not like the night of the squall. Then, he had reached the way a man throws out a hand to catch a falling child, without thinking. Now he thought. He knew exactly what he was doing. He reached down through the planks of the *Slip*, through her keel, into the flat calm water of the Kettles, and found it waiting for him, the way it was always waiting, cold and eager, and he let it come up into him. It came in through his feet and his hands and over his heart, and the cold went through him like a blade, and he did not flinch from it. He took it. He took more than he had ever taken.

And he pulled.

The sea under the *Bountiful* went away.

There was no other way to describe it. For a moment, the water simply dropped out from under her, a long smooth hollow opening in the flat calm as if a giant had scooped it out with his hand, and the brig fell into it. She fell a full fathom, all at once, with a crack that went through every timber in her. The swivel gun fired, wild, into the sky. And then the water came back.

It came back from both sides at once. It came over her rails like a pair of hands closing. He felt it go into her. He felt her seams open under it, one after another, like the buttons of a coat. He felt the water rushing into her hold, into the dark below decks where the smell came from, and filling it, and he thought, with a strange, distant satisfaction, *there, now it's clean.*

She went down in less time than it takes to say a prayer.

---

Afterwards, there were men in the water.

He could feel them. Thirty of them, perhaps less, thrashing in the dark among the wreckage, crying out. The *Slip*'s crew were at the rail with lines, throwing them, because sailors throw lines to men in the water, whoever they are; it is the oldest law of the sea. Tam was leaning out over the side with a boathook, shouting.

The lines fell short. All of them. Every one.

Faro was not doing it. He would tell himself that afterwards, for years. He was not doing it. He had let go. He was standing at the rail with his hands at his sides, and the cold in him was enormous, and the salt was so strong in his mouth that he could not swallow, and the sea around the wreck was still heaving, still turning in a slow dark circle over the place where the brig had gone down, and he was not doing it.

But he was not stopping it, either.

He could have. He knew it. He could feel the water, every swirl of it, and he could have reached out and laid it flat as a pond, and let them swim, and let Olaf and Tam haul them in. It would have been easy. It would have been the easiest thing in the world.

And something in him, something underneath the cold, said: *Why?*

It was not the Deep's voice. Or it was, and it was also his own. It sounded very calm. *They chained people in the dark,* it said. *As you were chained. As I was chained. Let them drink, little one. Let them learn what it tastes like.*

He saw, behind his eyes, as clear as day, a harbour full of white ships with gold suns on their sails, and fire falling on people standing in the sea up to their waists. He felt the sea's grief for them. And then he felt something else, something it had felt for three hundred years and never been able to do anything with, and which it was doing now, through him, for the first time, at last. It was very like joy.

The last voice in the water stopped.

The sea lay down.

---

Nobody on the *Slip* spoke to him that night. Seref Bal was gone; somewhere in the confusion he had gone over the side, or been pushed, and nobody had seen which, and nobody asked. The passengers sat in the hold in the dark, very quiet, and listened to the crew moving about overhead, and did not know what had happened, except that the ship that had come to take them was gone and the sea had taken it.

In the morning the old woman who had been carried down the beach came up on deck, alone, on her own feet, slowly. She walked to where Faro stood at the stern, and looked up at him, for a long time, with her olive-black eyes. Then she bent, with enormous difficulty, and kissed his hand.

He let her. He did not know what else to do.

Jac did not come near him for three days. When she did, it was on the night watch, at the wheel, and she stood a little way off, the way the crew had begun to stand, and did not look at him.

"Thirty men," she said.

"Yes."

"I threw them a line. Every one of them. It was like throwing it into a wall." She was silent for a long time. "I'm not sorry for them. I want you to know that. I've seen what Hesk's lot do. I'm not sorry." Her hands were in her pockets. "But I saw your face, Faro. In the lantern light, when they were going under. And I'll tell you what I've never told anyone. I was more frightened of that than I was of the squall."

He did not answer. He was looking at the sea.

It was very calm, and very beautiful, under the stars, and when he let his mind go down into it, it was glad.

### Later

In the Reach, they called it the Kettle after that: the place where the sea had boiled up and swallowed a slaver whole. By the second year of Cristal, every smuggler who put into the new harbour had heard the story, and every one of them told it differently, and in every version the man who ran the island was the one who had done it. Slavers stopped coming within a hundred miles of Cristal. No law was ever needed. The Sardhi families from the hold built their houses on the second terrace and hung a little blue charm over every door, the old sign for the sea's blessing, and their children grew up believing that the Warden could see them wherever they went, and they were good.

In the fifth year, in Mazrah, when Faro went to buy the Emir's daughter, the spice merchant who owned her heard the name of the island he came from and went the colour of whey. He did not haggle. He would not even take the full price. He stood her by the fountain in her silks and jewels and could not get her out of his house fast enough, and as Faro led her down to the harbour, Hayda looked back at the merchant cowering in his doorway, and then at Faro, for a long time.

"They say in the market," she said, in High Sardhi, on the deck of the *Slip*, "that you drowned the *Bountiful*. With all her crew. With your hands."

"It's true."

She considered that. Then she lifted her chin.

"Good," said Hayda.

He had expected many things from her. Fear. Disgust. Gratitude. He had not expected that. He stood on the deck with the purse and the paper in his hands and looked at her, at her clear proud face and her dark still eyes, and felt the cold move in him, glad, and understood that she was the first person who had ever looked at what he had done in the Kettles and approved of it, and that of all the ways she could have looked at him, it was the one that frightened him most.
