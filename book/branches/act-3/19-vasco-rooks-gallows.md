# Event 19: Vasco Rook's Gallows

*Branches from the years between Chapters 18 and 19: the hanging that the Count describes to Aren in Chapter 19, "The Road to Solace," when he says Rook "was going to be hanged, in a town in the south, and I happened to be passing, and I thought it would be a waste."*

*The canon path, saving him, is never shown on the page; the Count recalls it in [Chapter 19](../../19-the-road-to-solace.md).*

---

It was market day in Fendle, in the spring of the third year, and they were hanging a man in the square at noon.

Faro had not meant to stop. He was riding north on the marsh road as Master Wilde, the foreign banker, in the green silk coat, with two of Jac's men behind him, on his way to look at the Greyhills road he would one day take to Solace; he had stopped at the Crossed Keys only to water the horses. But the square was full, and the inn yard was full, and the landlady, when he asked, said it was Vasco Rook they were hanging. *The* Vasco Rook. The outlaw king of the Greyhills, that the Lord Marshal himself had been trying to catch for four years. And he had been caught here, in Fendle, of all places, by the town watch, like a common sheep-thief. He had come down out of the hills in disguise to bury his mother.

"She was a Fendle woman," said the landlady. "Kept the bakehouse by the church, forty years. He'd send her money, all those years, and she'd never touch it, gave it to the poor box. Said it was stolen." She sniffed. "Somebody knew him at the graveside. Sold him to the watch for twenty marks. The magistrate had him tried the same afternoon, and the Marshal's men are riding down from Solace to fetch him, but the magistrate won't wait for them. Wants the credit. So they're hanging him today."

Faro went out into the square.

The gallows stood in the middle of it, by the market cross: new timber, raw and yellow, knocked up that morning. A beam, a rope, a cart beneath. The crowd was packed in round it, market-folk and carters and farm-wives and children on their fathers' shoulders, and the noise was not quite what he had expected at a hanging. It was not a jeering crowd. It was quiet, mostly, and sullen, and here and there he heard a woman weeping.

They brought Rook out at a quarter to noon, in the back of the cart, with his hands tied behind him and a priest of the Noon beside him reading from a book. He was a big man, black-bearded, with a single gold earring and a shirt that had been white that morning, and a bruise over one eye, and he stood in the cart with his feet planted wide as if it were the deck of a ship, and looked out at the crowd, and grinned.

"Fendle!" he said. His voice was enormous; it filled the square. "I'd have come home more often if I'd known you'd all turn out!"

Somebody laughed. Somebody else shushed them. A girl near the front, perhaps twelve, thin as a rake, with a younger boy by the hand, began to cry loudly, and Rook looked down at her, and his grin went soft.

"Now then, Bet," he said, more quietly. "None of that. Your da'll have that roof fixed by harvest. I left the money with Father Amos." He looked up at the beam, and the rope, and back at the crowd. "Somebody take her home, eh? This isn't for children."

Faro stood at the back of the square in his green coat, and watched, and reached.

He found what he had expected: a thief, a brawler, a man who had robbed coaches on the king's road for fifteen years and enjoyed it. Vanity, a great deal of it. Greed. And under it, to his surprise, not much else that was ugly. No cruelty. No cold. A big warm untidy current of a man, full of appetite and noise. He had robbed a great many people, Faro thought. He had probably never killed anyone he did not have to. And there were people in this square, he could feel them, a great many of them, who had eaten through bad winters because Vasco Rook had robbed a coach.

The magistrate came out onto the steps of the town hall in his chain, a small fat pompous man, and read the sentence. The hangman climbed into the cart, a stooped grey man with a hood rolled up on his head, and put the noose over Rook's neck, and drew it tight.

The priest closed his book.

## Let him hang

*Heart − · Tide 0*

He could have stopped it.

He knew exactly how. He could feel the rope from where he stood, forty yards away: old hemp, damp from the morning, with the water in its fibres. A thread of current, a blade of water no thicker than a hair drawn out of the rope itself and the spring air around it, the thing he had done with the rope under the Teeth, and it would part at the beam the moment the cart moved. And there was an old custom in the south, old as the Sun's temples, that a man the rope refused had been refused by the Sun, and went free. The magistrate would not like it. The crowd would roar. Rook would walk out of Fendle with the whole town at his back, and the magistrate would not dare to stop him.

It would be nothing. A breath. A little cold.

*And for what?* said the cold, quite reasonably. *For a highwayman. A robber of coaches. A man who has made his living for fifteen years with a crossbow and a knife on the king's road, taking from travellers who never did him any harm. The law has caught him, by its own lights, fairly. Is it your law? No. But you did not come out of the sea to save every thief in Valemont from the rope. You came out of the sea for three men.*

He thought about Master Wilde, the foreign banker, standing in Fendle square on the day the rope refused Vasco Rook, and the Marshal's men riding in from Solace that evening and asking questions. He thought about how few people in Valemont knew yet that the Warden of Cristal existed, and how much he needed it to stay that way until the spring after next.

And under all of it, quieter, a thing he did not want to look at. A boy in a magistrate's study, with his wrists bound, and men in the doorway, and nobody, nobody at all, stepping forward.

*Nobody came for me.*

He stood at the back of the square with his hands clasped behind him, and did not reach.

The priest stepped down from the cart. Rook looked out over the square, once more, slowly, as if he were trying to fix it in his mind: the market cross, the church, the bakehouse beside it with its shutters closed. His eyes moved across the crowd and passed over Faro, a tall stranger in a green coat at the back, and did not stop.

"Well," he said, to nobody in particular. "Tell the Marshal he was too slow."

The hangman slapped the horse.

The cart went forward. The crowd made a sound, a long low sound like the sea pulling back over shingle. Faro did not look away. He made himself watch. He had watched men die in the Keep; he could watch this. It was not quick. Hangings in small towns seldom are; the hangman of Fendle was old, and the drop was short. Rook was a big man, and he was strong, and it took a long time.

The girl called Bet was screaming. Somebody had picked her up and was carrying her out of the square, and she was screaming over their shoulder, the whole way, until the sound was lost in the streets.

When it was done, the crowd broke up quickly and quietly, as crowds do after a hanging, without looking at each other. The magistrate went back inside. The hangman sat down on the edge of the empty cart and took off his hood and wiped his face with it. And Vasco Rook turned slowly at the end of the rope in the spring sunshine over Fendle market square, with his gold earring catching the light.

Faro walked back to the Crossed Keys, and paid for the water, and mounted his horse.

"Didn't fancy staying, sir?" said one of Jac's men, a Sardhi lad called Rashid, who had watched it all from the inn yard with his face very blank.

"No," said Faro.

They rode north out of Fendle on the marsh road. A mile out, he found that his hands on the reins were very cold, colder than they had any reason to be in the spring sun, and that the taste of salt was in his mouth, though he had not touched the sea. He did not understand it. He had done nothing. That was the whole of it. He had done nothing at all.

It came to him, a mile further on, that he had done exactly what another man had done once, in a tavern called the Three Anchors, with his head on the table: watched, and said nothing, and gone home to bed. And that he had told himself, as that man must have told himself, that it was not his business.

Beside him, Rashid cleared his throat.

"My mother's village is in the Greyhills, sir," he said. "On the Corley side. Before I went to sea." He kept his eyes on the road. "The winter I was nine, the snow came early and the harvest was still in the fields, and the lord's steward took what there was for the rent. We were eating bark. And one night, a cart came up the track with no driver, and a mule, and six sacks of flour in it, and a side of bacon, and a barrel of salt fish. Nobody saw who brought it." He was quiet a moment. "Everybody knew."

Faro said nothing.

"I'm not saying he was a good man, sir," said Rashid. "He robbed my uncle once, on the Solace road, and took his boots, and my uncle walked home barefoot in the rain and cursed him for a month. I'm only saying." He shrugged, very slightly, the Sardhi shrug that meant a great deal or nothing. "They'll be eating bark again, this winter, in the hills."

They rode on. The marsh opened out round them, flat and wet and grey under the spring sky, and the larks went up out of the reeds on either side of the road, singing.

That night, at an inn on the edge of the hills, Faro sat alone at the window of his room with a cup of wine he did not drink, and watched the water in it. It did not tremble. It lay perfectly still, a small dark disc with the candle in it. He had done nothing, after all. There was nothing in him for it to tremble at.

He thought about a cart in the snow with no driver. He thought about a girl called Bet screaming over a stranger's shoulder all the way out of the square. He thought about how easy it would have been: a thread of water, a breath, a little cold. Less than he spent in an afternoon holding a face. And he had not spent it, because the man was a thief, and because it was not his business, and because nobody had come for Edric Vane.

He found that the last reason was the true one, and that he did not like it, and that it had been in him, unexamined, like a stone in a shoe, for a very long time.

In the morning he rode on. He did not turn back. There was nothing to turn back for.

### Later

There was no Rook in the Greyhills when the Count of Cristal came north. There was Gil Harrow.

Harrow had been Rook's lieutenant, and was nothing like him: a narrow, sour, clever man of thirty with a crossbow and a long memory, who had taken the Greyhills band after the hanging and made it into something harder and meaner and more efficient. Harrow did not send corpses' fingers. When he took the Marshal's son off the Solace road in the spring, with his tutor and his outriders, he shot the tutor in the first hour for complaining, and sent Aren Morrow's own finger to Solace in a box on the third day, with the ransom letter.

Edric rode into the ravine on the fourth day with twelve men in black and silver, and there was no embrace in the cloister, and no *my lord, my lord*. There was a fight. Harrow's men had crossbows in the rocks above the monastery and the Count's men did not, and two of them died in the first volley; and then the Count stood in the middle of the ruined cloister, under the bolts, and the water came up out of the old monastery cistern and the stream in the ravine and the spring air itself, and Gil Harrow's band learned what had become of the blighted things on the beaches of Cristal.

Aren Morrow saw it all from the mouth of the cave, with his left hand wrapped in a bloody rag. He rode down out of the hills beside the man who had saved him and never once, the whole road to Solace, could bring himself to look at him. When his mother came running down the steps of the Marshal's palace, he went to her past the Count without a word. Edric saw her look at her son's hand, and then at him, and then at the Tidemark on his throat that he had been too tired to hide.

And in the autumn, when the Lord Treasurer rode north through the Greyhills at night with two million marks in his saddlebags, there was nobody in the ravine who owed the Count of Cristal anything. Harrow's band was dead or scattered. Dorran Quell rode through the passes untouched and over the border into the south, and Edric, alone in the study of the house with the blue door, had to reach for him across three hundred miles of land with Undertow, through the night, until his nose bled and the salt choked him, and pull the money out of the saddlebags letter of credit by letter of credit, from banks that suddenly would not honour them. He never saw Dorran's face. He never sat with him in a cave. Whatever mercy he might have found there, he never had the chance to find.

## Save him, and make him swear

He had a quarter of an hour, perhaps less. He did not reach for the rope.

He reached for the hangman.

He had seen him climb into the cart: a stooped grey man of sixty with the hood rolled up on his head and hands that were not quite steady, and he had felt him then, without trying, as he felt everyone, and passed on. Now he went back and found him, and looked properly. There was no relish in the man for his work; there was a dull, old, settled shame, the shame of a man who has done a thing for thirty years because it paid and nobody else would. There was a wife at home, coughing; Faro could feel the shape of her in him, a weight he carried everywhere, the way the blight-cough sat in the chests of half the coast. And there was a great, tired, aching want, very simple. Money for a physician. Money for a winter somewhere dry.

He walked through the crowd to the foot of the cart, quickly, a tall foreign gentleman in green silk who was plainly important and was let through, and stood by the wheel, and spoke to the hangman in a low voice while the priest was still reading.

"Your wife has the cough," he said.

The hangman's head jerked round.

"I'm a banker," said Faro. "From Port Jasmine. I deal in debts. Yours can be paid." He put his hand on the side of the cart, and when he took it away there was a purse lying on the boards by the hangman's foot, heavy and small. "There's a custom in the south. If the rope breaks, the Sun has refused him." He held the man's eyes. "Old rope breaks. Nobody blames the hangman for old rope."

The hangman looked at the purse. He looked at Faro, and Faro felt him try to get hold of the stranger's face, and fail, and give up. He looked at the noose in his own hands.

"They'll know," he said hoarsely.

"They'll suspect," said Faro. "That's not the same. And the crowd will be on your side. Listen to them."

The hangman listened. The square was full of that low, sullen, weeping sound. He looked down at the girl called Bet, crying in the front row with her little brother by the hand.

He put his foot over the purse.

Faro went back through the crowd to the steps of the town hall, and stood at the side, and waited. He watched the hangman climb up onto the beam, as hangmen do, to check the knot, and take a long time over it, with his back to the square, and a small knife in his hand that nobody saw. He watched him climb down, and draw the noose tight round Rook's neck, and step out of the cart, and slap the horse.

The cart went forward. Rook dropped.

And the rope parted at the beam with a crack like a pistol shot, and Vasco Rook fell six feet into the dirt of Fendle market square on his back with the noose still round his neck and the end of the rope trailing, and lay there, and stared up at the sky.

For a moment there was no sound at all.

Then the square exploded. He had never heard a noise like it, not in the Keep, not in a storm. Five hundred people roaring at once. *Refused! The Sun's refused him! Refused!* The girl Bet was screaming with joy. Men were throwing their hats in the air. The magistrate had come out onto the town hall steps, white with fury, shouting for the watch, and the watch looked at the crowd, and at each other, and stayed exactly where they were. And in the dirt, Vasco Rook sat up slowly, with his hands still tied behind him, and the frayed end of the rope in his lap, and looked at it, and began to laugh.

---

He found Rook that night in the stable of the Crossed Keys, where the landlady had hidden him in the hayloft from the Marshal's men, who had ridden in from Solace at dusk and were drinking in the taproom in a very bad temper. Rook was sitting in the hay with a jug of the landlady's best and a raw red ring round his neck, and when the tall foreign gentleman came up the ladder, he put down the jug.

"I saw you," he said. "In the square. By the steps. You talked to old Ned before the drop." He looked at Faro shrewdly in the light of the stable lantern. "It wasn't the Sun, was it?"

"No," said Faro.

"Didn't think so. The Sun and I've never been on speaking terms." Rook rubbed his neck. "What do you want, then? Nobody does a thing like that for nothing. You want men? I've got men. You want a coach robbed? A man killed? Name it."

"I want you to stop," said Faro.

Rook stared at him.

"Robbing travellers," said Faro. "On the king's road. I want you to swear you'll stop."

The outlaw king of the Greyhills looked at him as if he had asked for the moon in a sack.

"Stop," he said. "Just stop. And what are my lads to eat? Grass? There's sixty men in those hills, and their women, and their children. There's half of Fendle eats through the winter because I rob the Solace coaches. You saw that girl. Her da's roof." He laughed, not quite steadily. "You save my neck, and then you ask me to starve."

"No," said Faro. "I'll pay them."

Rook's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

"Sixty men," said Faro. "I'll pay them a wage, every quarter, through a banker in Corley, more than the coaches bring you in a good year. And they'll do what they do now: hold the Greyhills. Know every track and every cave. But instead of robbing what comes up the road, they'll keep it safe. No one robbed between Corley and the passes. No one hurt. You'll be the safest road in Valemont, and you'll know everything that moves on it, and you'll tell me." He paused. "And one day, perhaps in a few years, I'll ask you for a favour. Something you won't like. And you'll do it."

Rook looked at him for a long, long time in the lantern light.

"What are you?" he said at last.

"A banker. From Port Jasmine."

"Like hell." But he was smiling, slowly, a big untidy delighted smile, like a boy offered a new game. "You're mad. You know that? Paying bandits to guard the road." He picked up the jug, and drank, and put it down. "All right. All right, banker. I'll swear it."

He swore it the Greyhills way, which involved spitting on his palm and an oath about his mother's grave that he said with his eyes shut, very fast, and then once more, slowly, with them open. And then he held out his hand, and Faro took it, spit and all.

"No more travellers," said Vasco Rook. "On my mother. She'd have liked that, the old cow." His eyes were wet. He wiped them on his sleeve, and laughed. "Gods. She'd have laughed herself sick."

Faro climbed down the ladder, and walked out of the stable into the spring dark, and found, to his surprise, that he was not cold at all.

### Later

The Greyhills had been the safest road in Valemont for two years when the Count of Cristal came north in the spring. The carters said so, and the pilgrims; nobody knew why, and the Lord Marshal took the credit. The Marshal's son was taken that spring all the same, on the Solace road, by four young hotheads from Corley who had never been on a gallows and had never sworn anything; and it was Vasco Rook's men who found the boy in a sheep-fold on the second night, and Rook who sent to the Count, and held the boy as a guest in the ruined monastery, fed and comfortable and beaten at knucklebones, until the tall man in black rode up the ravine to take him home.

And when the Count's letter had come to the monastery, back in the spring, before he ever set foot in Solace, *if the Lord Treasurer ever comes north through the Greyhills alone, at night, with heavy saddlebags, keep him, feed him for a price, let him buy his bread with what he stole*, Rook read it twice, and sent it back.

*I swore to you on that rope,* he wrote, in a big, slow, laborious hand. *No more travellers. A man on the king's road with saddlebags is a traveller, my lord, whatever he's got in them. I'll stop him for you. I'll give him a bed and a fire and a supper, free, and send for you. But I'll not cage a man and starve him. Not for you, not for anyone. My mother would have my hide.*

So in the autumn, when the bank of Quell had fallen and the Lord Treasurer had fled north in the night, Rook's rider came to the house with the blue door; and Edric came up the ravine to find Dorran Quell not in a cave, starving, but sitting by the fire in the old monastery kitchen with his saddlebags at his feet and a bowl of mutton stew in his shaking hands, very frightened, untouched, and fed. And there was no eighth day, no letter of credit signed for a heel of bread, no man on a pallet of straw. There was only the purser of the *Gull's Promise* looking up from a bowl of stew at the face of the man he had sent to the Keep.

It was harder, that way. Edric had not expected that. There was nothing to forgive Dorran for that he had not already had nineteen years to forgive; nothing the hills had taken from him that Edric could give back. They sat across the fire from each other for a long time, the two of them, with Rook standing in the doorway, and neither of them knew how to begin.
