# Event 12: The Blighted Child

*Branches from Chapter 18, "Building on Ruins": in the fifth winter, some weeks after the night of "a shark that had come into the shallows and taken two of the watch before he could reach it", when one of the dead men's daughters begins to cough.*

## Heal her

*Heart + · Tide +*

Her name was Lissa Marrin, and she was eight years old, and on the night of the shark she had been sitting on the harbour steps below the wall with her father's supper in a cloth, waiting for him to come down off his watch.

She had seen it come out of the water. She had seen it take him. And then the wave of its going had taken her too, off the steps and into the black harbour, and she had been under for a long time before one of the Bettan boys dived in after her and dragged her out by the hair. She had swallowed half the harbour. She had coughed it up on the quay, and coughed, and coughed, and they had wrapped her in blankets and carried her home to her mother, and everyone had said how lucky she was.

That had been six weeks ago. She was still coughing.

Her mother came to the library on a wet night at the end of the winter. Dena Marrin was a Weir-woman, one of the second wave of families who had come out of the marsh in the third year, a thin dark fierce woman with her husband's death still sitting on her face like a bruise. She did not knock. She came in out of the rain and stood dripping on the floor of the library, in front of his table, and did not sit down when he asked her to.

"The physician says it's the blight-cough," she said. "From the water. From the thing. She swallowed its water, and it's in her now. He says there's nothing to be done." Her voice did not shake. "He says it will take her by the summer."

Faro put down his pen.

"They say in the Weir," said Dena Marrin, "that the old Wardens could draw sickness out of a body like a splinter. My grandmother said it. Old Ysolde said it." She was looking at him with her black eyes. "They say you're one of them. The last one. They say you can do anything." She was quiet a moment. "Can you do this?"

He did not answer at once. Across the room, by the fire, Hayda had looked up from her book, and was watching him.

He knew the answer. He had known it for a year. It was in the twelfth stone chest, in a slim book bound in grey sealskin, *On the Healing of the Waters*, written by a Warden of the House of Cristal who had been strong in Undertow and had spent her life in the city's hospitals. He had read it three times. *All sickness is a disturbance of the waters. A fever is a storm. A wasting is a drought. A drowning sickness is a flood. Find the current that is wrong, and draw it out, gently, as thou wouldst draw a splinter.* And then, on the last page, in a different ink, as if she had added it years afterwards: *But know that what thou drawest out must go somewhere. And the only place it can go is into thee.*

He thought of the blight. The sea's sickness, the thing three hundred years of chains had done. He thought of drawing that into himself, out of a child's lungs, on purpose. He thought of the cold, and the salt, and the Tidemark on his face, which had begun to climb in the autumn and had not stopped.

"Yes," he said. "I think I can."

---

The house was on the inner end of the harbour, among the Weir-folk stilt-houses, a single room on piles above the water with a stove and two beds and a net drying on the rafters. The child was in the smaller bed. She was awake. She looked at him with her mother's black eyes, enormous in her small thin face, and coughed, and he heard it: the wet, bubbling, drowning sound, deep in her chest.

"Hello, Lissa," he said. "I'm going to put my hand here. It'll be cold. I'm sorry."

"I know who you are," said the child, between coughs. "You're the Warden. You killed the shark."

"Not in time."

"No," she agreed. She looked at him gravely. "Papa said you're always cold. He said it's because the sea lives in you."

"Your papa was right." He sat down on the edge of the bed, and laid his hand flat on her chest, over the thin nightshirt. "Close your eyes, if you like."

She did not close them. She watched him the whole time.

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. He found the stillness, and in the stillness, he felt for the water in her.

It was like putting his hand into a pool and finding it full of weed. Her blood was quick and warm and clean, a child's blood. But in her lungs, at the bottom, there was something else: grey water, cold water, heavy and thick and wrong, lying in her like silt in a harbour, clinging to everything it touched. He knew it. He had felt it in the blighted things, a hundred times, on the shore at night: the same sickness, the same grey cold. It was in her the way it had been in the crab and the eel and the shark. It was the sea's own poison, and it was drowning her, a little more every day.

He reached for it, gently, the way you put your hand into a pool. And drew.

It did not want to come. It clung. He drew harder, and felt it begin to move, slowly, the way a stone moves when you lever it out of mud, and come up through her, out of the deep places of her lungs, into his hand. Into his arm. Into him.

It was cold. Not the clean cold of the Deep, the cold of the bottom of the world, that he had carried for five years and almost grown used to. This was a sick cold. A grey, slow, rotten cold, like the cold of a body left in the water too long. It went up his arm like a slug crawling. It went into his chest, and lay there, heavy, against his heart, and the Tidemark woke under it and burned, and he tasted salt, and rot, and saw, behind his eyes, the sea. The whole sea. Grey and thick and dying, with dead fish floating on it from horizon to horizon, and the weed rotting on the rocks, and something vast at the bottom of it, in the dark, choking.

He held on. He drew, and drew, for a long time, until there was nothing left in her lungs to draw, and the child's breathing under his hand was quiet and clear and slow.

Then he let go.

He got as far as the door. He got the door open, and leaned out over the rail of the walkway, and was sick into the harbour: sick with grey seawater, stinking, more of it than could possibly have been in him, on and on, until he was on his knees on the wet planks with his forehead against the rail and the rain on the back of his neck.

---

He did not remember getting back up the stair. Hayda told him afterwards that she and Dena Marrin had carried him between them, the whole way, through the rain, up four terraces, and that he had talked the whole time in a language neither of them knew, and that once, on the third terrace, he had stopped and laughed, a strange deep slow laugh that was not his, and Dena Marrin had nearly let go of him.

He slept for a day and a night. When he woke, in the library, by the fire, Hayda was sitting across from him with a cup of the Sardhi coffee going cold in her hands, watching him.

"She's asleep," Hayda said, before he could ask. "She's been asleep since you left her. She hasn't coughed once. Her mother sent this." She nodded at the table. There was a fish on it, a fine one, a sea bream, wrapped in leaves, and beside it a little clay lamp of the kind the Weir-folk floated on the harbour at Midsummer for the souls of the drowned. "The physician came to look at her this morning. He says it's impossible. He went away and got drunk."

He tried to sit up. The cold in him was enormous, heavier than it had ever been, and under it, very faint, there was a taste of rot that would not go away.

"Look at your face," said Hayda quietly.

He did not need to. He could feel it. The line on his left cheek, the single thin branching line that had begun to climb towards his eye in the autumn, had grown in the night. It had reached the eye. He could feel it there, cold, at the corner, like a tear that would not fall.

"That's what it costs," said Hayda. It was not a question. "To be kind."

"That's what it costs to do it this way."

"Is there another way?"

He thought about the book. *What thou drawest out must go somewhere.* He thought about the sea, grey and dying, and the thing at the bottom of it, choking on its own sickness. He thought about every child on every coast in Valemont with the blight-cough, and how many there were, and how much of the sea's poison he could carry before there was more of it in him than there was of him.

"Not while the chains hold," he said.

Hayda looked at him for a long time over her cold coffee. Then she got up, and poured it on the fire, where it hissed, and made him another, and put it into his hands.

"Then you'd better break them," she said. "Before you run out of face."

Far below, at the bottom of the world, something vast turned slowly in the dark, and he felt it, and felt it notice the new cold in him, the grey cold, and he thought he heard it say, very softly, with something in it that might have been pity and might have been satisfaction: *You took my sickness into you, little one. Now you carry a little more of me. Did you think kindness would be free?*

### Later

The letters came to Solace by every ship from the west. They were addressed in large careful capitals to THE COUNT OF CRISTAL, THE HOUSE WITH THE BLUE DOOR, and Bastien, who sorted the post, learned to put them on top.

*Dear Warden. I am learning to read. Tam says I am the best at the old letters. Mama says to tell you I am well and have not coughed. The sea talks at night now. Not in words. Tam says it is because you put some of yourself in me when you took the sick out, like when you pour water from one cup to another and a little stays. I am not frightened of it. It sounds sad. Is it sad? Your friend, Lissa Marrin.*

He read that one in the carriage on the way back from Grey Point, on the night he had sat for three hours by a mirror-keeper's granddaughter with his hand on her chest. It had been easier, the second time. He had known what was coming. He had known how to draw, and where to put it, and how to hold on afterwards until he reached the door. It had not hurt less. But he had known it would hurt, and that had made it possible to begin.

He had been sick on Hob Fennick's floor, all the same. And the line on his face had crept on past his eye, and the taste of rot sat at the back of his throat for a week.

He wrote back that night, from Solace, by the light of a single lamp, in large careful capitals so that she could read it herself.

*Dear Lissa. Yes. It is very sad. It has been sad for three hundred years. But I am going to try to make it stop. When it does, listen again, and tell me what it sounds like. Your friend, the Warden.*

## Send her to the hospital

Her name was Lissa Marrin, and she was eight years old, and on the night of the shark she had been sitting on the harbour steps below the wall with her father's supper in a cloth, waiting for him to come down off his watch. She had seen it take him. And then the wave of its going had taken her too, off the steps and into the black harbour, and she had swallowed half of it before one of the Bettan boys dragged her out by the hair.

That had been six weeks ago. She was still coughing.

Her mother came to the library on a wet night at the end of the winter, and stood dripping on the floor in front of his table, and told him what the physician had said. The blight-cough. From the water. It would take her by the summer.

"They say the old Wardens could draw sickness out like a splinter," said Dena Marrin. "Can you do this?"

He did not answer at once.

He knew the answer. It was in the twelfth stone chest, in a slim book bound in grey sealskin, *On the Healing of the Waters*. He had read it three times. He had read the last page more than three. *What thou drawest out must go somewhere. And the only place it can go is into thee.*

He thought of the blight. He had felt it a hundred times, on the shore, in the dark, in the blighted things he killed: a grey, rotten, heavy cold, the sea's own sickness. He thought of drawing it into himself, on purpose. He thought of the Tidemark on his face, which had begun to climb in the autumn and had not stopped. He thought of the spring, and Solace, and the three men waiting there, and how much of himself he would need to have left.

And he thought of the hospital.

It was on the second terrace, beside Tam's school, in the old Tidewarden house of healing whose name was still cut over the door. He had paid for it himself, every stone. It had twenty beds and two physicians and a Sardhi apothecary who knew more about the blight-cough than any man in Valemont, and steam-rooms where the sick could breathe the vapour of eucalyptus and pine, and good food, and clean water from the new cisterns. Children came out of it alive. Not all of them. But some.

"The hospital," he said. "She should go to the hospital. Tonight. Doctor Saleh is the best on the coast with the cough. I'll go with you and tell him she's to have whatever she needs."

Dena Marrin looked at him for a long time.

"The hospital," she said.

"It's the best place for her."

"I didn't ask what the best place for her was." Her voice was very quiet. "I asked if you could do it."

He met her eyes. He found he could not lie to her. He found, also, that he could not say yes.

"I don't know," he said. "I've never tried. It might not work. And if it does, it costs something. Not money." He touched his cheek, without meaning to. "I don't know how much I have left to spend."

She looked at his hand, and at the faint blue line under it, for a long moment. Then she looked away.

"The hospital, then," said Dena Marrin.

---

He went with them. He carried the child himself, wrapped in a blanket, up the stair in the rain, while her mother walked beside him and did not speak. Lissa was very light. She coughed against his shoulder the whole way, the wet, bubbling, drowning sound, and between coughs, she looked up at him with her mother's black eyes.

"You're the Warden," she said. "You killed the shark."

"Not in time."

"No," she agreed. "Papa said you're always cold. He said the sea lives in you."

"Your papa was right."

"Can it hear me?" said the child. "The sea? If I talk to it?"

He did not know how to answer that. "Maybe," he said. "I don't know."

"I'll ask it to make me better, then," said Lissa Marrin, and coughed, and put her head down on his shoulder, and was quiet.

The hospital was warm, and smelled of eucalyptus, and Doctor Saleh came down himself in his nightshirt, a small grey precise Sardhi with long clever fingers, and listened to the child's chest for a long time with his ear against it, and did not change his expression. He put her in a bed by the window, in the long ward, among eleven other beds. Every bed had a child in it. Every child was coughing. The sound of it filled the room like the sound of the sea in the caves under the island.

"How many?" said Faro, at the door, quietly.

Doctor Saleh did not pretend to misunderstand him. "Of the blight-cough? In this ward, since the autumn, nineteen." He took off his spectacles and polished them. "Eleven have gone home. Eight we have buried." He put the spectacles back on. "Her lungs are very full, Warden. She swallowed a great deal of the sick water. I will not tell you what I think. I will tell you that I will do everything there is, and that it is a little more than nothing."

"Whatever she needs."

"She needs a miracle," said Doctor Saleh. "I am an apothecary." He looked at Faro for a moment over his spectacles, with an expression that was not quite a question. Then he bowed, and went back to the ward.

Faro stood in the doorway a little longer. He could hear the child coughing, by the window. He could feel her, the way he felt everything on the island now, through the water: her quick warm clean blood, and under it, at the bottom of her lungs, the grey cold, heavy and clinging, like silt. He could feel exactly where it was. He could feel exactly how he would reach for it.

He turned and went out into the rain.

Halfway down the stair to the first terrace, he stopped. He stood on the wet stone with the rain running off his hat and his hand on the cold balustrade, and felt the hospital behind him, and the child in it, and the grey cold at the bottom of her lungs. He stood there for a long time. Long enough that a man of the watch, coming up the stair with a lantern, slowed and looked at him and asked if the Warden was well.

"Quite well," said Faro. "Thank you."

He went on down. He did not look back. He thought that if he looked back, he would go back, and he did not know, any more, whether that would be the brave thing or only the easy one.

Hayda was waiting for him at the foot of the library stair, with her cloak over her head. She had seen him go down with the child, and come back without her.

"Well?" she said.

"She's in the hospital. Saleh has her."

Hayda looked at him in the rain for a long time with her dark still eyes. She did not say anything. She did not need to.

"I know," he said. "I know."

He went up the stair past her to the library, and sat at his table, and opened the grey sealskin book to the last page, and read it again, and did not sleep.

### Later

He spent the rest of the winter pouring gold into the hospital. A new wing. Ten more beds. Two more physicians, brought from the Sardh at a price that made Bastien whistle. A ship sent to Mazrah for every medicine the apothecaries' quarter could sell, and another to the north for pine resin and eucalyptus by the barrel. Doctor Saleh accepted all of it with a grave bow and said nothing, and Faro was grateful to him for that.

In the spring, on the last morning before the *Slip* sailed for the mainland, he climbed to the second terrace and stood in the doorway of the long ward.

Lissa Marrin was sitting up in her bed by the window, thin as a reed, with a bowl of soup on her knees and a book open beside it that Tam had brought her. She was coughing still, a little. Doctor Saleh said she would always cough a little, now, in the winters, and would never be able to run as far as other children. But she was alive. She looked up and saw him in the doorway and waved her spoon.

The bed beside hers was empty, and freshly made. A Sardhi boy of six had been in it all winter. He had gone in the night, a week ago, the same night Lissa had first sat up and asked for soup. Doctor Saleh did not know why the one had lived and the other had not. Nobody knew. He had done the same for both of them. More than nothing. A little more.

"I asked the sea," Lissa told the Warden, when he came and sat on the edge of her bed. "Like I said. Every night. To make me better." She considered. "I don't think it heard. I think it was too far away."

"Maybe," said Faro.

He sat with her until the bell rang in the harbour tower for the tide. Then he went down to the ship, and stood at the stern as she sailed out past the light, and looked back at the second terrace for as long as he could see it, and thought about the empty bed, and about how he would never know, now, whether his hand would have made any difference to it at all.

## Refuse

*Heart − · Tide 0*

Her name was Lissa Marrin, and she was eight years old, and on the night of the shark she had been sitting on the harbour steps below the wall with her father's supper in a cloth, waiting for him to come down off his watch. She had seen it take him, and then the wave of its going had taken her, and she had swallowed half the harbour before a Bettan boy dragged her out by the hair.

That had been six weeks ago. She was still coughing.

Her mother came to the library on a wet night at the end of the winter and stood dripping on the floor in front of his table.

"The blight-cough," said Dena Marrin. "From the water. It will take her by the summer." Her voice did not shake. "They say the old Wardens could draw sickness out of a body like a splinter. They say you're one of them. Can you do this?"

Across the room, by the fire, Hayda had looked up from her book.

He knew the answer. It was in the twelfth stone chest, in a slim book bound in grey sealskin. *On the Healing of the Waters.* He had read it three times. He had read the last page more than three. *What thou drawest out must go somewhere. And the only place it can go is into thee.*

He had thought about it, a great deal, in the long nights in the library, long before tonight. He had known that someone would come one day and ask. He had worked it out the way Faro had taught him to work things out, coldly, all the way to the bottom.

There were children with the blight-cough in every town on the coast of Valemont. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. It had come up out of the sea in the last ten years, and it was getting worse every year, because the sea was getting sicker every year, because a man in a tower in Solace was drinking it to death. If he healed this child, he would be drawing the sea's sickness into himself, the Deep's own poison, and it would go into the cold in him and stay there and make it deeper. And then there would be another child. And another. And word would go round the Reach, the way word always did, and they would come from the mainland in boats, and he would be drawing the blight out of children every night of his life until there was more of the sea's sickness in him than there was of him. And the chains would still hold. And the King would still drink. And the children would still be dying, all along the coast, faster than one man could ever heal them.

In the spring, he was going to Solace. He was going to need everything he had. Every drop of himself that the Deep had not yet taken. And at the end of Solace there was the Teeth, and the Lock, and the chains, and if he could break them, the blight would end. Not for one child. For all of them. Forever.

*One child,* said the cold voice in him, very quietly, very reasonably. *Against all of them.*

"No," he said.

---

Dena Marrin did not move. The rain dripped off her shawl onto the library floor.

"No you can't," she said. "Or no you won't."

He made himself meet her eyes. He owed her that.

"I think I could," he said. "I'm not certain. But I think so. And it would cost me. It would put the sickness into me, and I need to be whole for what I have to do in the spring. If I begin with her, I'll never be able to stop. There are too many. And what I'm going to do in Solace," he hesitated, "what I'm going to try to do, if it works, will end the blight for everyone. Every child on the coast." He heard how it sounded. He said it anyway. "I'm sorry. I can't spend myself on one."

Dena Marrin looked at him for a long time.

"On one," she said. "My Lissa's *one*."

"Yes."

"Her father died on your wall." Her voice was perfectly steady. "He walked your wall every night for two years because you asked. Because you said Cristal needed a watch. And a thing came out of the sea, the sea you talk to, the sea that lives in you, and it took him while you were on your way down the stair. And now it's taking her. And you're going to let it, because you've somewhere to be in the spring." She pulled her shawl tight. "I hope Solace is worth it, Warden. I hope it's worth a great deal."

She went out into the rain. The door banged in the wind behind her.

---

He sat at his table for a long time. The fire burned down. He told himself that he was right. He went through it again, all of it, the thousands of children on the coast and the chains and the spring, and it came out the same every time, as neat as a column of Dorran's figures. He was right. He was almost sure of it. That was what made it unbearable.

Hayda had not moved from her chair. She had not said a word. She had not looked away, either. When he finally looked up, she was still watching him, with her dark still eyes, and there was nothing in her face at all: not anger, not disgust. Only attention. The way you watch a stranger.

"You think I'm wrong," he said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You don't have to."

"No," Hayda agreed. She closed her book. "I was only thinking. You said once, on the deck of the *Slip*, that it was my choice from then on. Always. Everything." She stood up. "And I was thinking that a man who knows how much a single person's choice is worth ought to know how much a single child is."

She went to the door, and stopped there, with her hand on the latch.

"My father," she said, without turning, "could have paid the pale captain off. When the city was falling. He had the gold. His advisers told him to. They said: give him what he asks, and save the city, and fight him later, when you're strong." She was quiet. "My father said no. He said, I will not spend my people to buy my war." She opened the door. "He lost. Everyone died. Perhaps he was a fool." She looked back at him. "But I loved him."

The door closed behind her.

He sat in the library in the dark, alone, with the grey sealskin book open on the table in front of him at the last page, and the rain on the broken dome, and down at the inner end of the harbour, among the Weir-folk stilt-houses, he could feel, without trying, without wanting to, a child coughing in her sleep. He could feel exactly where the grey cold lay in her. He could feel exactly how he would reach for it.

He sat there until dawn, and did not get up.

### Later

Lissa Marrin died in the first week of summer, with the windows open and the sea outside, and her mother holding her hand. The Weir-folk buried her on the headland above the harbour, beside her father's empty grave, and floated a lamp on the water for her that night, and a hundred more for every child on the island the blight had taken that year. The Warden was in Solace by then. They did not send to tell him. Jac told him, in a letter, in two lines.

He was at Grey Point when he read it, on the coast road twenty miles south of Solace, at the foot of a mirror tower, with the keeper's granddaughter asleep in the next room with colour in her cheeks for the first time in a year, and the taste of rot in his mouth, and the line on his face a finger's breadth nearer his eye. He had sat by the child for three hours with his hand on her chest. He had drawn the blight out of her the way the book said. And it had worked, and he had been sick on the floor with grey seawater, and in a week, when the old keeper was well enough to listen, he would ask him for a false signal from his mirror, and the old man would give it, because of what the Count had done for Nell.

He sat in the carriage outside the tower with Jac's letter on his knee for a long time.

*One child,* he had said. *Against all of them.*

He had not been able to spend himself on Lissa Marrin, who had nothing to give him. He had spent himself on Nell Fennick without a second thought, because her grandfather kept a mirror.

He folded the letter, very carefully, and put it in his coat, over his heart, where the cold was. He carried it there until the chains.
