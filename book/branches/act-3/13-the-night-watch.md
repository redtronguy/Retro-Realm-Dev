# Event 13: The Night Watch

*Branches from Chapter 18, "Building on Ruins": on a night in the second winter, when the blighted things come ashore in greater numbers than ever before, at the moment when "they rang the bell in the harbour tower, and Faro came down."*

*The canon path, in which he fights them alone with Riptide while the watch looks on, continues in [Chapter 18](../../18-building-on-ruins.md).*

## Fight beside them

*Heart + · Tide 0*

He heard the bell from the library, and was on the stair before the second stroke.

He felt them as he ran. He felt them coming up through the black water of the harbour like a fever coming up through a body: not one, tonight, or two, but many. Eight. Ten. More, further out, still coming. Crabs the size of carts. Things that had been eels, long and pale and blind. Something low and heavy and many-legged that he had no name for. All of them sick, all of them mad with it, all of them dragging themselves towards the lamps on the wall the way moths go to a flame.

The watch was on the shore wall below the Weir-folk's houses, where the shingle ran up from the water. Thirty of them, perhaps, in a ragged line along the top of the wall, with spears and nets and pitch-torches, and Jac in the middle of them with a cutlass in each hand. Below them, on the shingle, at the edge of the lamplight, the first of the things were coming out of the sea.

He came down the last flight of steps at a run, and stopped.

He could see what would happen. He had done it a dozen times. He would go down onto the shingle, alone, past the wall, and reach, and the sea would come up into him, eager, cold, and he would pull the water out of the stones and the air and the harbour into blades, thin hard edges of current, and drive them through the armour of the things one after another, cleanly, coldly, without wasted motion, the way a swordsman uses a sword. It would take a quarter of an hour. The watch would stand on the wall with their spears and their torches and watch him do it. They would not have to fight at all.

They had not had to fight at all for a year.

He looked along the wall at them. At their faces, in the torchlight. He saw relief in them. Relief that he had come. And under the relief, something else, something he had begun to see more and more in the last months and did not like: a kind of waiting. As if they were not a watch any more, but an audience. Two men at the far end of the line had not even brought spears.

And he felt the cold in him, at the bottom, under his heart, waiting too. Eager. *Go down, little one. Go down to the shore. You know you want to.*

He went up onto the wall instead, and took a spear from the rack at the corner tower, and stood in the line between Jac and a Weir-folk fisherman called Gennet Hale, and set the butt of the spear against the stone.

Jac looked at him sideways. "What are you doing?"

"Standing watch," said Faro.

"There's a dozen of them."

"I know."

"You could have them all on the shingle in a quarter of an hour."

"I know that too." He did not look at her. He was watching the first of the things, a crab as big as a rowing boat, dragging itself up the stones towards the foot of the wall. "And next time there'll be twenty, and I'll be in Solace, or asleep, or sick, and you'll all be standing up here waiting for me." He tightened his grip on the spear. "I'll help. I'll take the big ones. But not alone. Not any more."

Jac looked at him for a long moment. Then she raised her voice, the voice she used in a gale.

"You heard the Warden!" she bellowed along the wall. "Nets on the left! Fire on the right! Spears to me! And Olaf, if you drop that torch again, I'll feed you to the crabs myself!"

---

It was the longest night of his life since the Keep.

They came over the wall in a wave, all of them at once, as if the sea had pushed them. The crab first, its great blotched claw coming up over the parapet and closing on the stone with a crack that chipped it. Gennet Hale put his spear into the soft place under its eye-stalks and leaned on it with his whole weight, shouting, and the thing reared back, and the Bettan boys threw a net over its claw and hauled, and Faro, beside them, drove his own spear into the joint of its armour where the leg met the body, and felt it go in, and twist, and the thing screamed.

He did not use the sea. Not for that one. He used his arms, and his weight, and the spear, and he felt the ache of it in his shoulders, the burn, the way he had felt it on the capstan of the *Slip* in his first month at sea, and it was strange and hard and frightening, and it was his. He had forgotten what that felt like. To do a thing with only what he was.

The crab went down. Something else came over the wall.

It went on for hours. He lost count of them. An eel as long as a ship's mast, white and blind, that came over the wall at the Weir end and took a man off his feet before the nets could reach it; Faro reached for the sea then, because he had to, a single blade of water, quick and small, and cut it in half, and let go at once, before the cold could take hold. A thing like a seal, swollen and grey, that came up the steps by the harbour tower on its flippers, mewing like a child, and Big Olaf stood in its way with a torch in each hand and wept, and drove it back into the sea with fire. Two more crabs, together, on the left, where the nets were. And in the grey hour before dawn, the heavy many-legged thing he had no name for, which came out of the water slowly and climbed the wall slowly and was the strongest of all of them, and which took four spears and a net and Jac's two cutlasses and, at the end, Faro's own body thrown between it and Gennet Hale.

It caught him across the ribs with something hard and sharp. He felt it go through his coat and his shirt and into him, and then he was on his back on the stones of the parapet with the sky going round, and the thing above him, and Jac's voice screaming somewhere, and then Gennet Hale's spear, and the Bettan boys', and the thing falling sideways off the wall onto the shingle below with a sound like a cartload of stones.

The sea was right there. He could feel it, at the foot of the wall, waiting, eager. He could have lifted it up and over all of them like a hand and finished it. He lay on his back on the cold stones with his hand pressed to his side and the blood coming through his fingers, and did not reach.

The thing on the shingle twitched, and was still.

---

The sun came up on a beach covered in dead things, and a wall with a great many people sitting on it, very quietly, not speaking.

Three of the watch were hurt. One of them, a young man from the second terrace, would not walk again without a stick. Nobody had died. It was the first raid of that size, Jac said afterwards, when nobody had died except the things.

Faro sat with his back against the corner tower while the Sardhi apothecary from the hospital sewed up his side with a curved needle and black thread. It hurt a great deal. He found that he did not mind. It was a clean hurt, an ordinary hurt, the kind any man might have. There was no salt in it.

Gennet Hale came and sat down beside him, heavily, with his spear across his knees. The fisherman's face was grey with exhaustion and streaked with something black that was not blood.

"You took that thing off me," he said. "That last one. You put yourself in front of it." He was quiet. "You didn't have to do that, Warden. You could've just. You know." He made a vague gesture at the sea.

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

Faro looked along the wall at the watch. They were sitting in twos and threes, sharing water, binding each other's hands, laughing a little, the way men laugh after a fight they did not expect to survive. The Bettan boys were arguing about which of them had put the first net on the crab. Big Olaf was singing, quietly, to the dead seal-thing on the shingle below, a hymn.

"Because it's your wall," he said. "Not mine."

Gennet Hale looked at him for a long time. Then he nodded, slowly, and got up, and went down onto the shingle to help drag the dead things into a heap for burning.

That night, twelve more names went up on the board in the harbour tower, under *Watch*. The next week, twenty.

### Later

The bell rang in the harbour tower of Cristal on a night in the nineteenth year, at the dark of the moon, at the turn of the tide, when the Warden was a fortnight's sail away in a house with a blue door in Solace, and could not come down.

He felt it. He was standing on the flat roof of the house in the dark, looking west, and he felt it far off, through the water, through all the water in the world, the way you feel a door slam at the other end of a great house: a raid, a big one, coming ashore at Cristal. Twenty things. More. He stood with his hands on the parapet and his eyes shut and the cold surging in him, eager, and he could not reach that far. Nobody could.

Jac's letter came a fortnight later, on a Sardhi spice-trader. She had put in at Cristal with the *Slip* two days before the raid.

*Twenty-six of the beggars,* she wrote, in her big untidy hand. *Biggest raid yet. Came ashore at the Weir end and all along the hook. We had a hundred and ten on the wall, and Tam's schoolchildren carrying torches, and the Bettan grandmothers throwing boiling oil off their balconies, which I didn't ask them to do and won't stop them doing again. Gennet Hale commanded the Weir end. Lost two. Nobody else. Burned the lot on the shingle by morning.* And then, at the bottom, squeezed in: *Didn't miss you at all. Come home soon, you great fool. We kept your spear on the rack.*

He read it on the roof, in the morning sun, which hurt, and read it again. Then he folded it up and put it inside his coat, and found, a little to his own surprise, that his mouth had moved.

## Calm the creatures

*Heart + · Tide −*

He heard the bell from the library, and was on the stair before the second stroke.

He felt them as he ran: many of them tonight, more than ever before, coming up through the black water of the harbour like a fever coming up through a body. Crabs the size of carts. Things that had been eels. A seal, or what had been a seal. Something low and heavy and many-legged that he had no name for. All of them sick, all of them mad with it, dragging themselves towards the lamps on the wall the way moths go to a flame.

And he felt, as he always felt, what they were feeling. That was the thing he had never told anyone, not even Jac. When he reached for them, on the shore, before he killed them, he felt it: the pain. A grey, slow, rotten pain, everywhere, in every part of them, the sea's own sickness swollen up inside them until there was no room left for anything else. They did not come ashore to hunt. They came ashore to get away from the water, because the water hurt.

He had tried to calm them, in the first winter. He had reached into the first one, the crab on the shingle, the way he would reach for a sick man's fever, and tried to still it, and it had screamed and turned on him, and he had had to kill it. He had tried again, twice, since. It had been the same. You could not still a thing that was made of pain. Reaching into it only made the pain worse. He had learned that the only mercy was to end it quickly.

But on the stair tonight, running down towards the bell, he thought of a page in the twelfth stone chest. Not *On the Healing of the Waters*, which was about drawing out. Another book, older, which he had read once and not understood. *On the Keeping of the Balance.* A line near the end, in a cramped and ancient hand.

*The Warden does not command the sea. The Warden returns to it. In the old days, when a sickness came on the waters, the Wardens of the coast went down into the sea at the turn of the tide, and gave back to it what they had taken, and the sea was soothed.*

*Gave back.* He had not understood. He had thought it was a figure of speech. He had been holding the Deep for a year and a half now, carrying it, using it, taking more of it every time he fought, and he had never once thought of giving any of it back. He had not known you could.

He came down onto the shore wall below the Weir-folk's houses. The watch was there, thirty of them, in a ragged line along the top of the wall, with Jac in the middle, and they turned when they saw him and their faces in the torchlight filled with relief. Below them, on the shingle, at the edge of the lamplight, the first of the things were coming out of the sea.

He did not stop on the wall. He went down the steps onto the shingle, and past the crab that was dragging itself up the stones, so close he could have touched its blotched shell, and on, down to the water's edge, and into the sea.

"*Faro!*" Jac's voice, from the wall, cracking. "What in the nine hells—"

He walked out until the water was at his waist. It was cold, and black, and full of them. He could feel them all around him in the dark, moving, sick, mad. One of the eel-things went past his legs, long and pale and blind, and turned, and came back. He stood still. He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight.

And instead of reaching, he opened his hands.

---

It was the hardest thing he had ever done.

He had spent a year and a half learning to take. To reach for the sea and let it come up into him, the cold, the strength, the eager rushing power of it. He had never learned to let it go. The Deep did not want to go. It had found a place in him, under his heart, along the lines of the Tidemark, and it had made itself at home there, and when he reached for it now, not to use it but to give it away, it held on. It clung to him the way the blight clung to the blighted things.

*Little one,* said the voice, very far off, at the bottom of the world. Surprised. *What are you doing?*

He did not answer. He took hold of the cold in him, the clean cold, the cold of the bottom of the world that he had carried since the night under the Teeth, and pushed it out. Down his arms. Out through his open hands. Into the water.

It tore. It was like pulling a hook out of his own flesh, barbed, backwards. He felt it tear loose from under his heart and go out of him, and he cried out, and the sound went across the black harbour like a gull's cry, and on the wall, thirty people flinched. He felt the Tidemark on his chest burn as if someone had laid a brand on it. And he felt the cold go out into the sea around him, clean and deep and old, the water of the very bottom of the world, before the chains, before the sickness, the sea as it had been when the white city stood on the cliffs and people raised their hands to it from the shore.

It went out from him in a ring, like a stone dropped in a pond.

The eel-thing at his legs went still.

He felt it. He felt the grey rotten pain in it ease, just a little, just for a moment, where the clean water touched it. Not healed. Not cured. Only soothed: the way a cold cloth soothes a fever, the way a hand on a forehead soothes a child. The thing hung in the water beside him, quite still, with its blind white eyes, and he felt it feel the clean water, and he felt something in it that was not madness, for the first time. Something very small and very old. Like memory.

It turned, slowly, and went back out to sea.

He gave more. He kept giving. He stood in the black water with his hands open and the cold pouring out of him, and every drop of it hurt, and the ring went out wider, and wider, across the harbour. He felt the crab on the shingle stop, halfway up the stones, and turn, and drag itself back down into the water. He felt the seal-thing go still, mewing, and then quiet. He felt them all, one by one, the things in the dark, ease, and slow, and turn. Back to the sea. Back to the deep water. Away from the lamps.

Not all of them.

The heavy many-legged thing he had no name for did not turn. It was too far gone. He felt it, at the Weir end of the shore, at the foot of the stilt-houses, and there was nothing in it now but the pain, nothing at all, and the clean water broke over it like a wave over a rock and did nothing. It came out of the sea and up the shingle and over the wall at the Weir end, where he could not reach it, where he was standing in the harbour up to his waist with his hands open and his strength pouring out of him into the sea.

He heard the screams. He heard Jac's voice, and the clash of spears. He could not turn round. If he stopped now, if he closed his hands and reached for the sea to use it, the others would come back.

He did not stop.

---

They killed it, in the end, at the Weir end, with nets and spears and fire and Jac's two cutlasses. It took them half the night. It took a Weir-folk fisherman called Gennet Hale, who stood in its way on the walkway in front of his own house with his children behind him, and did not move, and did not get up again. It took the use of two fingers of Jac's left hand.

He came up out of the water at dawn.

He could barely walk. Two of the Bettan boys came down the shingle and took him under the arms and half-carried him up onto the wall, and he sat with his back against the corner tower, shaking. He was warm. That was the strangest thing. For the first time in a year and a half, he was warm, the ordinary warmth of a living man's blood, and he was so weak with it that he could not lift his hands. The salt was gone from his mouth. He looked down at his chest, through his open shirt, and saw the lines of the Tidemark, pale in the dawn, and they had drawn back. Not far. A finger's breadth, at the wrists. But back.

Jac came and stood in front of him. Her left hand was bound up in a rag, and there was blood on it, and her face was white.

"Gennet's dead," she said.

"I know. I felt it."

"You were in the *water*, Faro. Standing there with your arms out like a priest, while that thing came over the Weir wall. We needed you. *He* needed you."

"I know."

"Then *why*?"

He looked out at the harbour. It was very calm in the dawn, grey and flat and quiet. There was nothing on the shingle. No dead things. No heaps for burning. For the first time after a raid, there was nothing on the shore at all.

"Because they were in pain," he said. "All of them. Every one I ever killed. And I didn't know, until tonight, that there was anything else I could do." He closed his eyes. "I'm sorry about Gennet. I'm sorrier than I can say. But eleven of them went back to the sea tonight, Jac, and didn't die, and didn't kill anyone. And next time, there'll be fewer."

Jac looked at him for a long time. Then she sat down beside him against the tower, heavily, with her bound hand in her lap, and did not say anything else.

Down at the Weir end, Gennet Hale's widow was standing on her walkway in the grey light, looking at the water. And Faro, sitting against the tower, so weak he could hardly lift his head, felt the sea under the island for the first time in a year and a half not as something eager, reaching for him. But as something quiet. Something, for one moment, eased.

### Later

There were fewer, the next time. Four, instead of eleven. And the time after that, three. By the fourth year, the blighted things came ashore at Cristal so seldom that the watch walked the walls for months at a time without ringing the bell, and smugglers in the Reach began to say that the island had a blessing on it.

It was not a blessing. It was the Weir-folk.

They had watched him, that first night, from their stilt-houses: the Warden walking out into the black harbour up to his waist and standing there with his hands open, like the people in the oldest carvings in the citadel. Mother Bettan had watched longest. And at the next dark of the moon, when the bell rang, he went down to the shore and found her already there, at the water's edge, with her stick, and a dozen of the Weir-folk around her, and Gennet Hale's widow among them. They walked into the sea behind him. They stood in a line in the water with their hands open, the way he stood, though there was nothing in them to give. And they sang.

It was an old song, the oldest the Weir-folk had: a lullaby, Mother Bettan said, that had been sung to the sea long before anyone could remember why. He did not know if it did anything. Perhaps it only made the giving easier to bear. But the things came out of the dark water and stopped at the edge of the singing, and hung there, quiet, and turned, and went back. And once, in the fifth year, a blighted seal came all the way in through the line and lay down in the shallows at Mother Bettan's feet, and put its swollen grey head against her knee, and died there, quite peacefully, while she stroked it and sang.

The Tidemark never drew back further than that first finger's breadth. It was still on his face, the night before he sailed for Solace. But it had climbed more slowly than the books said it should, and on the nights after the singing, for an hour or two, he was warm.
