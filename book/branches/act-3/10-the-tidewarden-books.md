# Event 10: The Tidewarden Books

*Branches from Chapter 18, "Building on Ruins": in the first winter, in the room in the old library "where he had brought the twelve stone chests up from the sea-cave and unpacked them onto new shelves of new wood", when he reaches the bottom of the twelfth chest and finds it is not empty.*

## Seal it again

*Heart + · Tide 0*

The casket was at the very bottom of the twelfth chest, under the last layer of books, packed round with dry seaweed that crumbled to dust when he touched it.

It was not large. A box of grey lead, a little longer than his forearm, a little deeper than his hand, and heavier than it had any right to be. The lid was sealed all round with the same black pitch as the stone chests, hard as glass. But on the top, where the other chests had borne only the curling wave of the Order, this one bore the wave with a bar cut through it, deep and straight, as if someone had struck it out with a chisel. And under the wave, in the oldest form of the Weir-tongue, scratched into the lead with a point, there were three lines.

*Here are the Bindings, and the way of Ys.*
*Let no Warden open this alone.*
*Let no Warden open it at all, who has not first asked why he wishes to.*

He sat back on his heels on the cold stone floor and looked at it for a long time.

It was very late. The lamp on the table had burned low, and the wind was coming through the broken dome above him, and somewhere far below, at the foot of the island, the sea was singing in its caves. On the far side of the room, among the new shelves, Tam was asleep on a heap of sacking with a book open on his chest. The boy had been helping him unpack for a week, every night after the day's work on the quays, carrying the books up from the cave in baskets and laying them out on the shelves, and Faro had begun to teach him the old letters. Tam learned them the way a thirsty man drinks. He had fallen asleep in the middle of *On the Keeping of the Balance*, with his finger on the line.

Faro looked at the casket.

He knew what the Bindings were. He had read enough, already, in the first eleven chests, to know. They were mentioned everywhere, in the margins, in the footnotes, in the chronicles: always briefly, always with a kind of flinch. The art of reaching not into water but into the water in a person, and through it into the person themselves, into the will. Of making a man still, the way you might still a pool. Of making him want what you wanted. The Order had used them once, the chronicles said, in its first centuries, and then had stopped, and then had forbidden them, and then had stopped talking about them at all.

And the way of Ys. He did not need to be told what that was.

He put his hand on the lid.

He could feel it through the lead. Not words; the lead was too thick for that, and the pitch too hard. But the books inside were written in an ink made with seawater, the way all the Order's books were, and he could feel the water in the ink, faintly, the way you feel the warmth of a hearth through a wall. And it was not like the water in the other books. The water in the other books was quiet. This was not. It moved, very slightly, under his palm, the way a sleeping animal moves. It knew he was there. It wanted him to open it.

*Who has not first asked why he wishes to.*

He asked.

He sat on the floor of the library in the dark, with his hand on the lead, and asked himself why, honestly, the way Faro had taught him to ask questions: turn it over, look at every side.

Because it was knowledge, and he had been starved of knowledge for fourteen years, and every book in this room was a miracle. Because the old man had given his life to bring him here, and it seemed a kind of insult to leave any of it unread. Because he was going to Solace, one day, to stand in rooms full of the most powerful men in Valemont, men who had lied and bribed and murdered their way to the top of the world, and it would be very useful, there, to be able to make a man still. To make a man tell the truth. To make a man want what he wanted.

Because he wanted to.

That was the bottom of it, when he had turned it all the way over. He wanted to. He could feel the wanting in him, under the cold, like a current under still water. And the water in the casket could feel it too, and moved towards it, eager, the way the sea had moved towards him on the night of the squall.

He took his hand off the lid.

It was harder than he had expected. It was like taking his hand off a warm stove on a cold night. For a moment, the room seemed colder, and darker, and the singing in the caves below seemed to change its note, very slightly, as if it were disappointed.

He got up, and fetched the pot of pitch that the master mason used for sealing the new quays, and heated it over the lamp until it ran. Then he knelt by the casket and went round the seal with it, slowly, all four sides, laying a new seam of black over the old one, until there was not a hair's breadth of gap anywhere. When it had cooled, he took his knife and scratched a fourth line under the other three, in the same old letters.

*I asked. I did not open it.*

He did not sign it. It did not seem to need a name.

Then he wrapped the casket in a piece of old sailcloth, and carried it, not to the shelves, but down. Down the narrow north stair, past the seventh door, down the long stair through the rock to the sea-cave, where the water lay still and clear and singing in the dark. He went along the ledge to the oldest alcoves at the far end, where the stone chests had stood for three hundred years, and he set it there, in the deepest alcove of all, at the back, in the dark, where it had been before.

When he came back up, the sky over the broken dome was grey with dawn, and Tam was awake, sitting up on his sacking, rubbing his eyes.

"Where've you been?" said the boy. "I woke up and you were gone. I thought something had come out of the water."

"Putting something back," said Faro.

"What?"

He thought about how to answer. Tam was looking up at him with his thin, bright, clever face, the face of a boy who wanted to know everything, every word in every book in the room, and would one day, Faro thought, if he were let.

"A book that was better left closed," he said.

Tam frowned. "There's no such thing."

"I thought that too," said Faro. "When I was your age." He sat down at the table, and pulled the lamp towards him, and opened *On the Keeping of the Balance* at the place where the boy had fallen asleep. "Come here. You stopped in the middle of a sentence. Let's finish it."

The boy came and sat beside him, still frowning. But after a while, with his finger moving along the old letters, he forgot to frown, and read.

Faro read with him, and did not think about the casket in the dark at the bottom of the island. Or only a little. Or only, for years afterwards, on certain nights, when the wind was in the dome and the sea was singing, and he caught himself listening for it, the way a man who has given up drink listens for the sound of a cork.

### Later

In the nineteenth year, on the last night before the *Faro* sailed east for the Teeth, he went down to the sea-cave alone.

He did not know why. He told himself he was going to say goodbye to it. He went along the ledge with a lantern, past the empty alcoves where the gold had been, to the oldest ones at the far end, and there it was, in the deepest alcove, at the back, wrapped in rotting sailcloth, exactly where he had left it five years before.

He knelt and laid his hand on the lid. He had not touched it since that first winter. In five years, he had been to Solace and back. He had broken the three Hands of the King. He had drawn blackwater out of a girl's glass every night for a month, and worn a hundred faces, and pulled a banker's fortune out of the world like a plug out of a drain. The Tidemark was on his face now. The cold in him was very deep.

And the water in the casket moved under his palm, eager, and he felt himself want it, more than ever.

And then, under the wanting, something else. The pitch of the old seal, the first seal, under his own. There was water in it, too: a breath of it, three hundred years old, from the hand that had laid it. He felt it now, as he had not been able to feel it when he was new. A woman's hand. Old, and tired, and very steady. And in the water of the seal, the way a voice is kept in the shape of a shell, a last thought, left there for whoever came after.

*If thou hast read this far and not opened it,* the thought said, in the old tongue, very quietly, *then thou hast learned the one thing the bound cannot learn, and the binders never do: that a power may be held, and not used. That is the whole of the art, child. All the rest is method. And so I will tell thee what the Masters would not write, because it cannot be done by any who have opened this box.*

*There is another way to break a chain.*

He knelt in the dark at the bottom of the island for a long time, with his hand on the lead, listening.

## Study it

The casket was at the very bottom of the twelfth chest, under the last layer of books, packed round with dry seaweed that crumbled to dust when he touched it.

It was a box of grey lead, a little longer than his forearm, sealed all round with black pitch as hard as glass. On the lid, where the other chests had borne only the curling wave of the Order, this one bore the wave with a bar cut through it, deep and straight. And under the wave, scratched into the lead in the oldest form of the Weir-tongue, three lines.

*Here are the Bindings, and the way of Ys.*
*Let no Warden open this alone.*
*Let no Warden open it at all, who has not first asked why he wishes to.*

He read the lines twice. Then he looked across the dark library at Tam, asleep on his heap of sacking with a book open on his chest, and thought, *I'm not alone*, and knew that it was not what the words meant, and fetched the crowbar.

He told himself he was only going to look. He told himself that knowledge was not a crime; that the Order had forbidden these books because it had been afraid, and that he was not afraid; that he had been starved of knowledge for fourteen years in the dark and would not be starved again. He told himself that he was going to Solace one day, to stand in rooms full of men who had lied and bribed and murdered their way to the top of the world, and that it would be foolish, worse than foolish, to go there with one weapon left in its sheath.

All of that was true. None of it was why.

The seal broke with a crack like ice on a pond.

---

There were three books inside, wrapped in oiled silk. Two were thin and plain and bound in grey sealskin. The third was black, and thick, and when he lifted it out, it was cold. Not cool, like the others. Cold, like the water under the Teeth.

He opened the first of the thin ones.

*On the Stilling of the Will,* it said. *Being the Art of the Bindings, as Practised in the First Centuries of the Order, and Forbidden in the Three Hundred and Twelfth Year.*

He read it in one night. It was not long. It did not need to be. It was written in a plain, careful, almost apologetic hand, by someone who had plainly hated writing it, and it explained, step by step, with diagrams, the thing he had already half guessed. All water is one water: the sea, the rain, tears and blood. And there is water in a man, a great deal of it, in his blood and his breath and the soft grey stuff behind his eyes. And a Warden who could feel the water in a man as he felt the sea could reach into it, as he reached into the sea, and still it. Not the body. The will. The current of wanting that moved through a man the way the tide moved through a harbour. Still it, and the man would be quiet, and do as he was told, and not remember afterwards that he had done it.

*It is the same art,* the writer said, near the end, in a hand that had begun to shake, *by which the sea itself might be bound, if any were ever mad enough to attempt it. We set it down here so that it will be known what it is, if it is ever seen again. And then we close this book, and pray it is never opened.*

He sat back, and looked at the lamp for a long time.

*By which the sea itself might be bound.* Three hundred years ago, a prince of the Radiant Order had stood in a temple under a black rock and bound the Deep in chains. And here, in a book the Wardens had locked away a thousand years ago, was the same art, in small. The King's art. The thing that had been done to the sea, done to a man.

He should have closed it then. He knew that. He knew it the way a man knows, with his hand on a door, that he should not open it.

He turned the page, and began the second book.

---

He practised on a rat.

He was ashamed of that, afterwards, and could not have said why, since he had killed a great many rats in the Keep. There was one that lived in the wall behind the shelves, a big grey dockside rat that had come over from Corrow in a cask of flour, and in the small hours, when Tam was asleep and the island was silent, it came out to nose along the floor for crumbs. He sat very still at the table, and breathed, in for eight, out for eight, and reached.

It was not like reaching for the sea. The sea came to him; it wanted to. The rat did not. He felt the water in it, a tiny quick hot pulse of blood and breath, and the will moving through it like a current, all hunger and fear, and when he reached into the current, it fought him. It was like trying to close his hand on a fish. He held on. He felt the cold rise in him, and the salt, and he held on, and stilled it.

The rat stopped.

It sat in the middle of the floor with its whiskers trembling and its black eyes open, quite still, the way a pool goes still on a windless day. He thought, *come here*, and it came, across the stone, and climbed into his open palm, and sat there.

He could feel what was inside it. Or rather, what was not. Where the current had been, the hunger and the fear and the quick hot wanting, there was nothing. A flat grey stillness, like water in a basin. It was not asleep. It was not dead. It was simply waiting to be told what to do.

He let go, very quickly, and it was a rat again, and it bit him and fled, and he sat at the table with blood on his palm and his heart going like a drum.

It had been easy. That was what frightened him. After the first moment, after the struggle, it had been very easy indeed. And underneath the fear, underneath the cold, something in him had liked it. Not the power, exactly. The quiet. The quiet in the rat, where its wanting had been.

He looked at the third book, the black one, lying closed on the table at his elbow.

He did not open it that night. He told himself he would never open it. He put it back in the lead casket, alone, and closed the lid.

But he did not reseal it. And he kept the other two.

---

Tam found the casket a month later, in the corner behind the shelves, and asked what it was.

"Old books," said Faro. "Dull ones. Lists of tides."

The boy looked at the struck-out wave on the lid, and at the broken seal, and at Faro, for a moment longer than he needed to. He was a clever boy. He had learned the old letters in a month. He could read the three lines on the lid as well as anyone.

"All right," said Tam, and went back to his shelving, and did not ask again. But Faro noticed, after that, that the boy stopped falling asleep in the library. He took his books down to the school on the second terrace instead, and read them there.

### Later

In the nineteenth year, in Solace, at a supper in a great house on the Street of Lanterns, a young lord who had drunk too much took exception to the Count of Cristal's ward.

He did it the way such young men do, loudly, across the table, with a smile: something about the Sardh, and what Sardhi girls were bought for, and what a foreign Count might want with one. The table went silent. Hayda put down her knife, very carefully, and looked at him with her desert-well eyes, and Edric saw her hand go to her sleeve, where Bastien's knife was.

He did not raise his voice. He did not even look at the young lord. He looked at his wine. And he reached, under the table, under the candlelight and the silver and the silence, for the water in the young man's blood, and found the current of him, all wine and spite and wanting, and stilled it.

The young lord stopped talking in the middle of a word. He sat back in his chair. His face went smooth and empty, like a pool on a windless day, and he looked at his plate, and stayed looking at it, quite quietly, for the rest of the evening. When his friends spoke to him, he did not answer. When the supper ended, he got up and went home without a word. In the morning, his mother told everyone, he remembered nothing at all.

It had been very easy. It always was, now.

In the carriage, going home, Hayda sat across from him in the dark for a long time without speaking.

"I could have dealt with him," she said at last.

"I know."

"Then why didn't you let me?" She was looking at him with an expression he had never seen on her face before. Not anger. Something more careful. "I saw his face, Edric. When you did it. I saw it go out. Like a lamp." She was silent for a while. "Is that what you'd do to me? If I said something you didn't like?"

"No," he said. "Never."

She did not answer. She looked out of the window, at the Sunspire burning gold above the dark city.

And far below, at the bottom of the world, at the end of seven chains of gold, something vast turned slowly in the dark, and the voice that called him *little one* said, very softly, with something in it that might have been amusement: *So. You have learned how he does it.*

## Burn it

*Heart + · Tide −*

The casket was at the very bottom of the twelfth chest, under the last layer of books, packed round with dry seaweed that crumbled to dust when he touched it.

It was a box of grey lead, a little longer than his forearm, sealed all round with black pitch as hard as glass. On the lid, where the other chests had borne only the curling wave of the Order, this one bore the wave with a bar cut through it. And under the wave, scratched into the lead in the oldest form of the Weir-tongue, three lines.

*Here are the Bindings, and the way of Ys.*
*Let no Warden open this alone.*
*Let no Warden open it at all, who has not first asked why he wishes to.*

He put his hand on the lid, and felt the water in the ink inside move under his palm, eager, like a sleeping animal waking at a touch. And he felt himself want it. The wanting came up in him so suddenly and so strongly that he snatched his hand back as if he had been burned.

He sat on the floor in the dark for a long time, looking at it.

He knew what the Bindings were. He had read enough, in the first eleven chests, to know: the art of reaching into the water in a man and stilling his will. The Order had used it once, in its first centuries, and then forbidden it, and then stopped speaking of it at all. And the way of Ys was the way of the Warden who had drowned a city and walked into the sea laughing, and he did not need to read a word of it to know what it would say. He had felt it already, on the beach, with the blighted thing dead at his feet. Something underneath the cold that had enjoyed it.

He thought of the eye in the dark. *If you take enough, in the end, there will be more of me in you than there is of you.*

He thought: *Nobody will ever be able to open it, if there is nothing to open.*

---

He carried it down to the courtyard below the library, in the grey hour before dawn, while Tam was still asleep. He built a fire in the old stone basin of the dry fountain there, the one carved like a curling wave, out of driftwood and broken shelving and the dry seaweed from the chests, and when it was burning high, he took the crowbar to the casket.

He did not look at the books. That was the one rule he made himself. He broke the seal, and opened the lid, and did not look. He took them out one by one in their wrappings of oiled silk, three of them, two thin and one thick, and the thick one was cold in his hands, cold as the water under the Teeth, and he did not look at that one most of all, and he put them on the fire.

They would not burn.

The silk went, in a quick blue flare. The sealskin bindings blackened and curled. But the pages under them only smoked, and hissed, and lay there in the heart of the fire, and would not catch, and he understood, with a cold drop in his stomach, why. The ink. The Order wrote in ink made with seawater, all their books, and these had been written by Wardens who knew their art, and the water in the ink was holding on.

He could feel it. The water in the pages, fighting the fire. And behind it, under it, down through the island, through the rock and the caves, he could feel the sea. The Deep. It was paying attention. It did not like what he was doing.

*Little one,* said the voice, very far off. Not angry. Not yet. *These are mine. These were made from me. What are you doing?*

He knelt by the fountain with the heat on his face, and reached, the way he would have reached to still a pool. Not into the books. Into himself. Into the cold in him, the part of the Deep he had carried since the night under the Teeth, the part that was eager and listening and wanted the books open. He took hold of it. And he pushed it out.

It was the worst pain he had ever felt.

It was like pulling a hook out of his own flesh, barbed, backwards. He felt the cold tear loose from somewhere under his heart and go out of him, down his arms, out through his hands into the stone of the fountain, into the earth, down through the island to the sea where it had come from. He heard himself cry out. He felt the Tidemark on his chest burn as if someone had laid a brand on it, and then, for the first time since the night under the Teeth, he felt it go a little dimmer. He felt the salt go out of his mouth. And as it went, the water in the pages went with it, drawn after it, down and away, like a tide going out, and the fire caught.

The books burned.

They burned slowly, with a strange pale greenish flame, and a smell of salt and seaweed and something else, something old and sour. He knelt by the fountain and watched them go, page by page, until there was nothing in the basin but grey ash, and the sun came up over the eastern sea, and the wind took the ash and carried it away over the cliff.

He was shaking. He was warmer than he had been in months. He felt thin, and light, and weak, the way a man feels after a fever breaks.

*That was not wise, little one,* said the Deep, very far away. *But it was interesting.*

Then it was gone.

---

Tam found him there an hour later, still sitting on the edge of the fountain, with the empty lead casket at his feet and the ash blowing round him.

The boy looked at the casket. He looked at the three lines on the lid, which he could read; Faro had taught him the old letters himself. He looked at the ash in the basin of the fountain, and at the scorched corners of sealskin among it, and his face changed.

"You burned them," he said.

"Yes."

"Books. From the chests. You *burned* them." His voice cracked. He was fifteen, and he had never owned a book in his life until a month ago, and he had spent every night since then carrying them up from the cave in baskets as if they were made of glass. "They were the last ones. You said. The last books in the world. Everything the King didn't burn. And you burned them."

"They were dangerous, Tam."

"How do you know?" The boy was white. "Did you read them?"

"No."

"Then how do you *know*?" Tam's fists were clenched. "That's what *he* said, isn't it? The King. That's what he said about all of them. That they were dangerous. That's why he burned the library up there and left nothing but ash in the corners. You showed me. You stood there and showed me the ash and said *this is what they do, Tam, this is what fear does.*" He was crying now, furiously, without seeming to know it. "And now you've done it too."

He did not have an answer. He sat on the edge of the fountain and looked at the boy, and could not find one.

Tam turned and walked away across the courtyard, and up the stair to the library, and did not look back.

### Later

He kept teaching. That was the thing Faro never forgot, in all the years after. Tam kept carrying the books up, and kept learning the old letters, and kept reading, every night, in the library under the broken dome. In the third year, he opened the school on the second terrace and taught the children of smugglers and fishermen to read. He stayed behind to keep the school when the rest of them sailed for Solace, and in the siege he dragged Jac back up the processional stair by her collar with the Radiants at his heels, and in all those years he never once refused an order.

But he never again fell asleep in the library while Faro was in it. He never again asked him what a word meant. And on the shelf in the school on the second terrace, where the children could see it, he kept the empty lead casket with the struck-out wave on its lid, and when they asked him what it was, he told them.

"There were books in it once," he would say. "Old ones. The Warden burned them, before anyone could read them, because he was afraid of what they said." And when the children asked if the Warden had been right, Tam would look at the casket for a long time, and say, "I don't know. Nobody will ever know. That's the trouble with burning things."

After the chains broke and the King was dust, the old men of the Ebb took their seats in the Chamber of Lords, and the first thing they did was send to Cristal for copies of every book in the library. Tam had them made, every one, by the children of the school, in fair copies, and sent them east in a ship of their own.

He sent a letter with them, to the Chamber, in his own hand. It listed every book by title, in the old tongue and the new. And at the very bottom, under the last title, there was one more line.

*And three more, which no one now living has read, and no one ever will. Ask the Count of Cristal why.*
