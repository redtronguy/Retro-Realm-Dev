# Event 16: The Rival Smuggler-Lord

*Branches from Chapter 18, "Building on Ruins": in the third year, after the line "By the third year, Cristal had four thousand people," when the market, the school and the council are new, and the trade that once went through Corrow has begun to come to Cristal instead.*

*There is no canon option for this event. In the novel, Corrow never challenges Cristal.*

---

The *Gallows Bird* came into Cristal harbour on a bright cold morning at the end of the third summer, with two more ships behind her, and dropped anchor in the middle of the fairway where every other vessel would have to sail around her. She flew no flag. She did not need to. Every smuggler in the harbour knew her black hull, and the gibbet carved on her figurehead, and the man who came ashore from her in a gilded boat rowed by twelve men.

Saul Marrock called himself the King of Corrow, and for twenty years nobody in the Reach had argued. He was sixty, broad and brown and white-bearded, missing his left ear, which a customs officer had taken with a cutlass when Marrock was nineteen; Marrock had taken the customs officer's ship, and his crew, and his head. He owned every tavern in Corrow, and every chandler, and every whore-house, and the water-cistern, and the quay. He owned eight ships and six hundred men. And for twenty years, every cargo that came through the Reach and wanted a harbour had paid him for the privilege.

He did not own Cristal. That, it was plain to anyone, was what he had come about.

Faro saw him in the harbour tower, with Jac standing at the window and Bastien, who had come up from the quay with his face like a shut door, leaning against the wall by the stair.

"So you're the Warden," said Marrock pleasantly, and sat down without being asked, and helped himself to Jac's brandy. "I thought you'd be older. Everyone talks about you as if you were a hundred. The cursed island and the man the sea obeys." He drank, and smacked his lips. "Good brandy, this. Northern. I expect it came through Corrow, once. I expect a lot of things did, once."

"I expect so," said Faro.

"Three years." Marrock set down the glass. "Three years ago Corrow had sixty ships a month in her harbour. Last month she had nineteen. My taverns are empty. My cistern sells water to gulls. And every ship that used to pay me dues is lying here in your pretty harbour, under your pretty light, paying you." He smiled. He had very good teeth for a pirate. "I don't hold it against you, Warden. It's business. You've built a better harbour. Good luck to you. But the Reach has always been mine, and a man who comes into another man's house and starts selling bread at his back door ought to pay his respects to the master." He spread his hands. "One mark in ten. Of every cargo landed at Cristal. Paid at Corrow, at every new moon. And we'll say no more about it."

"And if I say no?"

Marrock's smile did not change.

"Then I'll come at the dark of the moon," he said, "with every hull I have. And I'll burn your quays, and your warehouses, and your pretty little Weir-folk houses on their stilts. And I'll take what's in your treasury, which I hear is a great deal. And then the ships will come back to Corrow, because there'll be nowhere else." He stood up. "You've until the next new moon. Twenty-six days. Thank you for the brandy."

When he had gone, nobody said anything for a while.

"He means it," said Jac at last, from the window, watching the gilded boat pull back to the *Gallows Bird*. "He burned Skerry Holm to the waterline, eight years ago, when it wouldn't pay. Women and children in the houses. I saw the smoke from the *Slip*." She turned round. "Eight ships, Faro. Six hundred men. We've got forty on the watch and three ships that aren't built for fighting."

"We've got him," said Bastien quietly, from the wall, and nodded at Faro.

Faro said nothing. He stood at the window and watched the black ship weigh anchor and stand out past the harbour light, and felt the cold come up in him, slow and interested, like something in deep water turning its head.

## Pay

*Heart 0 · Tide 0*

The council met three times in the old Court of Tides under the tree, and argued for six hours, and could not agree, and brought it to the Warden.

He listened to all of it. Old Hanna Bettan, who said the Weir-folk had already lost one home and would not lose another, and who wanted the walls finished before anything. The master mason, who said the walls would not stop eight ships and the Warden knew it. Jac, who wanted to fight and said so, loudly, every time anyone else drew breath. Tam, very young and very dignified, who said that if they paid Marrock one mark in ten it would come out of the school, and the hospital, and the new cistern on the third terrace, and that the children of Cristal would pay Marrock's tribute in leaking roofs and dirty water. A Sardhi grandmother who said she had seen what happened to a city that fought a stronger enemy, and she did not want to see it twice.

And at the end of it, they all looked at him.

He knew what they were waiting for. He could feel it in them, through the water, like the pressure before a storm: the faith. *The Warden will handle it.* They had seen him kill the blighted things on the shore with a blade of water. They had heard Jac's story of the squall. They thought he could stand on the harbour wall at the dark of the moon and send Saul Marrock's eight ships to the bottom with a wave of his hand.

He thought he probably could.

That was the trouble. He sat under the tree in the Court of Tides and thought about the beach, the first winter, and the thing he had felt rising in him with the dead crab at his feet. Something that had enjoyed it. He thought about six hundred men in the water, at night, and how easy it would be, and how cold he would be afterwards, and how much colder each time. He thought about the book in the library he did not open any more.

*It looked out of her eyes.*

"We'll pay," he said.

Jac stood up so fast her stool went over.

"You don't pay a man like Marrock once," she said. "You know that. You pay him once and he's back next year asking for two in ten, and the year after for three, because he knows you'll pay. He'll bleed this city white."

"Then he'll bleed it slowly," said Faro, "and nobody dies this winter. The ships keep coming. The money keeps coming. We build the walls a year later and the hospital two years later, and Tam's roof leaks." He looked at Tam. "I'm sorry."

"So am I," said Tam, very stiffly, and did not look at him.

---

He took the first payment to Corrow himself, in the *Slip*, on the night of the new moon: a small iron-bound chest, very heavy, which held one mark in every ten that the harbour of Cristal had taken in the last month. Jac sailed the ship and would not speak to him the whole way.

Corrow had not changed: the tarred shacks, the taverns built into the cliffs, the smell of fish-guts and pitch and spilled rum. But it was quieter than he remembered. There were empty berths along the quay, and boarded windows in the taverns, and men sitting idle on the bollards who looked up at the *Slip* as she came in with a flat, hungry, assessing look that he recognised. He had seen it in the Keep, at feeding time.

Marrock received him in the biggest tavern, in a back room hung with stolen tapestries, with two dozen of his captains round the walls, and had the chest opened on the table in front of them all, and counted it, slowly, coin by coin, while everyone watched.

"There," he said when he had finished, and looked up at Faro with his white-bearded grin. "That wasn't so hard, was it, Warden? A sensible man. I like a sensible man." He raised his cup to the room. "To the Warden of Cristal, lads. The richest friend Corrow ever had."

They drank. Faro stood by the table, with his face very still, and let them.

And because he could not help it, because it was in him now the way breathing was in him, he reached.

He felt the room. He felt two dozen captains, and the want in them, which was simple, the want of hungry men for gold. He felt Marrock. And in Marrock, under the grin, he felt something he had not expected, and did not like. It was not greed, or not only greed. It was a kind of slow surprise, turning, settling, hardening. The look of a man who has pushed on a door he thought was locked, and felt it give.

*He'll be back next year,* said the cold, quite calmly. *Asking for two in ten.*

"Same time next month, Warden," said Saul Marrock. "Don't be late."

---

Jac broke her silence on the second night out, at the wheel, under the stars, with the *Slip* running easy before a northerly and the lights of Corrow long gone astern.

"You could have sunk him," she said. She did not look at him. "Don't tell me you couldn't. I was there in the squall. I've seen you on the shore with the blighted things. You could have stood on the harbour wall and put every one of his hulls on the bottom, and we'd never have heard the name Marrock again."

"Yes," said Faro.

"Then why?"

He stood at the rail and looked at the black water sliding past, and felt it, as he always felt it: every current, every swell, the shoals moving far below, and under all of it, very far down, the slow cold patience turning in the dark. Waiting.

"Because every time I use it," he said at last, "there's less of me afterwards. Not much. A little. Like warmth going out of a room. I don't notice it go. I only notice, weeks later, that the room's cold." He was quiet for a while. "Six hundred men, Jac. In the water, at night. I don't know how much of me would come back from that. I don't know if anything would."

Jac Teague was silent for a long time. The *Slip* ran on under the stars.

"So we pay," she said finally, "so you can stay warm."

"So nobody drowns," said Faro. "And so I can stay warm. Both."

"Huh," said Jac. She adjusted the wheel a spoke, and said nothing else for a while. Then, gruffly, not looking at him: "Well. I'd rather have you warm, I suppose. Even if it's my brandy money keeping you that way."

It was the closest she came, that year, to forgiving him.

The walls on the shore of Cristal were not finished that winter, and when a blighted thing that had once been a seal dragged itself ashore in the dark of the moon and killed a net-mender's boy, it came in through the gap where the north wall should have been. Tam taught the children in their coats all winter, under a roof that leaked onto the slates, and did not complain once, and did not need to.

### Later

Marrock came back the next year, asking for two in ten. And the year after, for three. Faro paid it. The ships kept coming to Cristal and the money kept coming, and a great deal of it went out again at every new moon in an iron-bound chest to a back room in Corrow hung with stolen tapestries, where it bought Saul Marrock four new ships, and a house on the cliff, and the absolute conviction that the Warden of Cristal would pay anything at all rather than fight.

In the fifth year, a man with a King's warrant and a purse of Radiant gold came to Corrow by night, and sat in the back room, and asked Marrock what he knew about the way into Cristal: the channels through the Reach, the reefs, the soundings. He offered a King's pardon for every man in Corrow, and a great deal more gold than one mark in three. Marrock thought about it for exactly as long as it took to finish his cup.

They raised Cristal at sunset on the tenth day, when Edric came home from Solace with Lyse's thread fading beside his heart, and the white fleet was already there, lying in a long curve across the harbour mouth. Twelve ships, with golden suns on their sails and great brass mirrors on their decks. He had wondered, afterwards, how they had come through the Reach so fast, without a single ship lost on the reefs, when the King's customs cutters had never dared follow a smuggler past Hook Point in three hundred years.

Jac told him, on the ledge in the sea-cave, with her cutlass on her hip and her mouth very grim.

"There's a pilot on the flagship," she said. "I saw him this afternoon, through the glass, before they burned the tower. Standing by the wheel, bold as you like, giving the soundings." She spat into the black water. "White beard. No left ear."

He stood on the ledge and thought about an iron-bound chest, carried across the sea every month for five years, and about a man in a tapestried room counting it coin by coin. He had paid for peace. He had bought, it turned out, the pilot who would bring the fire.

## Fight

*Heart 0 · Tide +*

He gave Marrock his answer on the eleventh day, by a fishing boat sent to Corrow with a single line of writing in it, in his own hand: *Cristal pays its dues to no one. Come if you like.*

Then he set about making sure Marrock would regret it.

They had fifteen days. They used them. Jac took the *Slip* and the three merchantmen round to the sea-cave under the citadel, where no ship from outside could see them, and stripped them for fighting, and mounted on their decks every swivel-gun that could be bought or stolen in the Reach. The mason and forty men worked by lamplight to close the gap in the north wall. Bastien, who had been a smuggler on the north coast for thirty years and had fought customs cutters, privateers and other smugglers in every kind of weather, took the watch onto the harbour wall every evening and taught them how to hold a line of shields against men coming up out of boats, and how to throw fire, and when to run. Big Olaf made himself a club out of an oak capstan bar and practised with it on the quay, singing hymns.

And Faro walked the shore of the harbour every night, alone, and felt the water, and learned it.

He learned the harbour mouth: every current, every eddy, the exact place where the tide came round the end of the hook and set hard against the old stone of the quay. He learned the shoal at the inner end where the Weir-folk's houses stood. He learned the sandbar beyond the light tower, where the bottom came up suddenly from ten fathoms to two. He learned how much he could do, and how much it would cost, and he set himself a rule, in the library, on the last night, with his hands flat on the table and the lamp turned low. *Ships, not men. Ground them. Break them. Don't drown them. Don't drown anyone.*

He did not know if he could keep it. He did not know, if it came to it, whether he would want to.

---

They came at the dark of the moon, as Marrock had promised, an hour after midnight, with the tide flooding: eight black ships under shortened sail, with no lights, sliding in out of the dark towards the harbour mouth in a long line with the *Gallows Bird* at their head.

He felt them an hour before the lookouts saw them. He was standing on the harbour wall at the end of the hook, under the light tower, with Jac beside him and the watch strung out along the wall behind them with their shields and their spears, and he felt the eight hulls come through the water towards him like eight knives drawn slowly across a sheet.

He waited until the *Gallows Bird* was a cable's length from the sandbar. Then he reached.

It was like taking hold of the whole harbour by the edge and tilting it. He felt the flood tide coming round the end of the hook, and caught it, and turned it, a few points, no more, so that instead of running clean into the harbour mouth it set hard across it, sideways, towards the bar. The *Gallows Bird* staggered. He heard men shouting on her deck in the dark. He pushed. She went onto the sandbar with a sound like a tree falling, and stopped dead, and her foremast came down over her bows in a tangle of rigging and canvas, and the ship behind her ran into her stern.

The cold came into him like a wave going over a wall.

*Yes,* said something in him. *Yes. More.*

He held it. He held the rule in his mind like a lamp. *Ships, not men.* He found the third ship and turned the water under her keel so that she broached and lay over with her sails in the sea. He found the fourth and fifth and drove them together. He felt the timbers crack, and the men in them scrambling, and the water coming into the holds, and he did not reach for the men. Not once. Not one.

But he could not stop all eight. He had known he could not. Three of them got past him in the dark and the chaos, the last three in the line, which had seen what happened to the others and come in wide, close under the cliffs, where the water was deep and the current weak; and they ran in alongside the inner quays with grappling-hooks flying, and two hundred men came over their sides onto the stone with cutlasses and fire.

The fighting on the quays lasted an hour.

Afterwards he remembered it only in pieces. Jac on the wall with her cutlass, shouting. Bastien's line of shields, holding, bending, holding. Big Olaf, enormous in the firelight, swinging the capstan bar and roaring a hymn. A warehouse burning, and the light of it on the water, and the Weir-folk's stilt-houses at the inner end of the harbour, and three of Marrock's men with torches running along the shore towards them, and Faro reaching, without thinking, before he could think, and the shingle under their feet turning to water and the sea coming up over it in a single black wave and taking all three of them off their feet and out, out, into the deep water of the harbour, where they did not come up.

He stood on the shore with his hand still raised and the salt so strong in his mouth that he gagged.

*Three,* said the cold. *Only three. And they had torches.*

He did not answer it.

At dawn, Marrock's men who could still stand were on their knees on the quays, with their hands on their heads, and the *Gallows Bird* lay broken-backed on the bar with the tide going out round her. Saul Marrock was not among the prisoners. They found him in the morning on the shingle at the inner end of the harbour, under the Weir-folk's houses, with a capstan bar's mark on his skull. Big Olaf sat beside the body and wept like a child and would not be comforted, and went up to his ugly little temple on the third terrace and stayed there for two days.

Cristal had lost eleven of the watch, and two of the mason's men, and old Pike, Jac's bosun, who had sailed with her father and saved the Warden the best fish, and who had been standing at the gap in the north wall when the first boat came in. And a family of Sardhi refugees, a man and his wife and two little boys, whose house on the second terrace had caught from the burning warehouse.

Faro went to every one of the funerals. He stood at the back of each, in the shadows, and did not let anyone see his face. And every night that week he went down to the shore where the three men had gone into the sea, and stood there in the dark, and tried to remember whether, in the moment he reached, he had meant to save the stilt-houses, or only to see them go.

### Later

They cut the names into the wall of the harbour tower, eighteen of them, with Pike's last, and every year on the night of the dark moon at the end of summer the people of Cristal came down to the quays with lamps, and the Weir-folk floated candles out on the harbour for them, and Jac Teague got very drunk and told stories about Pike until she cried.

Corrow did not recover. With Marrock dead and his ships broken on the bar, the captains fought among themselves for what was left, and burned half the town doing it, and the rest drifted away. Some came to Cristal, in time, and asked for a place, and were given one under watch. Within two years there was nothing on the rock at the edge of the Reach but tarred ruins and gulls. The Reach had one harbour now, and one law, and the Warden's word was it.

And Cristal had a watch that had fought, and held, and knew it.

Years later, on the second day of the siege, when the Radiants came ashore in their boats with sunfire in their hands and came up the processional stair, it was Bastien's old shield-line from the Corrow night that held the gate on the first terrace for an hour against them: grey men now, most of them, who had stood on the harbour wall in the dark with spears in their hands while black ships came in, and had not run then, and did not run now. Edric saw them from the citadel, a thin dark line in the gold glare, bending and holding.

He thought of Pike, saving him the best fish. He thought of three men going into the black water with their torches still burning. He did not know which of them he was paying for, now, or which of them had paid for this.

## Ruin him with Undertow

*Heart 0 · Tide +*

He did not answer Marrock. He went up to the library that night, alone, and turned the lamp down low, and reached.

He had not done it since the night he first felt the currents of money run through the world like a tide, and the great drain in the east that he knew was Dorran's. He had been frightened, then. He was frightened now. But he thought about eight black ships at the dark of the moon, and the Weir-folk's stilt-houses burning, and six hundred men in the water at night; and he thought there was another way to sink a ship than with the sea.

He found Corrow easily. He knew the shape of it now. It was a small, dark, sluggish eddy at the edge of the Reach, where the currents of the smugglers' trade had once pooled and swirled and were now draining away towards Cristal like water out of a holed bucket. And at the centre of it, holding the whole eddy together, a single hard knot of want. Marrock.

He took hold of it.

It was nothing like the sea. The sea was vast and slow and heavy. This was a web of threads, thousands of them, fine and taut, running out from the knot in every direction: debts and dues and wages, promises and threats, fear and greed and habit, all the little currents that bound six hundred men and eight ships and a town to one man's will. He could feel every one of them. He could feel which were strong and which were fraying. And he found, with a cold, slow, wondering pleasure, that he could pull.

Not hard. He did not need to pull hard. He pulled on a thread here, and a thread there, and let the currents do the rest.

---

It took twenty days.

A banker in Port Jasmine who held Marrock's paper, and had never once in twenty years thought to call it in, woke one morning with a cold certainty in his stomach that the King of Corrow was finished, and called it in. A cargo of brandy in Marrock's biggest warehouse, which had been sound for a year, turned to vinegar in a week. Two of his ships, coming home from a run to the northern coast, met a current off Hook Point that no pilot in the Reach had ever seen there, and lost three days and half their cargo. A rumour went round the taverns of Corrow, and nobody could ever say who started it, that Marrock had promised his captains a share of the Cristal tribute and meant to keep it all himself; and another, that he had already sold them, all six hundred, to the King's customs for a pardon.

And the men began to go.

They went in ones and twos at first, slipping away on other ships, at night. Then in crews. Faro felt them go. He sat in the library every night with the lamp low and the salt thick in his mouth and the cold creeping up his throat towards his jaw, and he felt the threads that held them to Marrock part, one by one, like rotten cordage, and the men drift loose on the current; and he felt some of them, a good many of them, drift west. Towards Cristal. Towards the harbour where nobody asked where you came from, and the Warden had only three laws, and there was work.

He did not pull them there. He did not need to. He only had to stop pulling them anywhere else.

On the twelfth day, the first of them came into the harbour: a lean, scarred, grey-haired captain called Dunne, who had sailed under Marrock for fifteen years, with a leaking brig and thirty men and their families crammed into the hold. He stood on the quay in front of the Warden with his hat in his hands and could not quite meet his eyes.

"I don't know what's happening in Corrow," he said. "Nobody does. It's like the luck's run out of the whole rock all at once, like water out of a cracked jar. The bank's calling his paper. The brandy's gone off. There's men saying he means to sell us." He turned the hat round in his hands. "I don't believe it. Not of Saul. But I've children, Warden, and they've got to eat, and they say there's work here."

"There's work here," said Faro. "Keep the peace and pay your dues, and nobody will ask where you came from."

Dunne looked at him then, at last, and something in his weathered face changed, and he took a step back without seeming to know he had done it.

"You know," he said slowly. "Don't you? Whatever it is. You know."

Faro said nothing. Dunne put on his hat and went to find his people a place to sleep, and did not look back, and that night in the harbour tavern, Jac said afterwards, he told everyone who would listen that the Warden had eyes like the sea in winter.

On the eighteenth night, he felt something in the knot at the centre of Corrow crack. Not break; crack, like ice. A great, slow, disbelieving fear went through it, and something under the fear that was almost grief. A man sitting alone in a back room hung with stolen tapestries, with a cup in his hand and nobody left to drink with.

And Faro felt himself smile.

He did not mean to. He felt it on his face in the dark, his mouth moving, and he put his hand up to it as if it belonged to someone else. And he saw his reflection, faint, in the black window of the library, by the light of the low lamp: a lean grey-faced man, smiling, with a line of pale blue light climbing his throat above the collar of his coat.

He let go of everything at once, and sat back in his chair, and shook.

*The moment it becomes sweet, Edric Vane.*

He did not reach again. He did not need to. It was done.

---

On the night of the new moon, a single ship came into the harbour of Cristal: the *Gallows Bird*, with half a crew. She did not drop anchor in the fairway. She came alongside the quay like any other vessel, and a man came down her gangway alone: a broad, brown, white-bearded man of sixty, missing his left ear, who looked as if he had not slept in a week.

Faro met him on the quay.

"I don't know how you did it," said Saul Marrock. His voice was hoarse. "I don't know what you are. But I know it was you." He looked round the harbour, at the ships lying three deep along the quays, and the lights in the terraces, and the market awnings, and Faro saw him see, among the crews on the quayside, faces he knew. His own men. "I've nineteen men left, Warden. And a ship I can't pay for. And a town with nobody in it." He laughed, a short ugly sound. "I came to pay my respects. That's what you wanted, isn't it?"

"I didn't want anything from you," said Faro. "Except to be left alone."

"Well." Marrock looked at him for a long time. "You've got that now." And he turned, and walked back up his gangway, and the *Gallows Bird* stood out past the harbour light into the dark, and was not seen in the Reach again.

Faro stood on the quay and watched her go. Forty of Marrock's men took places in Cristal that month, under watch, and worked well, and gave no trouble. He told himself that was the end of it.

It was not quite the end of it.

### Later

Not all of them came west.

Six hundred men had sailed under Saul Marrock. Forty came to Cristal. A few dozen more found berths on honest ships, or went home to the coast, or drank themselves to death in the ruins of Corrow. The rest, four hundred or so, hard men with no trade but the sea and no master and nowhere to go, did what hard men with nowhere to go have always done. They took the ships Marrock could no longer pay for, and went raiding.

They raided the fishing villages on the coast of Valemont, the poor ones, the ones with no sunfire beacon and nothing worth stealing but nets and boats and the last of the winter's salt fish. They raided the Sardhi coasting traders. And in the fourth winter, they raided Cristal's own boats: a Weir-folk fishing smack working the reefs off Hook Point, with old Hanna Bettan's two grandsons in it, which did not come home.

Faro found the smack himself, three days later, drifting, with nobody in it. He stood in it in the grey swell with the empty nets around his feet, and reached out over the sea, and felt them, far off, a scatter of small hungry desperate currents moving among the reefs. He knew the shape of them. He had made it.

The worst of it did not come until Solace. In the spring before he went, the King's customs published a list of every pirate harbour in the Reach, and the attacks on the coast that had been laid to each one; and the attacks of the last two winters, every burned village and drowned fisherman, were laid at the door of a harbour on an island in the west, under a man they called the Warden. Nobody in Solace had heard of Corrow. Everybody had heard of the burned villages. And when, three years after Marrock's last voyage, a fleet of white ships with golden suns on their sails came west to end the pirate nest of Cristal, there were a great many people on the coast of Valemont who stood on the cliffs and cheered them on their way.

## Offer him a place on the council

*Heart + · Tide 0*

He sent a fishing boat to Corrow on the fifth day, with a letter in his own hand. It did not mention tribute. It said that the Warden of Cristal would be honoured if the King of Corrow would dine with him, at the citadel, on any night of his choosing, under a flag of truce; and that he should bring as many men as made him comfortable.

Marrock came on the ninth night, with sixty men and the *Gallows Bird*, and sat at the long table in the library at the top of the citadel with his back to the wall and a pistol by his plate, and ate Faro's food and drank Faro's brandy and watched him with his small shrewd eyes the whole time, like a man watching a dog he is not sure about.

"So," he said, when the plates were cleared. "You didn't ask me up here to feed me, Warden. Out with it. You'll pay, and you want to haggle the rate."

"No," said Faro. "I won't pay."

The pistol did not move. But the hand beside it did.

"I'll tell you what I know," said Faro, "and you can tell me if I'm wrong." He poured himself a little brandy, and did not drink it. "Corrow is dying. Not because of me, or not only. The fishing's failing, all along the coast. The storms come at the wrong time. The King's cutters are bolder every year. The trade through the Reach is half what it was ten years ago, and it was going to Cristal or going nowhere. You've six hundred men, and you can feed four hundred, and next winter you'll be able to feed three. And you're sixty." He looked at the old pirate across the table. "You came here to frighten me. But you're more frightened than I am."

Marrock looked at him for a long, long time.

"You've a nasty way of looking at a man, Warden," he said at last. "Like you can see through his coat." He did not deny it.

Faro had seen through his coat. He had reached for Marrock the moment he came into the library, as he reached for everyone now without trying, and found under the bluster and the brandy and the greed what he had felt the first day in the harbour tower, faint, and had not been sure of: a great, cold, settled fear. Not of the Warden. Of the end. Of a dying town, and hungry men, and his own hands on the table, which had started to shake in the mornings.

"There's a council on this island," said Faro. "Twelve men and women, one for each terrace, who meet in the Court of Tides and make the laws, and bring me the ones they can't agree on. I want to make it thirteen." He set down his glass. "I want a Master of the Reach. Someone who knows every channel and every reef and every captain between here and the Ember Coast. Someone with ships, to keep the approaches, and turn back the King's cutters, and put the blighted things down when they come at us from the sea. For that, he'd have a seat on the council, and a share of the harbour dues, and a house on the second terrace, and Cristal's word that his men would be fed, all of them, every winter." He paused. "And he'd have to keep Cristal's three laws, like everyone else. Pay his dues. Keep the peace. And tell no one in Valemont the way in."

Marrock stared at him. Then he began to laugh: a huge, rolling, incredulous laugh that filled the library and went up into the broken dome.

"A *council*," he said. "You want to make me an alderman. Me. Saul Marrock. Do you know what I've done, Warden? Do you know how many men I've drowned? I burned Skerry Holm with the children in the houses."

"I know," said Faro.

"And you'd sit me down under your tree with your fishwives and your schoolmasters." Marrock wiped his eyes. "You're mad."

"Possibly," said Faro. "But you're still frightened. And I'm still not paying."

---

The council did not like it. He had not expected them to.

Jac said he was out of his mind. She said it in the Court of Tides, in front of everyone, standing on the bench under the tree with her red hair flying, and then she said it again, louder, in case anyone at the back had missed it. "He'll sell us," she said. "The first time the King's men come to Corrow with a bag of gold, he'll sell them the way in, and they'll be in our harbour with fire before you can blink."

"Probably," said Faro.

"Then *why*?"

He looked round at them all, under the great tree in the old court where the Tidewardens had once sat: Hanna Bettan with her net-scarred hands, the mason, Tam, the Sardhi grandmother, the twelve he had not chosen and could not command, who argued with him every month and were right about half the time.

"Because every one of you was somebody's enemy once," he said. "Every one of you was somebody nobody wanted. Thieves, and debtors, and runaways, and people who prayed to the wrong god. And this island said: come in, and keep the peace, and we'll see. I don't see why it shouldn't say it to him." He was quiet a moment. "And because if I fight him, men die. And if I pay him, he bleeds us. And if I break him, his men starve and go raiding and come back to burn us anyway. This is the only way I can see where nobody drowns."

"Except us," said Jac, "if you're wrong."

"Except us," Faro agreed, "if I'm wrong."

They voted. It was seven to five. Jac voted against, and said so, and then went down to the quay that evening and spent two hours drinking with Marrock's captains in the harbour tavern, and came back and told Faro, sourly, which of them could be trusted and which could not.

Marrock came to the harbour tower that night, alone, without his pistol, and stood in the doorway.

"Seven to five," he said. "I heard. That redhead of yours voted against me."

"She did."

"Good. She's got sense." Marrock came in and sat down heavily across the table, an old man suddenly, in the lamplight, with his white beard and his missing ear. "Tell me the truth, Warden. Why? Not the speech you gave your fishwives. The real reason."

Faro looked at him for a long moment.

"Because a man once sat in a dark place for a very long time," he said, "and nobody gave him a second chance. Not one. And he's been wondering ever since what he'd have done with one, if anyone had."

Marrock looked at him for a long time across the table. Then he grunted, and got up, and went out without a word. But at the door he stopped, and put his hand on the frame, and stood there for a moment with his back turned, as if he were going to say something. He did not say it.

Marrock took his seat on the council at the next new moon. He sat on the end of the bench under the tree with his arms folded, looking enormously uncomfortable, and listened to the Sardhi grandmother complain about the drains on the second terrace for forty minutes, and at the end of it he leaned over to Faro and said, in a low and wondering voice, "Is it always like this?"

"Always," said Faro.

"Gods help us," said the King of Corrow, and to everyone's astonishment, including his own, came back the next month.

### Later

It was the King's agent who came, in the end, as Jac had said: a quiet man with a warrant and a purse of Radiant gold, in the spring of the fifth year, by night, to the house on the second terrace where the Master of the Reach now lived with a Weir-folk widow and two fat dogs. He offered a King's pardon and fifty thousand marks for the soundings through the Reach. Marrock took the gold. He told Faro so the next morning, in the Court of Tides, with the purse on the table between them.

"A man should be paid twice," he said, "when he can."

Faro looked at the purse, and at the old pirate, and reached, and could not tell.

He found out at the siege.

The white fleet came through the Reach on soundings that were very nearly right, and lay off the harbour mouth in their long curve with their mirrors burning, and twelve ships of the line could not see, from their decks, what lay among the reefs to the south: nine black hulls, low in the water, with no lights. On the second night, in the dark of the moon, while the Radiants slept and their great mirrors stood cold and blind until the sun came back, Saul Marrock's ships came out of the reefs on the flood tide with fire-rafts in tow, and burned the fleet's boats at their moorings, every one, and the supply ships behind them, and were gone back among the rocks before the first sunfire beam could find them.

The Radiants could not come ashore without boats. Not that night, or the next. It bought Cristal two days. Edric was never sure, afterwards, that two days had not been the whole of it.

They found Marrock on the third morning on his own quarterdeck, with a crossbow bolt from a Radiant marine in his chest, sitting against the mast with the King's purse of gold in his lap, quite dead, and smiling.

"Paid twice," said Jac Teague, standing over him, and did not say anything else, and took off her hat.
