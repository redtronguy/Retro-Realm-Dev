# Event 15: Old Ysolde's Crown

*Branches from Chapter 17, "The House of Moren": the moment on the end of the Weir walkway when Old Ysolde puts the wedding crown into his hands and tells him, "Give it to her, or give it to the sea, or give it to the fire. It's yours to decide."*

## Give it to the sea now

*Heart + · Tide −*

"Now go away," said the old woman. "I want to listen to the water. And you're too loud."

He did not go away.

He knelt on the wet planks with the crown in his hands and felt how little it weighed. Dried lavender and a few white shells. It weighed less than the purse he had left on his father's windowsill. It weighed less than a letter. He thought of a letter, once, in a sea-chest, that had weighed nothing at all.

"She said," he said slowly, "that if I came back, you were to give it to me. And if I didn't, to the sea."

"That's what she said."

"Then it's the sea's," he said. "I didn't come back, Grandmother. Edric Vane didn't come back. He drowned under the Teeth this year, on the night of a storm, and the sea took him. What came back is something else." He looked down at the crown. "You said it yourself. Cold as the bottom of the sea. She made this for a boy who used to laugh at herons. She didn't make it for me."

Old Ysolde said nothing for a long time. The water slapped at the stilts below them. Somewhere back along the walkway a child was crying, and a mother hushing it in the soft hissing Weir-tongue.

"That's a clever answer," said the old woman at last. "It's the kind of answer a man gives when he's afraid of the other one." She turned her white eyes on him. "But it's yours to decide. I said so. So decide."

He looked at the crown, and then at the channel beyond the end of the walkway, where the open water ran out between the last of the stilt-houses towards the harbour and the sea.

He thought of what he could do with it, if he kept it. He could carry it to Solace. He could put it in a drawer in some fine house and take it out at night and look at it. He could put it into the hands of a countess with diamonds in her hair, and watch her face, and see what that did to her, and to him. He found that he could imagine all of those things very clearly, and that every one of them was a kind of hook: something to hold on to, something to pull with. And he found that he did not want her pulled. Not by him. Not by anything he carried, out of the sea and the dark, into her new life.

"Help me do it properly," he said. "The Weir way. I was never taught. I was supposed to learn it on the morning of the wedding."

Old Ysolde's face, which was nothing but wrinkles, moved in a way that might have been grief or might have been laughter.

"Turn of the tide," she said. "Not long now. You'll feel it before I do." She shifted in her shawls. "When it turns, you put your hand in the water first, under the crown. Like the vow at the boat. That's the asking. Then you let it go. And you don't push it. Mind me, boy. You don't push it out. Whatever it is in you that pushes, you leave it be. You let the water take it or not take it. That's the whole of it." She settled back. "It's the not pushing that's hard."

---

He felt the tide turn twenty minutes later: the long slow pause at the bottom of the ebb, when the water in every channel of the Weir stood still for a breath and forgot which way it was going, and then the faint first pull outward as the sea began to draw it home.

He lay down flat on the wet planks at the very end of the walkway, where the boards ran out over the open channel, and reached down with his left hand into the black water.

It was cold. Not his cold; the plain honest cold of autumn marsh water, full of mud and weed and the small lives of the channel. He felt all of it. The water came up through his fingers into him the way it always did now, eager, familiar, like a dog that has been waiting at the door. He felt the channel, and the Weir, and the harbour beyond, and the open sea, and under the sea, far out to the south-west, a great black weight turning in the dark beneath a rock called the Teeth.

*Little one,* said the Deep, very faint, very far. *What are you doing?*

He laid the crown on the water above his hand.

It floated. It sat on the black surface of the channel, light as a leaf, the faded lavender pale in the lamplight from the walkway, the small white shells catching it. It did not move. The pull of the ebb was very faint yet, and the crown turned once, slowly, on the still water, and stopped, and sat there.

And everything in him wanted to push.

He could feel exactly how to do it. A thread of current, nothing, the smallest thing, a nudge under the crown to send it out down the channel and away. He had pulled the *Gull's Promise* through the Elbow against the tide with less thought. He could make it go. He could make it go now, this minute, and stand up, and walk away, and be done.

*You don't push it.*

He held his hand still under the water, and did not push.

And it was the hardest thing he had done since the Keep.

He had not known it would be. He had thought giving a thing away was simply a matter of opening your hand. But the sea in him did not want his hand open. It had been pouring into him since the night under the Teeth, filling him, and every time he used it, more of it came, and none of it ever went back. He had never once let any of it go. He did not know how. And now, lying on the planks with his hand in the channel and the crown sitting on the water above it, he felt the stillness Faro had taught him go down, and down, past the cold, past the salt, to a place where something in him was holding on with both fists, the way a drowning man holds a spar. Holding the sea. Holding the cold. Holding the anger, because the anger was the spar, and without it he would go under.

*Let it take it or not take it.*

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight.

He let go.

Not of the crown. Of the other thing. A little. Only a little: the smallest trickle of what he held, as if he had loosened his grip on the spar by one finger. And he felt it go out of him, down his arm, through his palm, into the black water of the Weir. It hurt. It hurt like drawing a fish-hook out of the flesh against the barb, a long, tearing, intimate pain, and he heard himself make a sound, and bit it off.

And the cold went with it. A little. For the first time since the night under the Teeth, the Tidemark over his heart was not quite so cold.

The ebb took the crown.

It went slowly. It turned on the water, once, twice, and then began to drift, out along the channel between the stilts, past the dark landing stages, past the Solens' empty mooring where a boat with no oars had once been tied with garlands, out towards the harbour. He lay with his hand in the water and watched it go until he could not see it any more. And then, for a while after that, he could still feel it, the smallest thing in the water: a light dry circle of lavender riding the ebb out past the harbour light towards the open sea and the south-west.

Behind him, Old Ysolde drew in a breath like a girl.

"Oh," she said. "Oh. Listen."

He felt it before he understood. Something in the channel under his hand. Movement. A flicker, and then another, and then a great many, all at once, cold and quick and silver, brushing his fingers: fish. A shoal of them, small and bright, the sprats the Weir-folk used to net by the bucketful at the turn of the autumn tide, that had not come into the Weir channels in ten years. They came up the channel against the ebb, round his hand, under the walkway, thousands of them, so many that the black water turned silver in the lamplight and the whole Weir seemed to wake. He heard doors open. He heard someone shout. He heard a woman laugh out loud in astonishment, somewhere along the walkway, and a man calling for a net.

He took his hand out of the water. It was numb to the wrist. He lay on the planks on his back and looked up at the dark, and the cold in his chest was a little less, and he did not know whether what he felt was loss or relief or something there was no name for.

"It said yes," said Old Ysolde softly. "Gods of the deep. It's been saying no for fifty years. And it said yes."

She was crying. He had never seen her cry. Tears were running out of her white eyes into the wrinkles and she did not wipe them.

"You've given away the only thing she left you," she said. "You know that?"

"She left it to the sea too."

"She did." The old woman wiped her face at last, with a corner of a shawl. "Well. The sea's got a long memory, boy. Longer than mine. It'll remember who gave it a gift, the first in three hundred years that wasn't a body." She turned her face away from him, out towards the dark water and the shoal still glittering under the lamps. "Now go away. I mean it this time."

He went. Halfway back along the walkway he stopped and looked down, and the fish were still there, silver under the boards, turning all together like one creature, the way he had seen them in the Deep's memories, in a sea he had never known.

He did not know, then, that they would follow him west.

### Later

The Weir-folk who came to Cristal in the second year called it *the bride's shoal*, and nobody could ever tell Faro who had named it.

It came into the harbour of Cristal every autumn at the turn of the tide, out of nowhere, a great bright cloud of sprats so thick that the Bettan grandsons could fill a boat with a single cast of the net; and in the winters, when the fishing failed all along the coast of Valemont and the nets came up empty from Saltmere to the Ember Coast, there were always fish in the water under the stilts at the inner end of the hook. Not many. Never as many as the old songs told. But always some. The children of Cristal grew up thinking it was the way of the world that the sea gave you something back. Tam taught them otherwise, in the school, out of the books, and they did not believe him.

Five years later, on the walkway in the Weir at Saltmere, on a grey evening, a woman in a grey shawl sat with her feet in the water, and did not have anything in her lap.

"Gran said she gave it to you," said Mira. "The crown. Before she died, she sent me a letter. Somebody wrote it for her. *He came back, and I gave it him.*" She did not look at him. "Where is it?"

He told her.

She was quiet for a very long time. Then she said, "Good," in a voice that did not mean good, and then, after a while, "Then I've nothing to put on the water."

And at the end of everything, in the dark under the Teeth, when the whole of the sea was in him and the wave was rising on every coast and the rage was three hundred years deep, he heard her singing on the ledge above the pool, and felt her hand go into the water, and waited for the small light thing that should have touched the water after it.

Nothing came. Only the hand, and the song, very faint, very far, with nothing for the sea to hold to. He felt himself going under. He felt the wave lean over the outer reefs of the Reach and begin to break.

And then, out of the drowned dark, out of three hundred years of being drunk, something turned in the water like a single bright shoal turning all together: the memory of a channel in the Weir on an autumn night, and a man's hand open under a crown, and the first time anyone had ever given it something back.

It was enough. Only just. The wave fell back into the sea before it reached the coast. But the reefs off Cristal were never the same shape again, and for the rest of his life, when people asked him how near it had been, he did not answer.

## Sell it

*Heart −− · Tide 0*

"Now go away," said the old woman. "I want to listen to the water. And you're too loud."

He went.

He walked back along the swaying walkways of the Weir with the bundle in his hands, past the Solens' empty landing stage, past the Weir-folk sitting with their feet in the water who fell silent as he went by, and he did not stop until he was on the hard cobbles of the harbour front with the marsh behind him. Then he stood under a lamp and unwrapped it again and looked at it.

Faded lavender. A few white shells. Two missing.

*She married him in grey, up the hill, at the Sun's temple. She wouldn't have it here.*

He had thought, on the walkway, with the old woman's hand on his face, that what he felt was grief. Standing under the lamp, he found that it was not. Grief was warm, and this was not warm. It came up from the Tidemark over his heart like the cold coming up out of deep water when you dive, and it was very clear, and very calm, and it told him the truth.

She had waited two years. Two years. He had waited fourteen, in the dark, with the water coming down the walls and the rats in the straw, and she had waited two, and then Fenrick Morrow had come back to the Weir in a lieutenant's coat bought with Edric Vane's arrest, and she had married him. She had married the man who posted the letter. She had worn grey and not smiled, Cade said, as if that made it better. As if not smiling were a kind of faithfulness. And then she had given this to an old woman to keep, so that she would not have to look at it, and gone north to a dry house with a fire, and had a son, and diamonds in her hair.

The crown was a promise for a wedding that had never happened. It was a lie made out of lavender. And Old Ysolde had kept it for fourteen years and put it in his hands as if it were a gift, when it was only a thing Mira Solen had not wanted to carry.

*Sentiment is a leak in the hull,* said the cold, very calm. *You know this. You learned it under the Teeth. Everything you carry that you cannot use is weight. Put it down.*

He could have thrown it into the harbour. He thought of that. He thought of laying it on the water, the Weir way, and letting the tide take it, as she had asked; and he found that he did not want to do what she had asked. He did not want to do anything she had asked, ever again.

He wrapped it up, and put it in the pocket of his green coat, and went back to his lodgings, and slept without dreaming.

---

There was a shop on Harbour Street, three doors down from the house of Moren, that had been there when he was a boy: a dark, crowded, low-ceilinged place that sold ships' salvage and sailors' curios and anything else a man might bring in off a boat. Brass lamps from wrecks. Carved whale-teeth. Southern idols and northern knives. As a boy he had pressed his nose against its window. The name over the door was the same, *Gedge, Dealer*, but the Gedge behind the counter was the son, grown fat and bald, and he did not know the foreign banker in the green coat who came in at nine in the morning and laid a bundle on the counter and unwrapped it.

The dealer looked at it. Then he looked at it again, more carefully, and took his hands off the counter.

"That's Weir-work, sir," he said.

"Yes."

"That's a bride's crown. Lavender and shell. The old women make them." He did not touch it. "Begging your pardon, sir, but where did you come by it? The Weir-folk don't sell these. Not ever. They give them to the sea, if the wedding doesn't happen. It's bad luck to keep one. Worse luck to sell one."

"I'm not keeping it," said the foreign gentleman. "I'm selling it. What will you give me?"

The dealer looked at him: at the hard brown face and the grey eyes and the stillness. He reached for the face, the way men always did, and it slid away from him, and the dealer's own face took on a faintly uneasy look, as if he had heard a door open somewhere in the empty back of the shop.

"It's not worth much," he said. "Dried flowers and a few shells. There's a lady in Solace collects marsh-folk curios, I send her a list every year, she might take it for the cabinet. Four marks, sir. Maybe."

"Three," said the foreign gentleman.

The dealer blinked.

"Three will do," said the foreign gentleman. "Write it in your book. And write where it came from, for your lady's cabinet. The Solen family, of the Weir. Made by the grandmother, Ysolde. For a wedding that didn't happen." He watched the dealer write it, slowly, in a cramped hand, in a ledger with a cracked spine. He did not ask himself why he wanted it written down. He did not want to know.

He took three copper marks from the counter. He went out into the bright cold morning. And he walked up the hill, through the town, to the Temple of the Noon, where a girl from the Weir had married a lieutenant in grey and not smiled once; and he went in, under the great gold sun over the door, and stood in the cool dim nave where the priests were sweeping, and dropped the three marks one by one into the poor-box by the altar.

They rang on the bottom of the box, three small flat sounds.

He waited for the cold to deepen, the way it did when he used the sea. It did not. The Tidemark lay quiet over his heart. He had not used anything, after all. He had only sold a few dried flowers.

But something had gone. He felt it go, standing in the nave, like warmth going out of a room; and he would feel it go again, many times, in the years ahead. Something small, and light, and dry, that he could not name. It had been very easy to put down.

He walked out into the sun, and down the hill to the harbour, and took a boat out to the *Slip*, and did not look towards the Weir.

Behind him, on the end of the walkway, Old Ysolde sat in her shawls in the morning light with her white eyes open, and listened to the water, and did not hear what she had been waiting fourteen years to hear: a crown on the tide. She listened for three days. On the fourth, a Bettan grandson came out along the walkway, breathless, and told her what was sitting in the window of Gedge's on Harbour Street, with a card beside it in a cramped hand.

She did not say anything. She turned her face to the sea and sat very still. And that winter, before she died, she told the Bettans to go west, to the place where the water was awake; but she would not say his name.

### Later

The Baroness Quell's cabinet of provincial curiosities stood in the long gallery of the Lord Treasurer's house of white marble, under a window, and on the night of the autumn ball half of Solace walked past it with a glass in their hands and did not look.

The Countess Morrow looked.

Edric was across the gallery, in black, talking to the young secretary from the Ministry with the sharp tongue, when he felt her stop. He felt it through the water in her, the way he felt everything now: a current breaking off sharp, as if a rope had parted. He turned his head.

She was standing in front of the glass case with her hand on the edge of it. Inside, on a square of faded blue velvet, among a carved fishing-float and a Weir-folk eel-spear and a child's shell necklace, sat a circlet of lavender gone the colour of dust, threaded with white shells. Two were missing. A card beside it, in the Baroness's elegant secretary's hand: *Bridal crown, marsh-folk of Saltmere. Made by the grandmother of the Solen family for a wedding that did not take place. Acquired from a dealer, 3 marks.*

She did not move. She stood with her hand on the glass for a long time, in the middle of the ball, with the music going on behind her. Then she lifted her head, and looked across the long gallery, through the crowd, straight at the Count of Cristal.

There was only one person in the world Old Ysolde would have given it to.

She came to him that night, late, in the dark, in the study of the house with the blue door. She did not sit down.

"Three marks," said Mira. Her voice was perfectly steady. "Gran kept it fourteen years. In a chest, by her chair, through every winter. She made it with her own hands when she could still see. And you sold it to Gedge's for three marks, and had him write our name in his book." She looked at him with her dark green eyes. "I could forgive you a great many things, Edric. I've been lying awake working out how many. I don't know if I can forgive you that."

He had no answer. He found, standing there with the Tidemark cold on his cheek, that he could no longer remember what had seemed so clear under the lamp on the harbour front.

"I bought it back," said Mira. "This evening. From the Baroness's steward. Forty marks, and he thought I was mad." She drew her shawl around her. "I'll keep it. I haven't decided what for."

She went out. He stood in the dark study a long time after the door closed.

Whether she would put it on the water for him now, at the end, when it mattered, he did not know. He did not think he had the right to ask.
