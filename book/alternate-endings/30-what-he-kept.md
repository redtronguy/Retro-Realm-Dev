## Chapter 30: What He Kept

*No,* said Edric.

The wave waited.

He could feel the people beneath it. Not as a crowd, not as the grey little shapes he had felt through the bowl in Solace, but separately: a woman on the quay at Saltmere gripping a child's wrist; a fisherman in the Sardh who had gone out onto the bare seabed to look for his boat; Tam at the top of the stair on Cristal, trying to make the children go inside. Thousands of small warm lives, and then thousands more, all along the edge of the world.

The sea could take them. He had only to stop holding it.

He held it.

*They did not chain you,* he said.

*They lived above the chains.*

*So did I.*

For a moment there was nothing but the pressure of the wave, the water gathered higher than any cliff, and his own small resistance beneath it. He understood now what Aurelian had meant by a dam. He understood how a man could stand in such a place and begin, after a while, to think the whole world owed him something.

He thought of the white cup.

He thought of Pip's wooden horse.

*Then give back,* said the Deep.

It was not the voice he had expected. The rage was still there, vast and near, but beneath it something tired was speaking. Something that had agreed to his promise, in the dark.

*You said you would. The chains are gone. Give back, little one. Let us see what is left.*

He reached for the sea in himself.

He knew the way. He had done it beneath the Pale Cliffs, with the black water at his chin and his teeth rattling. He could feel the first place to loosen, the first little channel through which the power would go out. Beyond it lay pain, and perhaps death. Beyond it, if he lived, lay his own hands, his own heart, a small boat that would go only as fast as the wind allowed.

And other things.

A child coughing in a house he could not reach in time. A letter posted in the night. Men at a table, deciding what another man's life was worth. Another prince, somewhere, learning how to make a chain.

He had broken these seven. He could not break the next seven if he gave everything back.

*When you are free,* he asked, *will you keep them safe?*

The Deep considered him.

*I am the sea.*

*That is not an answer.*

*It is the only answer I have.*

He felt a storm far to the south, beginning beyond the curve of the world. A real storm, born of warm water and cold air, with no hatred in it. It would cross a shipping lane in three days. There were boats already sailing towards it.

He could turn it now.

He did not loosen the channel.

---

In the temple, Mira knelt with one hand in the water and the other on the edge of the ledge. Her song had ended. The sea-lavender crown floated beyond her fingers, turning slowly where the chains had been.

The pool was rising.

It had risen past the lowest ledge, covering the salt crust, washing around her knees. The Hundred stood higher up with their hands dark and empty. None of them was fighting now. One had set down his sword. Another was helping an old knight who had fallen when the last chain broke.

Hayda came to kneel beside her.

"Edric," she called.

The water trembled under Mira's hand.

"He hears," said Mira.

"Then come back," said Hayda, to the pool. "You've done it. Come back."

Deep in the water, he felt them both. Mira's fingers, rough from nets. Hayda's hand closing on her wrist. Two lives he knew as well as his own, reaching for him from the edge of something they could not see.

He could go to them.

He could give back, and fall into their hands, and let them carry him. He saw it so clearly that for a moment he almost did: Jac swearing, someone wrapping him in a coat, dawn beyond the sea-gate. He would be weak. He would need them. There would be things he could not mend.

He felt how much he wanted it.

And how much it frightened him.

*You promised,* said the Deep.

*I know.*

*The old one taught you to give.*

*He taught me to keep the balance.*

The words had Faro's shape. Edric heard that as he made them. He heard, too, the old man's likely answer: sharp, impatient, not at all impressed by a pupil who had found a handsome way to deceive himself.

For a moment, in all the water of the world, he was ashamed.

He could still stop.

*I will hold the wave,* he said. *I will turn the storms. I will see the next chain before it is made.*

*You cannot hold all of me and remain what you are.*

He knew. His body was already failing on the ledge. He could feel its burned chest, the exhausted heart, the lungs that had begun to fill. Somewhere far away, Jac was trying to turn him over. She was calling him a fool in a voice he had never heard from her before.

There was another way to hold the sea. The old books had named it without explaining it. A human face with nothing human behind it. A Warden who walked into the water and did not come back.

He had feared it for six years.

*Then I will not remain what I am,* he said.

He said it deliberately. Not in anger. Not because the sea had swept him past the place where he could choose. He held the stillness, and saw what lay on either side, and chose.

Then he opened the last of himself.

---

It did not feel like being drowned.

It felt like putting down a burden.

The small warm thing at the bottom of him, the thing that had laughed in a boat and wept beside a dead child, loosened. He had spent so long protecting it, hiding it beneath the cold, finding it again when he needed to remember why a life mattered. Now the sea entered it, gently, and its edges began to go.

He remembered Tomas at the window.

He remembered the smoke from Olaf's temple.

He remembered Cade saying, *I said something.*

Each memory stayed. He lost none of them. But the pain went out of them, and with the pain something else he had not known was part of it. His father's face became the face of an old sailor who had died. Pip became a small body in a room. He could describe them exactly. He could no longer feel why those particular faces had once been more than any others.

Mira's song was the last thing.

For a moment he held its beginning: the hush in the Three Anchors, a bundle being unwrapped, his bride laughing beside him. He held it with everything he had left.

Then he let that go too.

---

All over the world, the wave lay down.

It sank back into the sea without breaking. The water returned across the bare mud, lifted the fishing boats, filled the harbours, and stopped at the familiar marks on the quay walls. People ran to it, laughing and crying. In Saltmere a mother lifted her child out of the way of the first little ripple, then laughed at herself for being afraid of something so small.

The water was blue.

In the Sardh, rain began over a village whose well had been dry for seven months. Off the Ember Coast, a storm turned away from three merchant ships. In a hospital at Harnmouth, a girl with the blight-cough sat up and asked for breakfast.

And in the temple under the Teeth, the body on the ledge became water.

Jac had her hand under his head when it happened. She felt his weight go out of her palm. His collar fell empty. The burned coat settled onto the stone, and water ran out of its sleeves, clean and blue, into the pool.

She stared at the coat.

"No," she said.

The pool rose in front of her.

A shape came up out of it, slowly, gathering height and shoulders and a head. Water darkened into cloth. A face formed above a high black collar: lean, scarred, grey at the temples, with a white line through the left eyebrow. Edric Vane's face.

The Tidemark covered every part of it. Through the branching blue lines, the skin was clear enough to show movement beneath it, small currents crossing where the veins should have been.

He stepped onto the ledge.

He made no sound.

Hayda stood up. She reached for his hand, then stopped with hers halfway raised.

"Edric?"

He looked at her. His eyes were dark blue. There was no pupil in them, only a depth that shifted as she watched.

"The sea is safe," he said.

It was his voice. She knew every part of it.

"What have you done?"

He looked past her, at Cassia lying by the boat. The High Radiant's breath was shallow. Aren held her against his knees, with his coat beneath her head.

"She will live," Edric said.

Water rose from the pool in two narrow threads and wound around Cassia's wrists. She cried out once, and then her breathing eased. The black ruined flesh remained, but the burning had stopped.

"That's not what she asked," said Mira.

He turned to her.

For a moment she waited for the little change she knew: the mouth softening, the eyes becoming his, the boy beneath the years recognising her. It had happened across a crowded hall in Solace when even his magic could not hide him.

It did not happen now.

"You are frightened," he said.

"Yes."

"There is no need."

Mira took her hand out of the water.

---

The Keep began to fall.

They heard the first crack high above the cave. Dust came down, and a stone struck the pool. Cassia tried to sit up. Jac seized the longboat's painter and shouted for everyone to get aboard.

Edric raised one hand.

The falling stone stopped over the water.

Above them, in the rock, he felt the cells. The men inside them, waking to the shaking walls. The guards on the stairs. A governor fumbling with a locked drawer because there were papers in it he thought he must save.

He had forgotten the prisoners when he first reached for the chains. Now he could feel every one.

Water entered the corridors through the window slits and rose beneath the doors. It lifted the men from their pallets, carried them past the broken locks, and bore them up the stairs through the dark. The guards went with them. The governor's drawer opened, and the papers washed away.

Outside the sea-gate, the water laid them on the decks of the *Faro* and the *Slip*, one by one, coughing and terrified, blinking at the dawn.

Edric held the mountain while they went.

Then he let the longboat pass out of the cave, and the Hundred follow in boats of water that he made beneath their feet. He waited until there was no life left anywhere inside the rock.

Only then did he lower his hand.

The Teeth fell behind them. The tower, the walls, the four hundred stairs. The cell where Faro had written poems on the stone. The sea received them, and the dust settled, and where the prison had been there was open water.

On the deck of the *Faro*, a prisoner in rags went down on his knees and kissed the wet boards. Another began to laugh and could not stop. Jac stood beside the longboat with Edric's empty coat in her good hand.

"You saved them," she said, when he stepped aboard.

"Yes."

She looked from the coat to his face.

"Come below," she said. "I'll get you something dry. A drink. You can tell us what's happened."

"I do not need to be dry."

"Then come below anyway."

He looked west. The sea beneath both ships began to move, carrying them towards Cristal.

Jac took his arm.

It felt solid. Cold, but solid. She tightened her grip, as she had on Gull Reef when she pulled a half-starved stranger to his feet.

"Look at me, Shipwreck."

He looked at her.

"Tell me one thing," she said. "Are you coming home?"

"I am taking you home."

She waited for him to hear the difference.

He turned back to the sea.

---

Mira found the crown in the bottom of the longboat. The sea had carried it out with them. Three of the little white shells remained; the lavender had softened in the water and was coming apart.

She took it to the stern and held it over the wake.

"You kept your word to the sea," she said, without turning.

Edric stood a few paces behind her.

"I broke the chains."

"You said you would give it back."

"There would have been storms. More kings."

"There still will be."

"Not if I prevent them."

Mira looked down at the crown in her hands. She could not stop seeing the old woman's fingers making it, patiently, by lamplight, before anyone in the Weir knew that the young captain was coming home.

"And if we don't want you to?"

He was quiet for a moment.

"You will," he said.

She dropped the crown into the wake.

It touched the surface and began to drift away. Then the water turned beneath it, brought it gently back to the ship, and lifted it onto the rail beside her hand.

"You will regret losing that," he said.

Mira did not pick it up.
