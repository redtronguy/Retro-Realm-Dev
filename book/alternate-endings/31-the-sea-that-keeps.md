## Chapter 31: The Sea That Keeps

Severin Valtor saw the sea turn blue.

Lyse had been sitting beside his chair all night, holding his hand, watching the harbour through the library window. At dawn, the dead fish began to sink. The grey water cleared, first near the quays and then out beyond the reefs, and the gulls rose screaming from the cliffs.

She fetched the board.

The old man's eyes moved to the letters, and she ran her finger along them, smiling, with tears on her face.

*H-E. D-I-D. I-T.*

"Yes," she said. "Yes, Grandfather."

His eyes went back to the sea. He seemed tired. She set down the board and drew the rug higher over his knees.

Towards morning, his breathing grew slow. She leaned close to hear it, and was about to call for Maxim when it stopped.

Then water rose out of the cup on the table beside his chair.

It hung in the air, a clear bead, no bigger than a pea. Lyse watched it move to her grandfather's mouth and disappear between his lips.

He breathed again.

His eyes opened. They looked at the cup, and at the window, and then at her.

She fetched the board with shaking hands.

*N-O,* said Severin Valtor.

She thought he was afraid. She kissed his forehead and told him it was over, that the King was dead, that Edric was coming home.

The old man blinked again.

*N-O.*

---

The *Faro* and the *Slip* came into Cristal harbour three days later, with their decks crowded with men in prison rags and knights whose white armour had lost its gold.

The whole island came down to meet them.

People cheered from the broken quays, from the terraces, from the shore where their houses had burned. Someone rang the harbour bell, the one they had dug out of the ruined tower. Tam brought the schoolchildren to the front so that they could see. Maxim stood beside Lyse, with her grandfather in his chair between them.

The Warden came down the gangway in black.

He walked past Tam and laid his hand on a child's head. The little boy had been burned in the Long Day; one side of his face was scarred, and he wore a cloth over the eye. Edric lifted the cloth. Water gathered beneath his fingers.

When he took his hand away, the eye was whole.

The boy touched his face. His mother began to cry. People pressed forward, holding out bandaged hands, bringing children, calling his name.

Jac stayed on the deck until the crowd had followed him up the processional stair.

"He can do that now," Tam said, from the foot of the gangway. His face was bright with it. "Did you see? He can heal them. All of them."

"I saw," said Jac.

Tam looked up at her. Then past her, at Mira standing by the rail with Aren beside her, and Hayda sitting on a sea-chest, holding a cup of coffee she had not drunk.

"What's wrong?"

Jac came down the gangway slowly. Her shoulder hurt. Everything hurt, though she could not have said where most of it was.

"Get them fed," she said. "The prisoners. Some of them haven't had a proper meal in twenty years. Start with that."

---

The first winter was the mildest anyone on Cristal could remember.

The boats came home full. No blighted thing climbed the shore. Rain fell over the gardens at night and stopped before the masons began work in the morning. When a little girl slipped from a walkway, the water lifted her back onto the planks before her brother had finished shouting.

People slept with their doors open.

The council met in the Court of Tides and argued about the new harbour wall. There was not enough stone ready, the mason said. Not enough men to cut it, and half of those men were needed on the houses. The Weir-folk wanted the stilt-houses finished first. The captains wanted deeper quays.

Edric listened.

Then he stood, and went down to the harbour, and raised the wall out of the seabed.

It took an afternoon. Pale stone rose through the water, shedding weed and sand, fitted itself into courses, and stood along the shore where the old wall had been. Steps formed down to the sea. Mooring rings hardened in the stone.

The mason walked its length in silence, feeling the joints with his fingers.

"Sound," he said at last. "Better than I could have laid it."

The council stopped arguing about the wall.

At its next meeting, the captains brought their plans for the quays straight to the Warden.

---

Hayda went to his room in the library on the first night of the winter rains.

He was standing at the window. He no longer slept there, or anywhere. The bed remained in the corner, neatly made, with a blanket folded at its foot. On the table lay an open book, and beside it a cup that nobody had used in weeks.

She set down a second cup.

"Coffee," she said. "The kind you like."

"Thank you."

He did not turn from the window.

She sat by the fire. It was cold in the room, though the logs were burning well.

"I went sailing today," she said. "In the small boat. Past the harbour mouth."

"I know."

"A gull took my bread."

"I know."

She waited.

"It was the same sort of gull," she said. "The one from before. The same ugly look about it. I said something very rude. You would have laughed."

He turned then. She almost wished he had not.

He smiled.

It was exactly the right smile. The little lift at one corner, the crease beside the eye. She had watched it return to him in a boat, after four days of trying, and she knew every part of it.

"Would you like me to?" he asked.

She stood up.

"No."

The smile went away at once. It left nothing behind.

"You are unhappy," he said. "I could help."

"How?"

"The memories that hurt you. Your father's room. The market at Qadir. I can quiet them. You would still know what happened. It would no longer keep you awake."

She thought of him on the deck of the *Slip*, speaking her father's language, putting a purse and a paper in her hands.

*It's your choice from now on, always, everything.*

"And if I say no?"

"Then I will wait."

"For what?"

"Until you are ready."

Hayda picked up the cup she had brought him.

"I am not asking you to wait," she said. "I am telling you no."

She took the coffee away. At the door, she stopped and looked back at the bed, the folded blanket, the room where she had once made a tired man remember that he wanted something.

"You said I was no one's," she said.

"You are no one's."

"Then believe me."

---

The first man taken on Cristal was a cooper named Jann.

He had come from Corrow in the second year and worked in the warehouses behind the quays. He had a wife, two children, and a brother on the mainland who owed money to a merchant. That winter, the merchant sent word: if Jann would carry a little salt ashore without paying Cristal's dues, his brother's debt would be forgiven.

Jann hid the letter beneath a loose board in his shop.

He had not answered it when the Warden came.

There were witnesses. Two men from the watch stood at the door while Edric set the letter on the cooper's bench. He explained the offer, the brother's debt, the barrels Jann had considered marking as empty.

"I hadn't done it," Jann said.

"You meant to."

"I thought about it."

"Yes."

They took him to a room beneath the new harbour wall. It was dry, and warm, with a bed and a window looking out over the water. His wife was allowed to visit. The children could bring him food. Everyone agreed it was a very good room.

The council met that night.

Tam asked what Jann was charged with.

"He would have stolen from the common treasury," Edric said.

"Would have isn't did."

"I saw the intention."

"Then we need to hear him say what he intended. In front of us. We need to decide."

Edric looked around the table at the twelve of them. Fishermen, captains, a mason, women who had kept children alive through the Long Day. People he had chosen to trust, years before, because a city was more than one man's will.

He could feel the water in them. The cooper's cousin among them. A captain who had smuggled cargo through Cristal without paying twice that summer. Tam's fear, and beneath it his stubbornness.

"You do not have all the facts," he said.

"Then give them to us."

"You would still be uncertain."

"We might," said Tam. "That's why there are twelve chairs."

Edric stood.

"Jann will be released when he is no longer a danger to the city. His family will be provided for. The debt on the mainland has been paid. There is nothing further to decide."

He went out, and left them sitting beneath the tree.

For a while nobody spoke.

Then the mason said that at least the brother was safe.

---

Jac took him down to the room under the wall the following morning.

She made him look through the open door. Jann sat on the bed with his little daughter beside him, teaching her to tie a knot in a piece of cord. The child's mother stood when she saw the Warden and began to thank him for paying the debt.

Jac closed the door.

"A man gets a trial," she said.

"He would not be punished if he did not intend harm."

"That's what the trial is for."

"I do not need one to know."

She looked at him. The blue lines moved beneath his face. They had begun to shine through his clothes at night; sailors could see him from the ships in the harbour, a light in the library window.

"Neither did Valtor," she said.

He was quiet.

Jac waited. She would remember waiting, afterwards: the foolish little hope that the name would hurt him, that anger would come into his face, that somewhere inside all that water a man would strike back.

"Valtor knew I was innocent," he said at last. "Jann is not."

"He hasn't done anything."

"I have prevented him."

"And when the child grows up and asks why you took her father?"

"I will explain."

Jac's hand went to the door behind her.

"You could explain anything now," she said.

---

In Solace, the Chamber of Lords met to choose what came after the Sun King.

Edric did not go. He stood in the library on Cristal, three hundred miles away, and listened through the water in their glasses.

He heard a lord propose that the old royal estates should be divided among the surviving great houses. He heard another ask whether the histories of the Drying truly needed rewriting. A third wanted the coast's taxes raised to replace the lost beacons, though there was no blight left for the beacons to keep away.

At noon, the water in every glass stood upright.

The Warden spoke through it.

The royal grain stores were to be opened. The Sun Warrants were abolished. Every prison would publish the names of the people it held, and their charges. The Tidewarden books would be copied, and the histories corrected. No lord would own a fishing ground merely because his grandfather had been given it by the King.

People on the streets cheered when the decrees were read.

The same voice announced that the Chamber would continue to meet, and to advise, and to govern those matters which did not threaten the peace of the coast.

Nobody asked who would decide which matters those were.

In a room on the Street of Lanterns, Corvan Valtor sat beside a window while his keeper read him the news. He did not seem to hear. His hands moved on his knees, slowly, as if he were digging.

"They must stay in the dark," he said.

The keeper put down the paper and drew the curtain.

---

Severin Valtor lived through the winter.

He grew no stronger. He sat in his chair by the library window, with his board beside him, and watched the sea. Once a week the bead rose out of his cup and touched his mouth. Each time, Lyse heard his breathing clear.

At first she thanked Edric.

Then, one afternoon, the old man asked her to bring the Warden to him.

Edric knelt by the chair and took the board.

*L-E-T. M-E. G-O.*

He read the words aloud. Lyse stood behind the chair with her hands on its back.

"You are not dying of the sea's sickness," Edric said. "Your body is only tired. I can keep it well."

*L-E-T. M-E. G-O.*

"There will be children learning Tidecraft this spring. You should see them."

The old man's eyes closed.

Edric waited until they opened, then held up the board again.

*Y-O-U. A-R-E. N-O-T. L-I-S-T-E-N-I-N-G.*

"Grandfather," Lyse whispered.

Edric set down the board. He straightened the rug over the old man's knees.

"He is distressed," he said to her. "I will give him a little quiet."

Lyse moved between him and the chair.

"No."

He looked at her.

She was afraid. He felt it. He had felt the same fear in a bedroom on the Street of Lanterns, when he came through a hole in the wall with bread in his hands.

"He asked you something," she said. "Answer him."

"He does not understand what I can do."

"He understands exactly what you're doing."

The water in the cup lay still.

At last Edric turned and went out.

Lyse knelt beside her grandfather and put her head against his knee. She stayed there until Maxim came to find her.

The following week, the bead rose again.

---

Hayda left Cristal in the spring.

She went on a merchant ship bound for Mazrah, with her own money and her own papers, in plain travelling clothes. She had spent the winter writing to survivors of her father's household. There were people in Qasr who knew her name now. She wanted to see what was left, and what might be built there without a guardian choosing it for her.

Bastien carried her chest down to the quay. Jac inspected the ship's rigging and made the captain promise to put into Corrow if the weather turned.

Edric stood at the foot of the gangway.

"The passage will be safe," he said.

"I know."

"You need not leave. There is work here. A house. A place on the council."

"I am leaving."

He looked at the purse at her belt. At the knife. At the papers folded inside her coat.

"You are free," he said.

She met his eyes.

"Then let the ship sail."

For a long moment, nothing moved in the harbour.

Then the wind came. The canvas filled. Hayda went up the gangway, and Bastien went with her to set down the chest, and came back alone.

The ship cleared the harbour mouth.

Edric watched it turn east. He could feel Hayda on its deck, holding the rail. He could have brought her back without a rope breaking, without a sailor falling, without anyone ashore knowing he had done it.

He let it go.

He was pleased with himself for that.

---

Mira went back to Saltmere with Aren that summer.

They worked on the boats. She bought back her family's house in the Weir with money she had earned herself, and mended its roof, and learned to sleep with the sound of the water beneath it again.

The fish were plentiful. The nets came home heavy. Her neighbours said the sea had never been so generous.

Sometimes, at night, a little light appeared beneath the walkways, blue and branching, and moved from house to house. When it passed beneath hers, she shut the window.

He came to her at the autumn spring tide.

She was on the landing stage, with her feet in the water. Beside her, tied to the old iron ring, sat a small grey boat with no oars. She had found it beneath the house, wrapped in sailcloth. She had mended one rotten plank and left the rest as it was.

There were no garlands on it.

She heard his step and did not turn.

"I brought something," he said.

He set the crown beside her. The lavender was purple again. Every shell was white and whole, even the ones that had been missing for years.

She looked at it, then at his hands.

"That isn't how it was."

"I remember how it was made."

"Gran's fingers shook. One side was always crooked."

He looked down at the perfect circle.

"I could change it."

"I know."

He sat beside her. The boards did not creak beneath his weight. She could see the moon through the faint blue lines in his wrist.

"The harbour at Cristal is finished," he said. "The children are learning. There are no hungry people on the island."

"And Jann?"

"He is safe."

"Is he home?"

Edric looked out at the channel.

Mira reached for the boat's rope.

"I kept thinking," she said, "that if I waited long enough, you'd come back."

"I am here."

"Yes."

She untied the knot.

The tide was high. It lifted the boat and took it gently away from the landing, out between the stilt-houses, turning towards the channel. Mira watched it go.

It stopped.

Not against a rope. Not against a shoal. It stopped in open water, and the tide passed beneath it, and it did not move.

"Let it go," she said.

"It is your wedding boat."

"I know what it is."

"You waited for it for twenty years."

"I waited for you."

He looked at her. She was crying. He could feel the tears, the hurt beneath them, the whole worn shape of her grief. He knew where to reach to quiet it.

He remembered that Hayda had said no.

So he did not touch Mira's grief. He left her that.

He kept the boat.

"There will be another tide," he said.

---

Long afterwards, visitors to Saltmere would ask about the little boat in the Weir.

It sat beyond the old Solens' landing stage, in the middle of the channel, empty. No rope held it. No storm moved it. Its wood never rotted. Children threw pebbles at it until their mothers made them stop.

The fish were thick beneath it. The water was always clear.

Some said it was a blessing. Some said it was a promise.

Mira never called it anything.

She grew old in the house with the leaning roof. Aren married, and had children, and taught them to swim among the walkways. In summer they sat on the landing stage with their grandmother, dangling their feet in the water, and sometimes she told them stories about a sailor who had come home through the narrows against the tide at nineteen, and laughed because he was happy.

Out in the channel, the boat waited.

And somewhere beneath it, deeper than the roots of the stilts, deeper than the harbour, something vast and watchful held it still.

It remembered every word of the stories.

It did not understand why she wept when she told them.

*THE END*
