# Part One: The Betrayal

## Chapter 1: The Gull's Promise

The lookout on the harbour wall of Saltmere saw the sails at dawn, and by the time the sun had cleared the eastern cliffs half the town knew that the *Gull's Promise* was coming home.

She came in slowly, with her topsails reefed and her flag at half-mast, and that was enough to bring people down to the quays. A ship that flies her flag low has lost someone. The fishwives stopped their gutting. The rope-makers came out of their long sheds. Children who should have been at their chores ran along the harbour wall shouting the news to anyone who would listen, and a good many people who had no business at the water at all found reasons to be there, because a ship coming home is the oldest kind of theatre there is, and Saltmere loved a show.

Standing at the end of the long quay, with his coat buttoned wrong and his hat in his hands, was Pieter Moren, who owned her.

He was a square, grey, careful man of fifty, a man who had built a fleet of four ships out of nothing by never once being rash, and he watched the *Gull's Promise* come in the way other men watch a house burn. She was his best ship. She had been gone eight months to the spice ports of the far south. If she had lost her cargo, Moren's house would not survive the year. If she had lost her captain, he had lost a friend of twenty years. He did not know which he feared more, and he was ashamed of not knowing.

The ship came round the harbour light. Moren could see men in her rigging now, small and quick against the morning. And then, at the stern, a figure he knew and had not expected: tall, lean, bareheaded, dark hair whipping in the wind. A young man stood at the helm as if he had been born there, and he was bringing a three-masted merchantman into Saltmere harbour against the tide, through the narrows where the old pilots swore and prayed, without a pilot boat, without slowing, without, as far as Moren could see, the slightest trouble at all.

"That's the Vane boy," said someone beside him. "Tomas's son. At the wheel."

"I can see who it is," said Moren.

The *Gull's Promise* slid alongside the quay with no more than a kiss of wood on stone. Lines flew. Men leapt ashore to make them fast. And the young man at the helm handed the wheel to the bosun, came down the ladder in three steps, crossed the deck and jumped the gap to the quay before the gangway was even out.

He was nineteen years old, and he had the kind of face that people trusted before he had said a word: open, sunburned, with grey eyes that were serious now and that were plainly more used to laughing. His name was Edric Vane.

"Master Moren," he said. "I'm sorry. Captain Halloran is dead."

---

They talked in the shade of the harbour master's office, Moren sitting on a coil of rope because his knees had suddenly declined to hold him, Edric standing.

"A fever," Edric said. "Off the Ember Coast, six weeks ago. It came on fast. The surgeon did what he could. Three days, and he was gone." He turned his hat in his hands. "He asked for you, at the end. He said to tell you he was sorry to leave you short a captain, and that you still owe him a bottle of the green Halvar from the wager about the whale."

Moren laughed despite himself, and wiped his eyes, and laughed again. "Twenty years," he said. "Twenty years I've owed him that bottle. He never let me forget it." He breathed out. "And the cargo?"

"All of it. Pepper, cloves, the indigo, the silk from Port Jasmine. We lost a little of the pepper to damp off the Cape of Storms. Less than a tenth. The rest is sound."

Moren closed his eyes. His house would live. "And who brought her home, these six weeks?"

Edric hesitated. "The crew elected me, sir. The mate went down with the same fever. Somebody had to."

"Somebody," said Moren. "Somebody brought my ship round the Cape of Storms in autumn, and up the coast, and through the narrows against the tide without a pilot, and all with no more than a first mate's ticket, and that somebody is nineteen years old." He looked up at the boy. "You know they'll say it was luck."

"It was mostly the crew, sir. They're a good crew."

"It is never mostly the crew. Not when it goes right." Moren got up. His knees had remembered their duty. "There's a thing I was going to do when Halloran came home. I was going to make you his first mate, and in two years, if he gave me a good account of you, a captain. Halloran gave me a good account of you every time he wrote. I don't see why I should wait two years for something I already know." He held out his hand. "Captain Vane. If you'll have her."

For a moment Edric did not seem to understand. Then he took the hand, and his face did the thing that made people trust him: it opened entirely, like a window flung wide.

"I'll have her," he said. "Gods, yes, I'll have her. Sir. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. You'll be cursing me in a month, when you've done the accounts." Moren clapped him on the shoulder. "Go and see your father. He's been asking every morning at my door whether there was news. And see that girl of yours, if she hasn't married a fisherman out of boredom while you were gone. I'll want you at the office tomorrow to sign the papers." He paused. "Was there anything else? Anything the captain left?"

Something moved behind Edric's eyes, a small, quick hesitation that Moren would remember later and wonder about.

"Some private things," Edric said. "A message he asked me to carry for him. Personal. I gave him my word."

"Then keep it," said Moren, who had never in his life asked a man to break his word. "Go on. Go home."

---

Dorran Quell watched them shake hands from the deck of the *Gull's Promise*, and something in his stomach turned to vinegar.

He was the ship's purser: the man who kept the accounts, bought the stores, counted the cargo and the coin, and was blamed whenever either came up short. He was twenty-six, neat, pale, and soft-spoken, and he had been with Moren's house for eight years. In eight years he had never once been late with a ledger or wrong in a sum. He had expected, when Halloran died, that the captaincy would go to someone experienced. He had not quite let himself expect that it would go to him. But he had let himself expect that it would not go to a boy who had been a deckhand five years ago, who could barely add a column of figures without moving his lips, and who had been elected by the crew, the *crew*, like a tavern king at a festival, because he laughed with them and climbed the rigging with them and knew all their wives' names.

He watched Moren clap the boy on the shoulder. He watched the boy's face light up. He did not need to hear the words.

"Well," said a voice at his elbow. It was Harl, the bosun, grinning through a gap in his teeth. "Looks like we've a new captain, Master Purser. Reckon he'll want the accounts for the voyage. Reckon he'll want to know about that business with the pepper."

Dorran looked at him without expression. "What business with the pepper?"

"The tenth that went to damp," said Harl. "Funny, that. Funny how the damp only got the casks in the aft hold, the ones you checked. Funny how the young captain went down himself to look at them, the night before we made Port Jasmine, and came up looking thoughtful."

"Damp is damp," said Dorran. "Casks are casks. The captain may look at whatever he likes."

"Oh, aye," said Harl. "Oh, aye. He may." He wandered off whistling.

Dorran stood very still. It was true that a tenth of the pepper had gone to damp. It was also true that a tenth of the pepper had first gone, quietly, by night, into a merchant's warehouse on the Ember Coast, at a very fair price, and that the casks that came home marked *spoiled* had been filled with wet sacking and sand. It was the kind of small, careful skimming that every purser on every ship did, a little here and a little there, and it had never hurt anyone. Halloran had never looked twice at the stores.

The Vane boy had gone down into the hold and looked.

He had said nothing. That was the worst of it. He had come up from the aft hold with his thoughtful face and said nothing to anyone, and in six weeks of sailing he had never once mentioned it, and Dorran did not know what that meant. Was he saving it? Was he waiting to tell Moren, now, today, in the harbour master's office, with the captaincy in his hand? Or was he simply so stupid, so trusting, that he had seen the sand and believed the story?

Dorran did not know, and not knowing was a hook in his chest.

Below him, on the quay, Edric turned and looked up, saw him at the rail, and raised a hand. He was grinning like a boy.

"Dorran! We're home!"

Dorran raised a hand back, and smiled, and the smile went no further than his teeth.

---

It was an hour before Edric could get away. There were men to pay off and men to thank, and a hundred people on the quay who wanted to shake his hand and hear about the Cape of Storms, and the harbour master wanted papers signed, and the customs officer wanted to see the manifest, and Captain Halloran's body had been buried at sea six weeks ago and so there was not even a coffin to carry ashore, which somehow made everything harder.

When at last he was free, Edric went back aboard alone and down to the captain's cabin, where he had not yet been able to bring himself to sleep.

It was a small room, panelled in dark wood, with a stern window full of light. Halloran's chair. Halloran's pipe on the desk, cold. Halloran's charts, rolled and tied, with his neat small handwriting in the margins. Edric stood in the doorway and let himself feel it, for a moment, the loss of the old man who had taken a sixteen-year-old dockhand's son and taught him the stars, and the currents, and how to read a sky.

Then he knelt beside the bunk and lifted the loose board beneath it, and took out the oilskin packet.

It was the size of a book, sealed with black wax, bound with cord. There was no name on it. Halloran had given it to him on the second night of the fever, when his skin was hot as an oven and his eyes were too bright.

*Listen, boy. Listen. There's a thing I carry. Not for Moren. Not for anyone in Saltmere. You'll take it to Solace for me.*

*Solace, sir? The capital?*

*There's a man. An old man. A judge. You'll find his house on the Street of Lanterns, near the Sunspire, a house with a blue door. His name is Severin. You'll give him this, into his own hands, and no one else's. And you'll tell him: "The tide is turning." That's all. Say that.*

*What is it, sir?*

Halloran's hand, burning, had closed on his wrist. *Don't ask. Better you don't know. It's nothing that'll hurt you, if you don't open it. Promise me. Promise me, Edric, on your mother's grave.*

Edric had promised. Halloran had slept, after that, and woken twice more, and not spoken of it again, and on the third day he had died.

Now Edric turned the packet over in his hands. It was heavier than paper alone; there was something else inside, small and hard, like a coin or a seal. *Don't ask.* He thought about the Street of Lanterns in the capital, where he had never been, and about an old judge called Severin, and about the strange look on the captain's face.

He was not a fool. He had grown up in Saltmere, where every third ship carried something it shouldn't, and every quay had its smugglers' steps. He knew that there were people in Valemont who said things about the Sun King in low voices, in back rooms: that three hundred years was too long for any man to rule, that the old stories about the Drying were not the whole truth. People called them the Ebb, when they called them anything. He knew that people who said such things sometimes disappeared.

But he had also known Halloran for three years, and Halloran had been the most decent man he had ever met. If Halloran had been carrying something for the Ebb, then the Ebb could not be so very terrible. And a promise to a dying man was a promise.

He wrapped the packet in a shirt and put it at the bottom of his sea-chest, under everything else. He would go to Solace after the wedding. It would be a fine trip: he could take Mira, and show her the capital, the white city on the cliffs that neither of them had ever seen. He would deliver the packet, say the words, and that would be the end of it.

He was nineteen. He believed in endings.

---

He came up on deck into the bright noon, and stopped at the rail to look at the harbour, because he had been gone eight months and had not yet really looked.

Saltmere sat in the curve of its bay like a coin in a palm: white houses with red roofs climbing the hill behind the quays, the old harbour wall curving out into the water, the light tower at its tip. To the west, the marsh-quarter they called the Weir, a tangle of stilt-houses and walkways and reed-beds where the river came down to the sea. To the east, the cliffs, and the road climbing away north towards the rest of Valemont, and the capital, and the King.

And out at sea, if you knew where to look, a smudge on the horizon to the south-west: the Teeth, the black rocks where they had built the Drowned Keep. Children in Saltmere were raised on stories of the Keep. Nobody came back from it. When a prisoner died there, they said, the guards sewed him into a sack and threw him off the cliffs, because the sea was the only grave the Keep allowed. On stormy nights in Saltmere, mothers told their children that the wind howling in the chimneys was the voices of the drowned men, calling to be let home.

Edric looked at the smudge for a moment, idly, as one looks at a cloud. Then he looked away, down at the water by the quay.

It was very clear today, and he could see the bottom. He could see the weed on the old stones, and the crabs in the cracks, and the bright shapes of small fish.

Not so many fish, he thought. There used to be more.

He had thought it before, on the voyage. The great shoals of silverfin off the Ember Coast that Halloran used to talk about, that would turn the sea white for a mile, had been small and thin and hard to find. The old fishermen in the south had grumbled about it, about weed-blight and empty nets and storms that came in the wrong months. *The sea's sick,* one of them had told him, an old woman mending nets on a beach. *Been sick all my life, and my mother's life before. Getting worse.*

Edric had laughed, and bought her a drink, and forgotten about it. Now, looking down into the clear, too-empty water of his own harbour, he felt for a moment something he could not name: a cold that came up from the stones, as if something very large and very far away had turned over in its sleep.

Then it was gone. A gull screamed. A boy on the quay shouted his name. The sun was warm on his neck, and he was a captain, and he was home, and up the hill his father was waiting, and down in the Weir there was a girl with salt in her hair who had promised, eight months ago, to marry him the week he came back.

Edric Vane laughed out loud, for no reason at all, and vaulted the rail onto the quay, and ran.
