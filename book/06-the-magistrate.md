## Chapter 6: The Magistrate

Corvan Valtor was twenty-eight years old, and he had been deputy Crown Magistrate of Saltmere for exactly eleven months, and he intended to be out of Saltmere within two years and never come back.

He had been sent here as a test. Everyone knew it, and nobody said it. The Ministry of Justice in Solace took its brightest young men and sent them to the provinces for a few years to see if they would break: to see if they could handle the smugglers and the fishermen's feuds and the Weir-folk, and the endless dull petty cases, and the loneliness of being the King's law in a town that did not much care for the King. The ones who broke stayed in the provinces forever. The ones who did not went back to Solace, to the white courts under the Sunspire, and rose.

Corvan Valtor had no intention of breaking. He worked sixteen hours a day. He had cleared a backlog of two hundred cases in his first six months. He had hanged three smugglers and transported a dozen more, and the harbour was quieter than it had been in a decade. He wrote to the Ministry every week, crisp, clear reports that never complained of anything. He was engaged to marry Lady Celestine Aubray, the daughter of a Radiant lord with a seat in the Chamber and the ear of the High Justicar himself, and the wedding was set for the spring, in Solace, in the Temple of the Noon. After the wedding, he expected to be recalled.

He had only one weakness, and it was not one he had chosen. It was his father.

---

The letter had been brought to him at a little before midnight, by the sergeant of the night watch, who had found it in the box by the magistrate's door where citizens left their complaints and petitions. Valtor had been at his desk; he was always at his desk. He read it twice by the light of his lamp, frowning at the blotted, childish hand.

*An agent of the traitors called the Ebb.*

Anonymous letters were common. Most of them were lies: neighbours settling grudges, wives informing on husbands, business rivals hoping to see each other ruined. A careful magistrate ignored nine out of ten of them. But a careful magistrate did not ignore a letter that used the word *Ebb*. Not this year. Not with the rising in the north barely three months crushed, and its leaders still being hunted, and every magistrate in the kingdom under orders from the High Justicar himself to report anything, anything at all, that touched on it.

And there was something else. Something that made Valtor's hand, holding the letter, go very still.

*To a conspirator in Solace.*

He sat for a long moment with the lamp burning and the night pressing against the windows. Then he called for the lieutenant of the Crown Guard and signed the warrant.

---

They brought the young sailor to him a little after one in the morning, in his study, because Valtor did not like to examine prisoners in the cells. Fear made men stupid, and stupid men told you nothing useful. Better to see them in a warm room, by a fire, where they could believe they were being treated as gentlemen, and talk.

He had expected a hard case. A dockside agitator, a smuggler with rebel friends. What came through the door between two guards was a boy.

He was tall and brown and lean, with a sailor's rolling walk and a wedding guest's good coat, and he stood in front of the magistrate's desk with his hands at his sides and looked at Valtor with a pair of steady grey eyes that held no fear at all, only a sort of polite, puzzled patience, as if he were waiting for a misunderstanding to be cleared up.

"Edric Vane?"

"Yes, sir."

"Sit down, Master Vane." Valtor gestured to the chair across the desk. The boy sat. "Do you know why you're here?"

"No, sir. The lieutenant said treason. I think there must be a mistake."

"Perhaps there is. Tell me about yourself."

So the boy did. Clearly, simply, without hesitating, the way a man talks when he has nothing to hide and has never in his life imagined he might need to. His father, an old sailor. His mother, dead, Weir-folk. Five years at sea on Moren's ships. The voyage south. Captain Halloran's death. The captaincy, given only three days ago. The wedding, tonight. The girl, whose name he said as if it were a prayer.

Valtor watched him, and listened, and felt something unfamiliar, something he did not like and could not quite name. He had sat across this desk from thieves and murderers and smugglers and liars of every kind. He was very good at telling a lie from the truth; it was, he sometimes thought, his only real gift. And he knew, before the boy had finished speaking, that every word of it was true.

"Do you have any political opinions, Master Vane?"

The boy looked genuinely startled. "Political? I don't think so, sir. I'm a sailor."

"You've heard of the Ebb?"

"Everybody's heard of the Ebb, sir. People talk about it in taverns. I've never had anything to do with it." He hesitated. "I don't think I'd know how."

"And the Sun King?"

"Long may he reign," said the boy, automatically, the way children in Valemont were taught to say it, and then, after a moment, more slowly, "I've never seen him. I suppose he must be very wise, to have lived so long."

Valtor almost smiled. He pushed the anonymous letter across the desk.

"Read that."

The boy read it. Valtor watched the colour leave his face, and then come back, hot and dark.

"This is a lie," said the boy, very quietly. "Sir. This is a lie. Someone has written this to hurt me. I don't know who. I don't have enemies. I don't think I have enemies."

"Every man with a ship and a pretty bride has enemies, Master Vane, whether he knows it or not," said Valtor. "Now. The letter says you carry messages from Captain Halloran to a conspirator in Solace. Is any of that true?"

The boy was silent for a moment. Then he looked up and met Valtor's eyes.

"Some of it," he said. "Captain Halloran gave me a packet, when he was dying. He asked me to take it to Solace, to an old man, a judge, and give it into his own hands. He made me promise not to open it. I don't know what's in it. I didn't ask. He was dying, sir, and he was my friend, and he asked me." His jaw set. "I'd do it again."

Valtor nodded slowly. His heart was beating, he noticed, a little faster than usual.

"The packet is in your sea-chest?"

"Yes, sir. At the bottom. Wrapped in a shirt."

"Your sea-chest was brought here an hour ago. Will you give me leave to open it?"

The boy looked at him with surprise. It had not occurred to him, Valtor realised, that he could refuse. "Of course, sir. I've nothing to hide."

---

The packet was exactly where he had said, wrapped in a clean linen shirt at the bottom of a battered sea-chest full of a sailor's few possessions: a spare coat, a Weir-folk knife with a bone handle, a bundle of letters tied with ribbon in a girl's untidy hand, a little carved wooden gull. Valtor laid the packet on his desk, under the lamp. Oilskin, sealed with black wax. No name.

"Did you know the man in Solace? The judge?"

"No, sir. The captain only told me his name, and where to find his house. Severin. A house with a blue door, on the Street of Lanterns."

Valtor did not move. He was very good at not moving. He had trained himself in it since he was a boy: to keep his face still, his hands still, his voice even, whatever happened, whatever he felt. He kept them still now.

"Severin," he repeated.

"Yes, sir. That's all the captain said. And I was to tell him: *the tide is turning*."

Valtor picked up a penknife from the desk and slit the wax.

Inside the oilskin was a folded letter, and wrapped in the letter was a small, hard object about the size of a coin. He tipped it into his palm. It was a disc of green sea-glass, worn smooth, carved on one face with a single curling wave. He had seen one like it once before, long ago, when he was a child: in a hidden drawer in his father's desk, which he had opened when he should not have, and which his father had found him looking into, and had closed, gently, and never spoken of.

He unfolded the letter. It was not long. It was addressed, in a firm, sloping hand that he did not know, to *S.V., Street of Lanterns, Solace*, and it said:

> *Brother,*
>
> *The north is lost, and many good friends with it. But the work is not finished. We have found him. The last Warden lives. They have him in the Keep on the Teeth, and have had him these thirty years, and do not know what he is, only that the King wants him kept. If he can be brought out, the Lock can be broken. The tide is turning. Send word by the bearer, who is honest and knows nothing. Burn this.*
>
> *H.*

Valtor read it twice. Then he folded it again, very carefully, along its creases.

He thought: *My father.*

He thought: *My father is the Ebb.*

He had known it. Somewhere, under everything, he had always known it. The hidden drawer. The old man's silences when the King was toasted at table. The friends who came to the house with the blue door late at night and left before dawn. The way his father had looked at him, the day he told him he had been accepted into the Ministry of Justice, with something in his eyes that Corvan, at nineteen, had chosen to call pride, because the other word for it was grief.

He had known. And he had never asked. And he had made himself into the King's most faithful young servant, and worked sixteen hours a day, and hanged smugglers, and courted a Radiant lord's daughter, and climbed, and climbed, so that whatever his father was, it would never, ever touch him.

And now here it was, on his desk, under his lamp, in his hand. *S.V.* Severin Valtor. The letter that could hang his father. The letter that would end his own career, his marriage, his recall to Solace, his whole life, the moment anyone in the Ministry learned that the deputy magistrate of Saltmere was the son of a leader of the Ebb.

And on the other side of the desk, waiting patiently for the misunderstanding to be cleared up, sat the only other person in the world who knew that the letter existed.

---

"Master Vane," said Valtor. His voice was perfectly even. "You said you haven't read this."

"No, sir. I gave my word."

"And have you told anyone else about it? Anyone at all? The name, the address, the message?"

The boy hesitated, very slightly, and Valtor saw it. Then he said: "No, sir. Nobody."

It was the first lie he had told all night, and Valtor knew it at once, and knew, too, why he had told it. *Someone close. A father, perhaps. He's protecting someone.* It did not matter. An old sailor who had heard there was a message for Solace knew nothing. The boy was the danger. The boy had carried it. The boy knew the name and the house and the blue door.

Valtor stood up, and went to the fire, and held the letter in the flames.

The boy half-rose from his chair. "Sir—"

"Sit down, Master Vane." The paper caught, and curled, and blackened. Valtor held it until the flame reached his fingertips, and then dropped the last corner into the grate and watched it burn to nothing. The disc of sea-glass he closed in his left hand. "There," he said, and turned back with a smile. "You see? It's gone. No one will ever see it now. That letter, if anyone had read it, would have done you a great deal of harm. More harm than you can imagine. I've done you a kindness."

The boy stared at him, and then at the grate, and then at him again. Slowly, the puzzled patience in his face gave way to something warmer: gratitude, and relief, and trust.

"Thank you, sir," he said. "I didn't know. I swear I didn't know what it was."

"I believe you," said Valtor, and it was true. "But I'm afraid I can't simply let you go. Not tonight. The letter that accused you came to me, but it may have gone to others too. There will be questions. It would look very strange if I released a man accused of treason the same night he was arrested. You'll need to stay in custody, just for a little while, until I can make the proper report and clear your name. A formality."

"How long, sir?" said the boy. "It's only—the tide is at dawn. My wedding."

Valtor looked at him for a long moment.

"I'll do what I can," he said. "That's all I can promise. Go with the guards, now. They'll take you somewhere safe."

---

After the boy had gone, Valtor sat at his desk for a very long time.

He took the disc of sea-glass out of his fist and looked at it under the lamp. It had left a red mark on his palm, a curling wave, from how tightly he had been holding it. He should throw it in the harbour. He should crush it and scatter the pieces. He opened the hidden drawer in his own desk, the one he had built into it with his own hands the week he arrived in Saltmere and had never yet had cause to use, and put it inside, and locked it, and did not know why.

Then he took a sheet of heavy paper from another drawer, a sheet stamped at its head with the golden sun of Aurelian, and dipped his pen.

A Sun Warrant. Every magistrate in Valemont kept a few of them, and almost none of them ever used one. They were meant for the gravest cases only: for the King's enemies, for traitors whose very trial would be a danger to the realm. A man taken under a Sun Warrant was not charged, not tried, not sentenced. His name was not entered in any court roll. He was simply taken, under the King's own seal, to wherever the warrant said, and held there for as long as the King pleased. Which was to say, forever. Because once a man was taken under a Sun Warrant, no one ever spoke of him again, and the King had a great many things to think about, and three hundred years was a long time to remember a name.

Corvan Valtor wrote the name *Edric Vane* on the warrant in his clear, careful hand, and under *crime* he wrote *treason, the details of which are a danger to the Throne*, and under *place of holding* he wrote *the Drowned Keep, on the Teeth, for the remainder of his natural life*. And he signed it, and sealed it, and sat looking at it until the ink was dry.

He thought about the boy's steady grey eyes. *I'd do it again.*

He thought about his father's face, the day he joined the Ministry.

He thought about the Temple of the Noon in Solace, in the spring, and Lady Celestine in white, and the High Justicar's hand on his shoulder.

And he folded the warrant, and called for the lieutenant, and gave it to him, and went to bed, and slept, after a while, quite well.

---

They came for Edric an hour before dawn.

He had been sitting on the bench in the cell beneath the magistrate's house, in the dark, not sleeping, counting the hours until the tide. He was not afraid. He had been afraid for a moment, when the magistrate put the letter in the fire, and then he had understood, and the fear had turned into a great warm flood of relief. The magistrate was a good man. The magistrate had seen the truth and protected him. In a day or two, perhaps less, it would all be cleared up, and he would go home, and they would wait for the next spring tide, and marry then. A month's delay. That was all. Mira would understand.

So when the door opened and the lieutenant said, "On your feet, Master Vane," he stood up at once, smiling.

"Am I free?"

The lieutenant did not answer. He did not look at Edric at all.

They took him out through a back door into a narrow lane that ran down the hill to the harbour, with a guard on either side and the lieutenant walking ahead. The sky was grey in the east. Edric could smell the sea, and hear it, and his heart lifted. They were taking him down to the harbour. They were taking him home. Perhaps they would let him go at the quay. Perhaps there was still time. The tide was not yet at the full. If he ran, from the quay to the Weir, he could be at the Solens' landing in a quarter of an hour.

At the bottom of the lane there was a boat waiting at the water steps: a long, low, dark boat with six oarsmen and a man at the tiller, and no lamp. The guards handed Edric down into it and sat on either side of him in the stern. The lieutenant stood on the steps and did not get in.

"Lieutenant?" said Edric.

The lieutenant looked at him for the first time. It was the same look he had worn in the tavern: not unkind, not cruel. Only tired. Only sorry.

"Good luck, Master Vane," he said, and stepped back, and the oarsmen pushed off.

The boat slid out across the harbour, past the moored ships, past the *Gull's Promise* with her stern lantern still burning. Edric turned his head to look at her as they passed, and thought of the captain's cabin, and the charts, and the old man's pipe on the desk. He looked across the harbour towards the Weir, where the first lamps were being lit along the walkways for the dawn, where a small boat with no oars would be waiting at a landing stage garlanded with sea-lavender.

The boat did not turn towards the Weir.

It did not turn towards the quays.

It went out past the harbour wall, past the light tower, out through the narrows into the open sea, where the swell was long and grey and the wind came cold off the water. And the oarsmen pulled, and the man at the tiller leaned on it, and the bow came round, slowly, until it was pointing south and west, towards the grey horizon, where a smudge of black rock rose out of the sea like broken teeth, and a single red light still burned against the dawn.

Edric looked at it for a long moment, and did not understand.

Then he did.

"No," he said. "No. There's been a mistake. The magistrate said, he said it was a formality, he burned the letter, he said he'd clear my name. No. Turn back. *Turn back.*"

The guards' hands closed on his arms.

He fought. He had not known he could fight like that: like a trapped animal, like a drowning man, with his feet and his elbows and his head, with a sound coming out of him that he had never heard before. He got one arm free and threw himself towards the side of the boat, towards the grey water, towards Saltmere and the Weir and the tide, because he was a strong swimmer and it was only a mile, only a mile, he could make it. And then something hard came down on the back of his skull, and the grey sky went white, and then black.

When he woke, the boat was grinding against a stone landing under a cliff of black rock so high that it blotted out half the sky. Above it rose walls, and above the walls a tower, and on the tower a red lantern still burned, though the sun was up.

Behind him, across the miles of water, so far away that he could have covered it with his thumb, he could just make out the white houses of Saltmere climbing their hill in the morning light.

The tide had turned. It was going out.
