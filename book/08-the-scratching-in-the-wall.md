# Part Two: The Deep

## Chapter 8: The Scratching in the Wall

It took him nine days to be able to stand again, and another five before he could walk the length of the cell without holding on to the wall.

He ate everything the guard pushed through the slot. He ate it slowly, the way Halloran had taught him to feed half-drowned men pulled out of the sea, a few mouthfuls at a time. He drank all his water. He lay on the pallet when he had to and walked when he could. And the whole time, every hour of every day and most of every night, he listened.

The scratching came and went. It would start in the early morning, before the grey light came through the slit, and go on for hours. *Scrape. Scrape. Scrape.* Pause. Then a long silence, sometimes half a day, sometimes a whole day. Then it would start again. Sometimes there was the voice, muttering; more often there was not. Once he heard what sounded like a small stone falling, and a curse, and then laughter, an old man's cracked, delighted laughter, which went on for a long time and ended in a fit of coughing.

Edric lay with his ear to the wall at the head of his pallet and tried to work out what he was hearing.

It was not a rat. Rats did not mutter about degrees. It was a man. A man in the next cell, the cell of the "mad priest," doing something to the stone. Digging. It could only be digging. A man with some kind of tool, chipping patiently away at the rock, a little at a time, and carrying the chips away somewhere, and hiding what he had done from the guards.

A man trying to get out.

He thought about that for a long time. The scratching was coming from the wall between their two cells, but it did not sound as if it was coming *through* the wall. It sounded as if it was moving *inside* it. Some days it was louder, closer to the head of his pallet; some days it was fainter, as if further along, towards the corridor. As if the man were tunnelling not into Edric's cell, but alongside it, inside the thickness of the stone. Going somewhere. Going, Edric thought, remembering the old man's muttered *thirty-one degrees*, in a very particular direction.

On the fifteenth day after he started eating again, he made up his mind.

He waited until the scratching started, early in the morning. He waited until it paused. And then, with his heart hammering so hard he could feel it in his throat, he knelt on the pallet and knocked on the wall with his knuckles. Three times.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

Silence.

The silence went on for so long that he began to think he had imagined the whole thing, the scratching and the muttering and the laughter. That he had gone mad after all, as the guard had said men did, from the dark and the sea.

Then, from inside the wall, very close to his head, so close it made him jump, came three answering knocks.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

And then a voice, an old man's voice, muffled by the stone, but sharp as a knife:

"Who's there? Who are you? Speak up, damn you. I'm old, not deaf."

---

They talked through the wall for hours that first day, with their mouths pressed to the stone, which carried sound better than air, the old man said, if you knew where to put your lips. The old man knew exactly where. He seemed to know everything.

"Edric Vane," the old man repeated, when Edric had told him. "Sailor. Of Saltmere. Taken under a Sun Warrant. When?"

"I don't know exactly. More than a year. I stopped counting." He tried to work it out. "It was the spring tide in the Month of Storms. The year the rising in the north was put down."

"Ah," said the old man. "The rising in the north. I heard about that. The guards were very pleased with themselves for a week. Fools." A pause. "A year and four months, then, near enough. And how old are you?"

"Twenty."

"Twenty," said the old man, in an odd voice. "Twenty. Gods of the deep. I was in here before you were born." Another pause, longer. "And what did you do, Edric Vane, at nineteen, to earn a Sun Warrant?"

"Nothing," said Edric. "I swear it. Nothing."

He had expected the old man to laugh, as the guard had laughed. *Every man in this keep is innocent, if you ask him.* But the old man did not laugh. He was silent for a while, and then he said, quite gently:

"Tell me."

So Edric told him. All of it. The voyage, the captain's death, the captaincy, the wedding, the anonymous letter, the dead man's packet, the magistrate who burned it and called it a kindness and then sent him here. He had told it to the guard a dozen times, shouting through the slot, and the guard had never listened. The old man listened. He did not interrupt once. When Edric had finished, and fallen silent, the old man was quiet for so long that Edric thought he had gone away.

Then he said: "And you have spent a year and four months in the dark, asking yourself who did this to you, and why."

"Yes."

"And you have no answer."

"No."

"Hm," said the old man. "Hm. Well. We'll come back to that. That's a puzzle for another day. A good puzzle. I like puzzles." His voice changed, became brisk. "Now. Listen to me. I've a question for you, and it's important, so think before you answer. Your cell. How long is it, exactly, from the door to the far wall? In paces. *Your* paces."

Edric blinked in the dark. "Eight."

"And the width?"

"Five."

"And the window, the slit, it's in the far wall? Opposite the door? High up?"

"Yes."

There was a long, long silence from inside the wall. Then the old man said, in a voice of the most profound disgust: "*Thirty-one degrees.* I *said* thirty-one. Six years. Six years of my life, and I'm in the wrong damned cell."

---

He came through the wall two days later.

Edric had been told what to do. Where to kneel, at the head of the pallet, and which stone to watch: a big, square, ordinary-looking block, low down, at the join of the floor and the wall. He watched it for the whole of a morning. And then, just after the grey light came through the slit, he saw the mortar around it begin to crumble. Grains of it trickled out onto the floor. The block shifted. It moved outwards an inch, scraping, and then another inch, and then it tipped forward and fell with a soft thud onto the pallet, and in the dark square hole behind it, a face appeared.

It was the strangest face Edric had ever seen. It was very old, and very thin, all bone and wrinkle, with a great tangled beard as white as sea-foam that went down past where the chin must be, and wild white hair that went up and out in every direction like a gull's nest. It was filthy, streaked with grey stone-dust. And in the middle of it, under two great white shaggy eyebrows, were two eyes of the brightest, sharpest, most astonishing blue, the blue of deep water on a summer day, which looked at Edric for a long moment, up and down, the way a sea-captain looks at a new hand.

"Hm," said the face. "You're bigger than I expected. And thinner. Well. Help me out of this, will you? My knees aren't what they were."

Edric took hold of two thin, bony, astonishingly strong hands and pulled, and the old man came out of the hole in a cloud of dust like a badger out of a sett, all elbows and beard. He was wearing what had once been a long grey robe and was now mostly holes. He sat on the edge of Edric's pallet, coughing, and beating dust out of his beard, and then he looked around the cell, at the walls, the window, the door, with a deep and personal resentment.

"Eight paces," he said. "Five. Yes. Yes, of course. I see it now. I took my bearing from the wrong corner of my own cell. One mistake. One mistake, six years ago, on the very first day, and I've been digging in the wrong direction ever since. I should have been under the outer wall by now. I should have been at the sea." He shook his head. "Old fool. Blind old fool." He turned the blue eyes on Edric. "You've not got anything to eat, I suppose?"

Edric gave him the half bowl of porridge he had been saving. The old man ate it in six neat spoonfuls, scraped the bowl, and licked the spoon, all without taking his eyes off Edric's face.

"Who are you?" said Edric.

"Who am I." The old man set the bowl down. "That's a question. That's a question with a great many answers. To the governor, I'm number one hundred and four. To the guards, I'm the mad priest. To the King, if he remembers me at all, which he does, I promise you, because he never forgets anything, I'm a loose end. To my mother, gods rest her, I was a disappointment. To myself, these days, I'm mostly a very tired old man with very sore knees." He held out one thin, dust-grey hand. "My name is Faro. Master Faro, once, when that meant something. You may call me Faro. And I am very, very glad to meet you, Edric Vane."

Edric took the hand. It was dry and warm, and the grip was much stronger than it looked.

"Why do they call you the mad priest?"

"Because I told them the truth," said Faro. "Which is always the quickest way to be thought mad." He smiled, and his smile was surprising: it went all the way up into the blue eyes and lit them like a lamp. "And because I was a priest, of a sort, a very long time ago. Not of the Sun. Of something older." He looked down at the floor, at the stone under their feet, through which the sound of the sea came booming, slow and deep. "Can you hear it?"

"The sea?"

"The sea," said Faro softly. "Yes. Some of the men in here go mad from it, you know. They hear it all day and all night, and it gets into them, and they start to think it's talking to them." He was still looking at the floor. "I've heard it for thirty years. It never drove me mad. But then, I've always known what it is."

"What is it?"

The old man raised his eyes, and for a moment Edric saw something in them that he would spend the next twelve years coming to understand: an old, old grief, and under the grief, something that was not grief at all, but hope, stubborn and patient and very nearly as old as the stones.

"Another day," said Faro. "That's a story for another day. We have a great many days, you and I. Let's not spend them all at once." He slapped his knees and stood up. "Now. Come and see my tunnel. I'm afraid you'll think very little of an old man's engineering, but I'm rather proud of it, all the same."

---

The tunnel was a narrow, low, crooked shaft that ran through the thickness of the stone, just wide enough for a thin man to crawl along on his belly. It went from the back of the old man's cell, behind his pallet, and wandered, as far as Edric could tell in the pitch dark, for about forty feet, bending twice, and ended, now, in Edric's wall.

Faro had dug it with two tools. One was a chisel, which he had made, over a period of eight months, from an iron strap he had worked loose from the frame of his bed, grinding it to an edge against the stone floor. The other was a spoon, a pewter spoon, worn down by six years of digging to a stub barely as long as his finger. He showed them both to Edric with immense pride.

"And where do you put the stone? The chips. The guards would see."

"Ah," said Faro. "That's the clever part." He took Edric back to his cell, which was the same as Edric's except that it was very slightly larger, and very much messier, and had a fireplace: a real fireplace, in one corner, with a chimney so narrow you could not have put your hand up it. "When they first brought me here, they thought I was an important prisoner. They gave me a fire. Very civil. That was thirty years ago, and they've forgotten all about me since, and they stopped bringing wood twenty-five years ago, but the chimney's still here." He knelt and reached up into it, and Edric heard something shift. "There's a gap behind the flue. Goes down into the foundations, a long way. I've been feeding my rubble into it a handful at a time for six years. I've no idea where it ends. Somewhere under the sea, I expect."

Edric looked at the old man: at the wild white hair and the beard and the holes in his robe, at the stub of a spoon and the chisel made from a bed-strap, at the narrow black mouth of the tunnel behind the pallet. Six years. Six years of scraping at the rock in the dark, a handful at a time, with nothing but a spoon, towards a wall that he had missed by a single bearing on the very first day.

"Weren't you ever afraid you'd never get out?" he said.

Faro sat down on his own pallet. He looked at Edric for a long moment.

"I've been in here thirty years," he said. "In thirty years a man learns that there are only two ways to live in a place like this. You can hope, or you can stop. Most stop. I watched you stop, Edric Vane. I heard you, through the wall, those last weeks. I heard you push the bowl back through the slot." His blue eyes were very steady. "I'm old. I've not much time left, and I don't intend to spend it watching a boy of twenty starve himself to death on the other side of a wall. So I dug a little faster." He smiled. "And look. I missed the sea. But I found you."

Edric found that he could not speak. He sat down on the floor of the old man's cell, with his back against the wall, and put his face in his hands, and for the first time since the night of the Feast of Lamps, he wept, and could not stop, and did not try.

Faro let him. He sat on his pallet and waited, the way the sea waits, and said nothing at all. When at last it was over, he got up, slowly, on his sore old knees, and came and sat down on the floor beside him, and put a thin, dusty arm across his shoulders.

"There," he said. "There. That's better. That's been waiting a long time to come out." He patted Edric's shoulder. "Now. You'll want to wash your face. And then, I think, you and I should start making some plans."

"Plans?"

"For getting out," said Master Faro. "What else?"
