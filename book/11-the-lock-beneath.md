## Chapter 11: The Lock Beneath

It took him three more years to raise a drop of water without hating anything.

Three years of sitting cross-legged on the wet stone floor of Faro's cell with his eyes closed and his hands open, breathing. In for a count of eight. Hold for eight. Out for eight. Hold for eight. Over and over, hour after hour, until the counting stopped being counting and became something more like the tide: a rhythm that breathed him, instead of the other way round.

"Stillness," Faro said, a thousand times. "Not emptiness. Stillness. An empty mind is a dead mind. A still mind is like a pool in a cave: nothing moving on the surface, and everything moving underneath. Find the stillness. Then listen. Then reach. But gently. As if you were putting your hand into a pool to touch a fish without frightening it."

For the first year, he found nothing. He would breathe himself into stillness, and listen, and reach, and there would be only the stone and the wet and the dark. And then the anger would come, as it always came, swimming up from the bottom of him like a shark: *Valtor. Dorran. Fenrick.* And he would feel the water leap towards him like a dog towards its master. And he would open his eyes, and stop, and begin again.

In the second year, he began to hear it.

Not the sea. Not the booming under the floor; he had always heard that. Something beneath the booming, the way there is sometimes a second voice beneath the first in a choir. Something very faint and very far and very, very large. It had no words. It was not a sound at all, really: more a feeling, a pressure, like the change in the air before a storm. And what it felt like, when he finally let himself feel it, was pain.

He opened his eyes the first time he felt it, gasping, and found that his face was wet with tears that were not his own.

"Yes," said Faro, watching him. "Yes. That's it. That's the Deep. That's what it's like." He looked away. "You'll get used to it. I never have. But you'll get used to it."

In the third year, on a day like any other, Edric breathed, and found the stillness, and listened through the pain, and reached, gently, without anger, without hate, with nothing at all but a kind of grave and patient attention, like putting a hand into a pool. And on the stone beneath his palm, a film of damp crept together into a bead, and the bead trembled, and rose, and hung in the air, turning, catching the light.

He held it for a count of eight. Then he let it fall.

He opened his eyes. Faro was sitting on his pallet, very still, with tears running down into his beard.

"My mother did that," the old man said, "the first time, when she was six."

"I'm twenty-seven," said Edric.

"Yes," said Faro. "And she had a sea to listen to that wasn't screaming." He wiped his eyes on his sleeve. "Well done, Edric. Well done. That's the hardest drop of water you'll ever raise. All the rest are easier."

---

They broke through in the eighth year.

The tunnel had gone down and down, under the foundations of the tower, through layer after layer of grey rock, following a seam Faro had chosen that seemed softer than the rest. The sound of the sea had grown so loud that they could no longer talk while they dug; they worked in silence, in turns, passing the chisel back and forth in the dark. Edric had stopped thinking of the tunnel as a way out. It had become something more like a ritual: something he did, every day, with his hands, while his mind went elsewhere.

And then, one morning, he struck the chisel into the rock at the end of the tunnel, and the rock gave way.

It did not crumble, as it usually did. It simply fell away in front of him, a piece the size of a cartwheel, and dropped into darkness. He heard it fall for a long, long time. Then a splash, deep and hollow and echoing.

And through the hole came air: warm air, warmer than anything he had felt in eight years, damp and heavy and smelling of salt, and something else, something sharp and strange, like the air after lightning. And with it came light.

It was very faint. A dim gold glow, coming up from far below, so faint he would not have seen it if his eyes had not spent eight years in the dark. But it was light. Gold light. Moving, slowly, the way light moves on water.

He lay at the end of the tunnel with his face at the hole and stared down into it for a long time. Then he wriggled back, all forty yards of it, as fast as he had ever moved, and came out into Faro's cell in a shower of grit.

"Faro," he said. "Faro, you need to come and see this."

---

It took them three days to make a rope long enough. They unpicked their blankets thread by thread and plaited the threads into cords and the cords into a rope, as Edric had learned to do on his first ship. It took them another day to get Faro down the tunnel; the old man was seventy-three now, by his own reckoning, and his knees were very bad, and he had to stop every few yards to rest. But he would not hear of staying behind.

"I've waited thirty-eight years for this," he said. "If you think I'm going to let you go and look at it without me, you've learned nothing at all."

They tied the rope to a spur of rock at the end of the tunnel, and Edric went down first.

The hole opened into the roof of a chamber. A cave. He swung on the rope in a vast warm darkness, turning slowly, and below him, a long way below, the gold light moved. He went down hand over hand, and down, and down, until his feet touched stone: a ledge, a broad flat shelf of rock, wet and slippery, running along the wall of the cave. He stood on it and looked around, and forgot to breathe.

It was enormous. A cave as large as the whole of the Three Anchors, as large as the square in front of the harbour master's office in Saltmere, as large as a temple. Its roof was lost in darkness above him. Its walls curved away on either side, ribbed and fluted like the inside of a shell. And its floor was water: black, still water, filling the cave from wall to wall, except for the ledge he was standing on and others like it, stepping down towards the surface like the tiers of a theatre.

On the walls, there were carvings.

He saw them as his eyes adjusted. Figures, cut into the rock in long rows, running around the cave on every tier. Men and women in long robes, with their hands raised. Waves. Fish. Ships. Great curling shapes that might have been the sea itself, drawn by someone who loved it. They were very old, worn soft by centuries of damp, and some of them had been hacked at, deliberately, long ago, so that whole rows of faces were missing. But many remained.

And in the middle of the cave, rising out of the black water, were the chains.

There were seven of them. They came down out of the darkness of the roof, from somewhere far above, from the foundations of the Keep, each one as thick as Edric's body. They were not iron. They were made of light: gold light, solid and heavy and burning, each link the size of a cart wheel, glowing with a fierce, steady, shimmering radiance like the noon sun seen through closed eyelids. They came down through the dark air in seven long, straight, taut lines, and plunged into the black water in the middle of the cave, and went down, and down, and down. Edric could see them going down through the water, glowing, growing fainter and fainter, a long way, an impossible way, until they vanished into a depth that had no bottom.

The air around them shimmered with heat. The water where they entered it steamed, very faintly. And they were taut. Unbearably, impossibly taut, the way a cable is taut when it is holding up a ship in a storm. And every so often, every few heartbeats, they moved: a long, slow, terrible shudder that ran up all seven at once, from the water to the roof, and made the whole cave tremble, and sent ripples across the black water to lap against the ledges.

*Boom.*

He felt it through his feet, through his chest. The sound he had heard every day and every night for eight years. He understood now. It was not the sea beating against the rock. It was something below the chains, far below, in the depth where they disappeared, pulling against them. Heaving. Over and over, every few heartbeats, for three hundred years.

Faro came down the rope behind him, slowly, with many stops. When his feet touched the ledge, his knees gave way, and he sat down on the wet stone with a soft thud. Edric turned to help him up. But the old man was not looking at him. He was looking at the chains.

His face, in the gold light, was something Edric would never forget.

"The Lock," said Master Faro. "Oh. Oh, gods. Oh, Mother. There you are."

---

They stayed there for a long time.

Faro sat on the ledge and wept, quite openly, the way a man weeps at a grave. Edric sat beside him and did not know what to say. After a while, he got up and walked along the ledge, looking at the carvings.

He began to understand them. They told a story, going round the walls of the cave in rows, from the top tier down. The Tidewardens, in their robes, standing on the shore with their hands raised to the sea. The sea, a great curling shape, lifting up to meet them. Ships, sailing safely. Fish, filling nets. Storms, turned away from a town. A procession of people coming down into this very cave, carrying lamps, and kneeling at the edge of the water. This had been their temple, he realised. Their holiest place. The place where the Deep came closest to the surface. The place where they came to listen to it.

And then, in the lowest row, the carvings changed. The faces in the lowest row had been hacked away, all of them, deliberately, by someone who had come down here with a hammer. But the shapes were still clear. Men with suns on their chests. Fire. People falling. And a figure in a crown, standing at the edge of the water with its arms outstretched, and seven lines coming down from the roof.

Somebody had carved that, Edric thought. Somebody had come down here, after the King had built his chains, and carved what had happened onto the wall, in secret, so that it would be remembered. And somebody else, later, had come down and smashed the faces off.

He looked down from the lowest ledge to the black water. It was very close here: an arm's length below. He could feel the heat coming off the chains, thirty yards away. He could smell the strange sharp lightning smell, stronger now. And he could feel something else, too, so strong here that it was almost unbearable: the pressure he had felt in Faro's cell, the faint vast pain beneath the booming. Here it was not faint. Here it was everywhere. It filled the cave like water fills a bowl. It pressed on his skull, on his chest, on his eyes. It was grief and fury and hunger and a terrible, terrible patience, three hundred years of it, and it was coming from below, from the depth where the chains went down, and it was aware. It was aware of *him*.

He knelt down, without meaning to, at the very edge of the lowest ledge, and reached out his hand towards the water.

"*Don't.*"

Faro's voice cracked off the walls of the cave like a whip. Edric snatched his hand back.

The old man had got to his feet somehow, and was coming down the ledges towards him, step by step, leaning on the wall. He was white to the lips.

"Not here," he said. "Not here. Never here. You're not ready. Do you hear me? You touch that water, here, this close, with what you are, and it'll come up your arm and into you and there'll be nothing left of Edric Vane but a shell for it to walk around in." He reached the lowest ledge and caught Edric's arm in his thin, strong fingers. "It's not kind, Edric. I told you. It's not cruel either. It's *hungry*. Three hundred years hungry. It'll take anything it can reach."

Edric stared at him. Then at the water. His hand was tingling where he had reached towards it, as if he had held it too close to a fire.

"Can it be broken?" he said. "The Lock. Can it be opened?"

Faro let go of his arm. He sat down again, heavily, on the edge of the ledge, and looked at the seven chains for a long time.

"Not by us," he said at last. "Not by any Tidewarden that ever lived. Not with Tidecraft as it is now, a trickle, a drop of water and a sore head. To break chains like that, you'd need power on the scale of the King's. Sunfire on the scale of the King's. Or—" He stopped.

"Or what?"

"Or the Deep's own strength," said Faro slowly. "Turned against them. From the inside. If a Tidewarden could somehow take the god's own power into himself, all of it, everything that's been trapped down there for three hundred years, and use it, here, at the Lock." He shook his head. "But no living man could hold that. It would burn you to nothing. Or drown you. Or worse." He was quiet a moment. "The old books talk about it. A thing that was done, once or twice, long ago, in the oldest days, when the Deep chose someone. They called it *being taken by the tide*. They talk about it the way you'd talk about being struck by lightning. A thing that happens to you. A thing you don't survive."

They sat for a while longer, watching the chains shudder. *Boom.* The ripples ran across the black water and lapped at the ledge by their feet.

"Faro," said Edric. "What's that?"

He was pointing across the cave. On the far side, opposite the ledge they were sitting on, the wall of the cave curved down to meet the water. And there, at the waterline, was an opening: a great arch of rock, half-submerged, like the mouth of a tunnel. And beyond it, very faint, he thought he could see a different kind of light. Not gold. Grey-blue. Daylight, filtered through water.

"The sea-gate," said Faro. "The way into the temple, in the old days. It opens onto the sea, under the cliffs. The Tidewardens came in by boat, at low tide." He sighed. "Look closer."

Edric looked closer. Across the mouth of the arch, from the roof of it down into the water, ran bars. Thin bars of gold light, a dozen of them, a hand's width apart: a gate, a portcullis, made of the same burning light as the chains. Even from here, he could see the water steaming around them.

"Sunfire," said Faro. "Nothing passes it. Not a fish, not a man. Touch it and it'll cook you like a crab." He laughed, wearily. "Thirty-eight years. Thirty-eight years I've been digging for the sea. And here it is, fifty yards away, on the other side of a gate I can't open. Aurelian has a sense of humour. I'll give him that."

---

On the way back up, it happened.

They were on the rope. Edric had climbed up first, to the tunnel mouth, and was hauling Faro up after him, hand over hand, the old man helping as he could with his feet against the wall. They were halfway. And then the rope went suddenly heavy, a dead weight, and Faro made a sound: a small, strange, strangled sound, like a word that had got stuck.

Edric looked down. The old man was hanging from the rope by one hand. The other arm hung limp at his side. His face, in the gold light from below, had gone strange. One side of it had slipped, like wax near a candle: the eye half-closed, the mouth dragged down.

"*Faro!*"

He never remembered how he got the old man up. He remembered the rope burning through his palms. He remembered a strength he had never had before, a strength that seemed to come up out of the rock and into his arms. He remembered the water in the cave far below suddenly lifting, a great slow swell, as if the whole black surface had taken a breath, and the chains shuddering, *boom*, harder than he had ever felt them. He remembered dragging Faro over the lip of the hole into the tunnel, and lying there in the dark with the old man in his arms, both of them gasping.

And he remembered Faro's voice, slurred, thick, coming out of only half his mouth.

"It's—all right. All right. I know what this is. My mother had it. And her father." A long, wheezing breath. "The first one's a warning. You get three. The third one kills you."

"Don't talk."

"Must talk. While I can." The good hand found Edric's wrist in the dark and gripped it, hard. "Listen. There's a thing I haven't told you. I was going to wait. I was going to wait until we were out. But I might not get out now. So listen."

"Faro—"

"*Listen.*" The grip tightened. "The Isle of Cristal. In the Shattered Reach. West of here, far west, past the last lights, where the smugglers go. A small island, high cliffs, a harbour on the south side, shaped like a hook. There's nothing there now. Ruins. Everyone thinks it's cursed. Good. Let them." A ragged breath. "It was ours. The Tidewardens'. Our last great house. Our citadel. When the Drying came, they knew it would fall. So they took everything they had, everything the order had gathered in a thousand years, and they hid it. In a sea-cave under the citadel. My mother told me where. Her mother told her. Every one of us, all the way down. So that one day, if one of us ever got free—"

He stopped, coughing. Edric held him.

"Gold," Faro whispered, when he could. "Gold and silver and jewels. More than you can imagine. Enough to buy a fleet. Enough to buy a city. Enough to buy a kingdom, Edric, if a man were clever and patient about it. And books. The last books. Everything the Drying didn't burn." The good eye found Edric's face in the dark. "It's yours. If you get out. When you get out. It's yours."

"It's *ours*," said Edric. "We're getting out together."

"Learn the way," said Faro, as if he had not heard. "I'll teach you. Every turn, every mark, every rock. You'll learn it the way you learned your letters. And when you get there, you'll use it. For justice, Edric. Not for revenge. For justice. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Good," said Master Faro. "Good." And his grip on Edric's wrist loosened, and his breathing slowed, and he slept.

Edric lay in the dark tunnel with the old man in his arms, and listened to him breathe. Below them, through the hole, the gold light of the chains moved on the black water, and the sea boomed, slow and deep, and Edric thought, for the first time, that it did not sound angry at all.

It sounded like it was waiting.
