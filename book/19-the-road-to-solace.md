## Chapter 19: The Road to Solace

Aren Morrow was sixteen years old, and he had been a prisoner of the bandit king Vasco Rook for two days, and he had decided, on the whole, that he was not going to cry about it.

He had been very close, on the first night. When the men came down out of the rocks on either side of the road through the Greyhills, a dozen of them, in leather and rags, with crossbows and long knives and their faces wrapped in scarves, and cut the traces of the carriage and dragged his tutor out by the collar and shot both the outriders' horses. When they tied his hands behind him and threw him over the back of a mule like a sack of meal and led him up into the hills by tracks that were not tracks at all, for hours, in the dark, with the mule's spine grinding into his stomach at every step. When they finally put him down in a cave in the side of a ravine, in the ruins of what had once been a monastery, and a big man with a black beard and one gold earring came and squatted in front of him and looked at him by torchlight and said, quite cheerfully, "So this is the Marshal's boy. You're worth a great deal of money, little lord. Let's hope your father thinks so too."

He had been very close then. But he had remembered what his father always said, *a Morrow does not weep in front of his enemies*, and he had bitten the inside of his cheek until it bled, and stared at the big man with what he hoped was contempt, and said, "My father will hang you for this."

The big man had laughed.

"Your father," he said, "has been trying to hang me for six years, little lord. He'll have to catch me first." And he had gone away, still laughing, and left Aren alone in the cave with a guard at the door and a jug of water and a heel of bread, and Aren had sat with his back against the cold rock all night and not cried, not once, though it had been very close.

That had been two days ago. Since then he had been fed, twice a day, quite well. He had been allowed out of the cave, under guard, to walk in the ruined cloister of the monastery and look at the sky. Nobody had hurt him. The bandits, when they spoke to him at all, were almost friendly. One of them, a boy not much older than Aren with a squint and a missing front tooth, had shown him how to play a game with knucklebones, and beaten him at it eleven times running.

And this morning, the big man with the black beard, who was Vasco Rook himself, the outlaw king of the Greyhills, the most wanted man in Valemont, had come and told him that the ransom letter had been sent to his father in Solace: a hundred thousand marks, to be paid by noon in four days' time, at a place in the hills that would be named, or the Marshal's boy would be sent home in pieces.

"Not really," Rook had added, seeing his face. "I'm not a monster, little lord. Probably just a finger."

Aren had decided, then, that if they came to cut off his finger, he would fight. He had no weapon. His hands were untied now, but he was sixteen and slight and had only ever fenced with blunted foils in the Marshal's courtyard. They would kill him. But a Morrow did not let himself be cut up like a joint of meat.

He was sitting in the cave in the late afternoon, thinking about this, and about his mother, and trying not to think about his mother, when he heard the horses.

---

There were a dozen of them, coming up the ravine at a walk. He heard the bandits' lookouts call out from the rocks above, and the answering calls, and then, to his astonishment, laughter: a great bellow of laughter from Vasco Rook in the cloister, and running feet, and voices raised in greeting.

He went to the mouth of the cave. The guard did not stop him; the guard was looking too.

The horses came into the cloister one by one, through the broken arch of the monastery gate. They were magnificent horses, Sardhi greys, with silver on their bridles, and the men riding them were dressed in black and silver too, a livery Aren did not know, with a device on their breasts of a single curling wave. They were armed, but their weapons were sheathed. At their head rode a tall man in a long black riding coat with a high collar, and a broad-brimmed black hat, and as he rode into the cloister, Vasco Rook, outlaw king of the Greyhills, came striding across the broken paving towards him with his arms wide, and the tall man swung down from his horse, and they embraced like brothers.

"My lord!" Rook was saying. "My lord, my lord! You should have sent word! I'd have killed a sheep! Two sheep!"

"I didn't come to eat your sheep, Vasco," said the tall man. His voice was quiet and level, with a faint foreign lilt to it, and it carried across the whole cloister without seeming to try. "I came because I heard a rumour, in Fendle, that you'd taken a guest. A young one."

Rook's face changed. Something went across it that Aren, watching from the cave mouth, could not read: embarrassment, perhaps. Or fear.

"Ah," said Rook. "Ah. Yes. The Marshal's boy. My lord, it's only business. You know how it is. A hundred thousand marks. Times are hard in the hills."

"I know how it is," said the tall man. "And you know how I feel about children."

"He's not a child, my lord. He's sixteen."

"He's a child," said the tall man. "Where is he?"

Rook turned, and pointed, and the tall man turned too, and looked across the cloister at the mouth of the cave, where Aren Morrow stood with his hand on the rock, and for the first time, Aren saw his face.

It was a strange face. That was his first thought, and he never afterwards found a better one. It was a lean, hard, brown face, with a long jaw and a straight mouth and deep lines at the corners of the eyes, and there was nothing strange about any of those things. But it was very still. Stiller than any face Aren had ever seen. It was like looking at the surface of a pool in a cave, on a day without wind: nothing moving, nothing at all, and yet the sense that underneath, a long way down, things were moving that you could not see. The eyes were grey, and they looked at him for a long, long moment. And something happened in them, just for a moment, that Aren did not understand. Something that rippled, and was gone.

Then the tall man crossed the cloister towards him, and stopped in front of him, and took off his hat, and bowed.

"Lord Aren Morrow?"

"Yes," said Aren. His voice cracked on it. He made it steady. "Who are you, sir?"

"My name is Cristal," said the tall man. "The Count of Cristal. I'm a traveller, recently arrived in Valemont from the south. And I'm afraid my friend Vasco has been very rude to you. On his behalf, I apologise." He turned his head, without taking his eyes off Aren. "Vasco. Lord Morrow's horse, if you please. And his tutor, wherever you've put him. And his purse, which I'm sure you've been keeping safe for him. All of it."

"My lord—"

"*All* of it, Vasco."

There was a short silence in the cloister. Twenty bandits, armed to the teeth, stood among the broken pillars with their crossbows and their knives. A dozen men in black and silver sat their horses with their swords sheathed. And Vasco Rook, the outlaw king of the Greyhills, whom the Lord Marshal of Valemont had been trying to hang for six years, looked at the tall, quiet man in the black coat, and swallowed, and said: "Yes, my lord."

---

They rode down out of the hills together in the evening light, the Count's men around them, and Aren's tutor, a dry, nervous scholar named Master Pell who had spent two days in a sheep-pen and was not yet over it, jogging along behind on a borrowed mule, complaining under his breath.

Aren rode beside the Count. He had not meant to; it had simply happened. He found that he could not stop looking at him.

"How do you know him?" he said at last. "Rook?"

"I did him a service once," said the Count. "Some years ago. He was going to be hanged, in a town in the south, and I happened to be passing, and I thought it would be a waste. So I arranged for him not to be." He said it the way another man might say *I lent him a cloak*. "He's been grateful ever since. He's not a bad man, as such men go. He doesn't kill, unless he has to. He doesn't take from the poor. He only takes from people who can afford it." He glanced at Aren, sideways. "Like you."

"He was going to cut off my finger."

"He was going to cut off a finger," the Count agreed. "From one of the corpses in the old monastery crypt. And send that to your father. He's done it before. He's not as fierce as he likes people to think." The straight mouth moved, very slightly, in what might have been a smile. "Though I'm glad you didn't have to find that out."

Aren rode in silence for a while.

"Why did you come?" he said. "For me. You don't know me. You don't know my father. Why would you ride into the Greyhills, into Vasco Rook's own camp, for a stranger?"

The Count did not answer at once. He rode on, looking ahead down the road, with his still face shaded by the brim of his hat.

"I heard there was a boy in trouble," he said at last. "That's all. When I was not much older than you, I was in trouble too. A great deal of trouble. And nobody came for me. Not for a long time." He was quiet a moment. "It's not a thing I'd wish on anyone."

There was something in his voice that made Aren look at him again. But the face was as still as ever, and the eyes were on the road.

"Well," said Aren, a little awkwardly, "thank you. Truly. My father will want to thank you too. And my mother." He hesitated. "You said you were travelling. Are you going to Solace?"

"I am."

"Then you must let us repay you. Please. You must come to us. To the Marshal's palace. My father will want to meet you, and my mother will want to—well. She'll want to meet the man who brought me home." Aren felt himself flushing. "She worries. She'll have heard by now. She'll be frantic. She doesn't show it, she never shows anything, but she'll be frantic."

The Count turned his head and looked at him.

It was the same look as in the cloister: long and still, with something moving underneath it, far down. And Aren, meeting it, suddenly felt, for no reason he could name, that he had said something important. Something that mattered more to the man beside him than it should.

"Your mother," said the Count of Cristal, very quietly. "Yes. I would be honoured to meet your mother."

Then he looked away, down the road, and rode on, and did not say anything else for a long time.

---

Aren looked at him a good deal, on that ride, and in the days that followed, on the road north to Solace, when the Count, as a matter of course, it seemed, simply took charge of everything: sent riders ahead to Solace to tell the Marshal his son was safe; found them rooms at the best inn in every town, and paid for them; provided Master Pell with a horse, and a new coat, and a bottle of very good Sardhi brandy, after which Master Pell stopped complaining altogether and began to tell everyone that he had personally faced down Vasco Rook.

Aren looked at the Count, and tried to work him out, and could not.

He was rich. That much was obvious. Richer than anyone Aren had ever met, and Aren's father was the Lord Marshal of Valemont. He spent gold the way other men spent copper, without counting it, without seeming to notice. His men, the riders in black and silver, would have died for him; you could see it in the way they watched him, the way they moved when he moved. They called him *my lord* and sometimes, among themselves, when they thought no one was listening, something else, a word Aren did not know, that sounded like *Warden*.

He was clever. Frighteningly clever. He spoke High Sardhi with the innkeeper's wife in Fendle, and the southern trade-tongue with a merchant at an inn in Corley, and something else entirely, something old and soft and hissing, with a fishwife on the quay at Harnmouth who wept when he spoke to her and kissed his hand. He knew everything: the history of every town they passed through, the name of every lord whose lands they crossed, how much the harvest had been worth last year and why the price of salt had gone up. He asked Aren questions about his studies, and when Aren answered, he asked more questions, sideways questions, gentle ones, that somehow made Aren understand his own lessons better than Master Pell had ever managed.

He was kind. That was the strangest thing. He gave money to every beggar they passed, quietly, without making a show of it. He stopped the whole party for an hour on the road outside Corley because an old woman's cart had lost a wheel in a ditch, and got down and helped his men lift it, with his own hands, in his fine black coat. He listened to Master Pell's endless stories with every appearance of interest.

And he was cold.

Aren could not have explained it. It was not that the Count was unfriendly; he was always courteous, always attentive. It was something underneath the courtesy. Something that you felt, if you stood close to him, the way you feel the cold coming off the sea on a summer evening. There were moments, Aren noticed, when the Count would go very still, stiller even than usual, and his face would go somewhere a long way off, and the air around him would seem to drop a few degrees. Once, at an inn in Harnmouth, Aren had seen him standing alone at the window of the common room, looking out at the dark harbour, with a cup of wine in his hand, and the wine in the cup was trembling. Shivering, in small tight rings, as if the cup were on a ship in a storm. But the Count's hand was perfectly steady.

And once, on the last night before Solace, at an inn high on the cliffs above the sea, Aren had gone out into the yard to look at the stars and found the Count already there, standing at the edge of the cliff in the dark with his collar open and his hat off. And Aren had seen, just for a moment, before the Count heard his step and turned and buttoned his collar, something on the man's throat. Something faint and blue and shimmering, just under the skin, like light at the bottom of clear water. Like lines. Like veins of light.

He had blinked, and it was gone, and the Count was saying, quite calmly, "Can't sleep? Nor can I. It's a beautiful night."

Aren never asked about it. He did not know why. Something in the Count's face, that night, had told him not to.

---

They came to Solace on a morning in late spring, with the sun just risen.

The road came over the crest of the coastal hills and there it was, all at once, laid out below them like a jewel on a cloth: the white city on its cliffs. It climbed up from the sea in tiers, terrace on terrace, white walls and gold roofs and green gardens, bridges leaping across ravines, towers and domes and colonnades, until at the very top, on the highest point of the highest cliff, it rose into the Sunspire. The Sunspire was a single tower of white stone, so tall that its summit was lost in the morning haze, so slender that it seemed impossible it could stand. At its peak, even in daylight, something burned: a point of light so fierce and gold and steady that it hurt to look at, like a second sun hung in the sky.

All along the cliffs, at intervals, other lights flashed: the mirror towers, catching the morning sun and throwing it in long coded bursts across the hills to the next tower, and the next, carrying the King's word to every corner of Valemont faster than any horse could ride.

Aren had seen it all his life. He still caught his breath every time.

He turned to look at the Count, to see his face, and stopped.

The Count had reined in his horse at the crest of the hill. He was sitting very still in the saddle, staring at the city. Staring at the Sunspire. And his face had changed. It had gone pale, not with fear, Aren thought, or not only with fear, but with something else. Pain. As if the light at the top of the tower were a spike being driven into him. His hand had gone to his chest, over his heart, and was pressing there, hard, through the black coat. His lips were white.

"My lord?" said Aren. "Are you well?"

For a moment, the Count did not seem to hear. He was looking at the Sunspire, and at the gold light burning on its summit, and his grey eyes were narrowed against it as if against a gale.

*There you are,* Aren thought he heard him say. Very low. Not to Aren. To the tower. *There you are.*

Then the Count blinked, and took his hand from his chest, and turned to Aren with his still, courteous face, as if nothing had happened.

"Quite well, thank you," he said. "The sun's very bright, after the hills. Shall we go down? I believe your mother is waiting."

And he touched his heels to his horse, and rode down the long white road towards the city, with his men behind him in their black and silver, and his back very straight, and his hat pulled low against the light.

Aren rode after him, and did not look back at the Sunspire. But for the rest of his life, he would remember the way the Count had looked at it, on that morning in the spring, from the crest of the hill. It was the way Aren's father looked at a city he meant to besiege.
