# Part Three: The Count of Cristal

## Chapter 14: Salt and Smugglers

The *Slip* was the fastest ship in the Shattered Reach, and Jac Teague was the best smuggler, and both of these things were true mainly because Jac Teague said so, loudly and often, to anyone who would listen. But after a month aboard, the man who called himself Faro had come to believe that she was right.

The *Slip* carried everything. Brandy from the vineyards of the northern coast, on which the King's excise was so high that a cask in Saltmere cost a month's wages. Silk and spice from the southern empires, off-loaded at night from the holds of honest merchantmen at sea, before the honest merchantmen reached port and declared a lighter cargo than they had sailed with. Salt, which was a royal monopoly, and therefore worth smuggling in quantity. Letters that people did not want opened. People who did not want to be found. Jac took them all, at a fair price, and asked no questions, and took them where they needed to go, through the reefs and channels of the Reach where no customs cutter dared to follow, and never once in eleven years had she lost a cargo, a passenger or a ship.

She had a crew of nine, now ten. They were men from everywhere: a pair of brothers from the Sardh, lean and dark and silent; an enormous fair-haired northerner called Big Olaf who sang hymns to the Sun while he hauled lines and swore like a demon when he stubbed his toe; an old one-eyed bosun called Pike who had sailed with Jac's father; a boy of fifteen called Tam who had run away from an orphanage in Solace. They were thieves, most of them, and liars, some of them, and they were the best crew Faro had ever sailed with, including the crew of the *Gull's Promise*.

They took him in the way the sea takes in a stone: without fuss, closing over him as if he had always been there.

---

He had to learn everything again.

The sun was the first thing. For the first week, he could barely look at it. His eyes, after fourteen years in the dark, wept and burned in the daylight, and he went about the deck squinting like a mole, with a rag tied over his brow. His skin, which had been the colour of a fish's belly, burned red in an hour and blistered by evening. Jac laughed at him and gave him an old straw hat and a pot of evil-smelling grease made from fish oil and something that might once have been a plant, and after a while, his skin went pink, and then brown, and his eyes stopped weeping, and he found that he could stand at the rail in the middle of the day and look out at the blue sea under the blue sky, and that it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

His body was the second thing. Thirteen years of porridge and stone had left him thin as a rope and weak as a child. He could haul a line, as he had told Jac, but not for long; after an hour at the capstan his arms shook and his vision swam. Pike the bosun watched him for a few days, and then took him aside and made him eat: salt fish and hard bread and dried fruit and the thick black coffee the Sardhi brothers brewed, three times a day, whether he wanted it or not. And made him climb: up the ratlines to the masthead and down again, ten times, twenty, until his hands bled and then hardened and his shoulders began to fill out.

His hair was the third thing.

"You look," said Jac Teague, on the fourth day, studying him across the breakfast pot, "like a mop that's been in a fight with a bigger mop. And lost. Sit down."

He sat on an upturned bucket on the deck while she cut it off with a pair of sail-shears. Fourteen years of it fell around him in great matted ropes, and the wind took it and carried it away over the side. Then she lathered his face with a cake of sailor's soap and shaved him with a razor so sharp he did not feel it, standing over him with her tongue between her teeth like a woman icing a cake. When she had finished, she stepped back and looked at him, and her eyebrows went up.

"Well," she said. "Well, well. There's a face under there after all. Not a bad one, either, if you like them grim." She handed him a bit of broken mirror. "Have a look, Shipwreck."

He looked. A man looked back. Lean, brown, hard-faced, with a long jaw and a straight mouth and lines carved deep at the corners of the eyes. There was a scar he did not remember getting, a thin white line through his left eyebrow. There was grey in his hair, at the temples. He was thirty-three, and he looked forty, and he looked like no one he had ever met.

"You were in prison," said Jac. It was not a question.

He looked at her. She looked back, quite calmly, with her red hair blowing.

"I've seen it before," she said. "The skin. The eyes. The way you flinch at loud noises and stand with your back to a wall. The way you eat, like someone's going to take it away. Pike was in the King's galleys for six years; he was the same." She shrugged. "I said no questions, and I meant it. I don't care what you did. I don't care where you were or how you got out. You work hard, and you know the sea better than any man on this ship, and that's all I care about." She held out her hand for the mirror. "But don't treat me like a fool, Faro. I don't like it."

He gave her back the mirror.

"I won't," he said. "And thank you."

"Don't thank me," said Jac Teague. "Haul that line."

---

And then there was the sea.

He did not touch it, at first. He was afraid to.

He could feel it. All the time, every hour of every day, he could feel it, the way he had felt it in the water under the Teeth: every current, every swell, every eddy for miles around. He could feel the shoals of fish moving under the hull. He could feel the reefs before the lookout saw them. He could feel the weather building, days off, a pressure and a turning in the water that he could read like Halloran had read the sky. It was like having been half-deaf all his life and suddenly being able to hear.

And every so often, in the quiet hours of the night watch, standing at the wheel under the stars, he would feel something else. Something very far away, and very deep, down at the bottom of the world. Something huge and patient, turning slowly in the dark. Watching him.

He did not reach for it. He kept his hands on the wheel, and his mind on the course, and he did not reach.

He lasted six weeks.

---

It was a squall that did it: a sudden, savage, unforecast squall that came out of a clear sky off the northern edge of the Reach, in the middle of a night watch, and caught the *Slip* with all her canvas up.

He felt it coming a quarter of an hour before it hit. But a quarter of an hour was not enough. He shouted the warning, and Jac came up on deck in her shirt, cursing, and the crew tumbled up after her and swarmed into the rigging to get the sails in. But the squall came faster than they could work. It came out of the dark like a wall, a howling black wall of wind and rain, and hit the *Slip* broadside, and laid her over on her beam ends, so far over that the lee rail went under and the sea came pouring green across the deck.

He was at the wheel. He felt the ship go over. He felt her hang there, at the point of no return, with her masts almost flat to the water and the sea pouring into her and every man aboard clinging to whatever he could reach. He heard Tam, the boy, screaming somewhere in the dark. He felt her start to go.

And he reached.

It was not a decision. It was like throwing out a hand to catch a falling child. He reached for the sea, and the sea was there, waiting, eager, the way a dog waits at a door. He felt it come up into him through the deck, through the wheel, through his hands: cold, cold, colder than anything, a rush of cold that went straight to his heart and lit the lines on his chest like lamp-wicks. And he pushed.

The water under the *Slip*'s lee side heaved. A great slow swell rose out of the dark beneath her, not a wave but a lifting, a hand under the hull, and it pushed up, and up, against the wind and the weight and the sea pouring in. And the *Slip* came up. She came up off her beam ends like a horse struggling to its feet, groaning in every timber, shedding water from her decks in white sheets, until her masts were upright and her keel was under her and she was riding the squall instead of drowning in it. And he held her there, with the sea, with his hands on the wheel and his teeth bared and the cold in him like a fire, until the crew had the canvas in. Until the squall had blown itself through and away. Until the stars came out again, and the wind dropped, and the sea lay down.

Then he let go.

He let go, and the cold went out of him all at once, and he staggered and fell against the wheel and slid down it onto the wet deck, and sat there, shaking.

He tasted salt. Not on his lips: in his mouth, at the back of his throat, as if he had swallowed a mouthful of sea. And he saw something, for a moment, behind his eyes, as clear as if he were looking at it.

He saw a coast. Not this coast, not any coast he knew. A wild grey coast of cliffs and foam, under a sky full of strange stars, and on the cliffs a city, white and green and shining, and people on the shore in long robes with their hands raised to the sea. And the sea rising up to meet them, gently, the way a cat rises to meet a hand. And he felt, for that moment, what the sea felt, looking at those people on the shore: not love, exactly, not anything with a name, but something vast and warm and old, like the feeling of the sun on your back at the end of a long day.

Then it was gone. He was sitting on the wet deck of the *Slip* in the dark, and Jac Teague was crouching in front of him with a lantern, staring at him.

"What," she said, very quietly, "in the name of every god there is and some there aren't, was that?"

He looked at her. Behind her, the crew of the *Slip* were standing in a half-circle on the deck, soaked, silent, staring at him too.

He looked down at himself. His shirt had come open in the struggle. On his chest, over his heart, in the lantern light, the lines of the Tidemark were glowing. Not faintly, as they did in daylight, but brightly: a cold, shimmering, sea-blue light, branching out across his chest and down his arms and up his throat, pulsing slowly, like a heart.

He closed the shirt. It did not matter. They had all seen.

---

He told Jac, the next morning, in her cabin, with the door shut.

Not everything. Not the Keep, or the betrayal, or the Lock, or the King. But enough. That there had been people, long ago, who could talk to the sea. That they had been called Tidewardens. That an old man had taught him their ways. That something had happened to him, in the water, the night she found him on Gull Reef, that he did not fully understand. That he could feel the sea now, and sometimes, if he had to, he could make it move.

She listened without interrupting, with her boots up on the table and her arms folded. When he had finished, she was quiet for a long time.

"Tidewardens," she said at last. "My gran used to tell stories about them. Up in the north, where she came from. Witches who could call the fish and stop the storms. She said the King burned them all, a long time ago, and it was the worst thing that ever happened to the sea." She looked at him. "I thought it was a fairy story."

"So did I."

"Huh." She took her boots off the table. "And it was you, all along. The customs cutter we lost off the Hound's Teeth last month, that ran aground on a sandbar that wasn't on any chart. The fair wind we had all the way from Gull Reef, that never shifted once. The fish." She narrowed her eyes. "Olaf's been catching fish off the stern every night since you came aboard. He thinks the Sun's blessing him. He's been singing about it."

He had not meant to do any of those things. Or he had not known he was doing them. But as she said it, he knew it was true. The sea had been doing what he wanted, quietly, without being asked, because it was listening to him all the time, and wanting him to want things.

"Yes," he said. "I think it was me."

"Can you do it again? Last night?"

"Yes."

"Will you?"

He thought about the cold. About the taste of salt, and the city on the cliffs that was not his memory. About the eye in the dark, saying *a little more of me will come into you, every time you ask.*

"Only when I have to," he said.

Jac Teague looked at him for a long, long moment.

"Right," she said. Then she got up, and opened the door, and bellowed up the companionway for Pike to break out the good brandy. "Because I don't know about you, Shipwreck, but I'm not sure I want to know any more than that, and I'm absolutely sure I need a drink."

---

The crew never spoke of it again, not to him. But things changed, after that.

They called him Faro to his face, and something else behind his back: *the Warden*, he heard Tam say once, in an awed whisper, to one of the Sardhi brothers, and the brother made a sign with his fingers that Faro recognised from the old books as a ward against the evil eye, and then, a moment later, a second sign, older still, which meant *the sea's blessing*. Big Olaf stopped singing hymns to the Sun. Pike started saving him the best fish. And every so often, on a night watch, Faro would turn from the wheel and find one or other of them standing a little way off, watching him with the expression of a man watching a lighthouse: half fear, half gratitude, and something underneath that he did not want to look at too closely, because it looked like faith.

He did not use the sea again for a month. And then only small things: a current nudged, a reef felt in the dark, a storm turned a few points so it passed them by. Each time, a little cold. Each time, a little salt. Each time, a flicker of something that was not his own memory: a glimpse of a shoal of fish so vast it turned the sea silver from horizon to horizon; the feeling of a whale's song going through the water like a bell; the slow cold patience of a reef growing, a finger's breadth in a hundred years.

They were not bad memories. That was the thing that frightened him most. They were beautiful. They were the most beautiful things he had ever felt. And every time he let go of the sea, he missed them, the way a man misses a voice in an empty room.

---

They were in a smugglers' port called Corrow, on a rock at the very edge of the Reach, when he asked her.

Corrow was a filthy, crowded, lawless place of tarred shacks and taverns built into the cliffs, where every ship that did not want to be found came sooner or later to take on water and sell its cargo. Faro had gone ashore with Jac to help her bargain. He had found, to his surprise, that he was very good at it. Faro had taught him the lever, and he could see it now in every man they dealt with: what he wanted, and what he feared, and how much he would really pay. Jac had made half again what she had expected on the brandy, and was in a very good mood.

They were sitting in a tavern on the quay, drinking her profits, when he said: "Have you ever heard of the Isle of Cristal?"

Jac put down her mug.

"Everyone in the Reach has heard of Cristal," she said. "Way out west, past Hook Point, past the last of the lights. It's a big rock with a ruined castle on it. Cursed." She made the same sign the Sardhi brother had made. "Nobody goes there. There's no water and no game and nothing to steal, and they say the castle's full of ghosts. Sailors who anchor there overnight say they hear voices coming up out of the ground. Singing." She looked at him narrowly. "Why?"

"I want to go there."

"Of course you do," said Jac Teague. "Of course you bloody do." She drank. "Why?"

He thought about how much to tell her. Then he thought about the night of the squall, and her face in the lantern light, and *don't treat me like a fool.*

"Because the old man who taught me," he said, "the one who knew about the Tidewardens. He told me there was something there. Something they left behind, a long time ago, when the King came for them. Something they hid. He told me where it was. He made me learn the way, every rock and turn, the way you'd learn a prayer."

"Something," said Jac. "What kind of something?"

"The kind," said Faro, "that would buy you a fleet of ships like the *Slip*. And a harbour to keep them in. And a town round the harbour. And never have to run from a customs cutter again for the rest of your life."

Jac Teague stared at him across the table for a long time. Then she picked up her mug, and finished it, and set it down very carefully.

"I knew it," she said. "I knew, the moment I saw you lying on that reef, that you were going to be more trouble than you were worth." She stood up, and threw a coin on the table. "We sail on the morning tide."
