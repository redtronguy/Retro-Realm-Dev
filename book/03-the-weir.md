## Chapter 3: The Weir

Where the river Sallow came down out of the hills to meet the sea, it lost its way. It split into a hundred channels, wandered among reed-beds and mudbanks, pooled and spread and doubled back on itself, until it was hard to say where the river stopped and the sea began. The people who lived there called it the Weir, and they called themselves the Weir-folk, and the rest of Saltmere called them a good many other things, most of them unkind.

They had been there before Saltmere. That was what the Weir-folk said, and nobody had ever been able to prove them wrong. They had been there before the harbour wall, before the town, before the King. They built their houses on stilts above the water, of driftwood and reed and old ship's timbers, and joined them together with swaying walkways of rope and plank, so that the whole quarter was one great creaking, lamp-hung web strung across the marsh. They fished with nets their grandmothers had knotted and boats their grandfathers had built. They married among themselves, mostly. They spoke the common tongue of Valemont with an accent that turned every *s* into a soft hiss, like water over sand, and among themselves they spoke a language of their own that no one else in Saltmere understood. They paid their taxes and kept to themselves and were watched, always, by the town on the hill, with the particular mixture of suspicion and envy that settled people feel for those who seem to need less than they do.

Edric's mother had been Weir-folk. She had married a sailor from the town, which her family had not forgiven, and moved up the hill to Ropewalk Lane, which they had forgiven even less. But she had taken her son down to the Weir every summer when he was small, to see his grandmother before the old woman died, and so Edric had grown up with a foot in both worlds. He could walk the swaying planks in the dark without a lamp. He could understand the Weir-tongue, mostly, if people spoke slowly. And when he was fifteen, on the night of the Midsummer lanterns, when the whole Weir floated candles out on the water to light the way for the souls of the drowned, he had met Mira Solen.

---

She was on her family's landing stage when he came down the walkway, sitting with her legs dangling over the water and a net across her knees, mending it by the light of a lamp. She did not look up. She had heard his step on the planks a hundred yards away, as Weir-folk always did, and known it, and decided to make him wait.

"You're back, then," she said to the net.

"I'm back."

"Eight months, you said. It's been eight months and nine days."

"There was a storm off the Cape," said Edric. "Several storms. And the captain died. And I was very busy being a hero."

"Were you." She tied off a knot, bit through the twine with her teeth. "The whole Weir's been talking about it since noon. Old Bettan saw you come in through the narrows. She said you took the *Gull* through the Elbow against the tide, and she spat in the water, which is what she does when she's impressed and doesn't want anybody to know." She still had not looked up. "And then you went up the hill to see your father, which was right. And then you went to the bakehouse and the chandler's and the butcher's and the wine merchant's, which the whole town saw. And then you sat in your father's rooms for five hours. And *then*, when it's dark and every lamp in the Weir is lit and every old woman in the Weir is watching from her window to see what I'll do, then you come down here." She put the net aside. "Five hours, Edric Vane."

"He was starving, Mira. He'd been living on oats."

That made her look up.

She was nineteen, the same as he was, and she was not beautiful, exactly, not in the way the town girls on the hill were beautiful with their pale faces and their ribbons. She was brown from the sun and the wind, with a sharp chin and a wide mouth and a scattering of freckles across her nose that she hated. Her hair was black and thick and always coming loose from whatever she tied it with, and smelled of salt and smoke and the river. Her eyes were the dark green of deep water in shade. She had a temper like a summer squall and a laugh like a gull's cry, and when she was angry, as she was now, she went very still, and her eyes went very bright.

The stillness went out of her when she saw his face.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, Edric. Is he all right?"

"He's all right. He is now. I fed him till he begged me to stop." He sat down beside her on the landing stage, and their shoulders touched. "I didn't know. He never wrote. He was saving my wages. For us. For the wedding."

"Stubborn old goat," said Mira, but softly. She took his hand. Her own was rough with net-cord and cold from the water, and it fitted his exactly, as it always had. "And you? Are you all right? Captain Halloran. I'm sorry. I know what he was to you."

"I'm all right."

"You're not," she said. "But you will be." And then, because she was Mira, and could not stay soft for long: "Captain Vane. They're saying Moren gave you the ship."

"He did. This morning. On the quay."

She let out a whoop that sent a heron flapping up out of the reeds, and threw her arms round his neck, and kissed him so hard that they both nearly went into the water, and from a dozen windows across the dark marsh came the sound of old women cackling.

---

Later, when the cackling had died down and the lamps were burning low, they sat with their feet in the water, which was the oldest Weir-folk way of sitting, and talked about the wedding.

"Three days," said Mira. "Gran says the tides are right in three days. Spring tide, full moon. The luckiest wedding tide of the year."

"Three days," said Edric. "Can it be done?"

"It's already done. It's been done for a month. Ma's made the cakes. Gran's made the crown." The Weir-folk crowned their brides with woven sea-lavender and white shells, and the grandmother made it, always, and kept it hidden until the day. "We were only waiting for the groom. Which some of us had started to wonder whether we'd ever see again."

"I'm here."

"You are." She leaned against him. "Where will we have it? Here, or up the hill?"

"Here," said Edric at once. "On the water. Your way. And then a feast. A proper one. I'll hire the Three Anchors, the whole of it, and everyone can come. The crew, and Master Moren, and Father, and all the Weir. Everyone."

"Everyone," said Mira. "Even the ones who'll spit in the beer because I'm marrying a hill-boy?"

"Especially them. They can spit in the beer. I'll buy more beer."

She laughed. Then she was quiet a moment, looking out across the marsh to the dark line of the sea.

"Ma's not well," she said.

He turned to look at her.

"It's her chest. It's been bad this winter. Worse than last. The healer says she needs to be somewhere dry, away from the marsh, with a fire every night and good food. She says the damp will kill her otherwise." Mira's voice was quite steady, the way it always was when she was frightened. "We can't afford it. We can barely afford the healer."

"Then she'll come and live with us," said Edric. "Up the hill. In the house with the garden. I'll have it in two years, Mira, I promised Father, and I'm promising you. A house on the hill, dry as a bone, with a fire in every room. Your mother will sit by the fire and boss the cook, and my father will sit on the other side and boss the cook, and the cook will leave inside a week and you'll have to do it all yourself."

She laughed, a little wetly.

"Two years," she said. "Will you swear it?"

"My father says never swear. He says the sea hears."

"The sea does hear," said Mira, with the flat certainty of the Weir. "That's why you should swear it. So it knows."

So he swore it, there on the landing stage, with his feet in the cold dark water: that in two years there would be a house on the hill, and a fire in every room, and Mira's mother and his father warm and fed. And Mira, solemnly, dipped her hand into the water and touched it to his forehead, the way the Weir-folk sealed a vow.

The water was very cold. Colder than it should have been, even at night. For a moment, as her wet fingers touched his skin, Edric thought he felt it again, what he had felt on the deck that morning: something vast and far away, turning in its sleep.

He shivered. Mira felt it.

"What?"

"Nothing. A goose on my grave."

She looked at him strangely, and then out at the sea again.

"Gran says the sea's been restless," she said. "These last months. She says it's dreaming badly. She sits out on the end of the walkway every night and listens, and she won't tell anybody what she hears." Mira shook her head. "Old women's stories. Come on. You'll want to see Ma. She'll want to tell you how thin you are."

---

They were halfway along the walkway to the Solens' house when a figure stepped out of the shadows of a doorway and stood in their way.

He was a tall man, as tall as Edric and heavier, with black hair cropped short in the soldier's style and a hard, handsome, brooding face. He wore the blue coat of the King's Coastal Guard, unbuttoned, and there was a sword on his hip. He had been drinking. Edric could smell it from three paces off.

"Fenrick," said Mira, in a voice that had gone flat and careful. "I thought you were back at the garrison."

"I've three days' leave," said Fenrick Morrow. "Thought I'd spend them at home." He did not look at her. He was looking at Edric. "So. The sailor's back."

"Hello, Fenrick," said Edric. He had known Fenrick for four years, since he first started courting Mira, and in all those years the man had never once said more than a dozen words to him, and none of them friendly. Fenrick was Mira's cousin, the son of her mother's brother. He had grown up three houses along the walkway from her. He had loved her, everyone in the Weir knew, since they were both children, and it was also known, though nobody said it aloud, that he had asked her to marry him when she was sixteen, and that she had said no, kindly but completely, and that he had gone off the next week and enlisted in the Coastal Guard, and had come home on leave every few months ever since to stand in doorways and look at her.

"Captain, I hear," said Fenrick. "Moren's made you captain."

"He has."

"Funny, that. A boy who was hauling nets four years ago." Fenrick's mouth twisted. "Some people have all the luck."

"Fenrick," said Mira. "Go home."

"I am home. This is my home. I was born here. Same as you." Now at last he looked at her, and what was in his face was so raw that Edric, despite himself, felt something close to pity. "He's not one of us, Mira. He's a hill-boy. His mother left the Weir and spat on us. He'll take you up the hill and in five years you'll be one of them, with your hair in ribbons and your nose in the air, ashamed of your own kin."

"That's enough," said Edric.

"Is it?" Fenrick took a step closer. His hand was resting on the hilt of his sword: not gripping it, not drawing, only resting there, the way a man rests his hand on a dog's head. "Is it, Captain?"

The walkway was narrow, no more than a yard wide, with the black water four feet below on either side. Lamps swung on their ropes. Across the marsh, windows had gone dark, the way windows in the Weir did when there was trouble, so that the people behind them could watch without being seen.

Edric did not move. He did not put his hand to his own belt, where there was only a sailor's knife. He looked at Fenrick and said, quite quietly:

"She chose, Fenrick. Three years ago, she chose. You know she did. I'm sorry it wasn't you. I truly am. But it's done. We're marrying in three days, on the spring tide, and I'd like you to come. You're her family. I'd like you to be there."

For a long moment Fenrick stared at him. His knuckles whitened on the sword hilt. Mira had stopped breathing.

Then Fenrick laughed: a short, ugly bark with nothing in it. He took his hand off the sword.

"Three days," he said. "Spring tide. Lucky." He stepped aside, back into the shadow of the doorway, and made them a bow, a mocking, sweeping, drunken bow that nearly toppled him off the walkway. "Congratulations, cousin. Congratulations, Captain. May the sea give you everything you deserve."

They walked past him. Edric felt the man's eyes on his back all the way along the walkway, like a knife between his shoulders, until they turned the corner by the net-sheds and the Solens' lamp came into view.

"I'm sorry," Mira whispered. Her hand was shaking in his. "I'm sorry. He's not, he wasn't always like that. When we were small, he was kind. He used to carry me on his shoulders at the Midsummer lanterns. He—" She stopped. "The army's made him hard. And the drink."

"It's all right," said Edric.

"It isn't. He frightens me sometimes. The way he looks." She shook her head. "Three days. Then he'll go back to his garrison and we'll be married and he can stand in all the doorways he likes."

"Three days," said Edric, and squeezed her hand, and put Fenrick Morrow out of his mind entirely, because he was nineteen, and in love, and a captain, and the world was his.

---

Fenrick stood in the doorway for a long time after they had gone, watching the Solens' lamp, and then the lamp going out.

Then he took a flask from his coat and drank from it, and found it empty, and threw it into the marsh, and walked. He walked the length of the Weir and up onto the harbour road, and along the quays, past the dark hulls of the ships at their moorings, past the *Gull's Promise* with a single lantern burning at her stern. He walked without thinking where he was going, the way a man walks when he is trying to get away from himself.

And presently he found that he had walked to the door of the Three Anchors, where the windows were yellow and the noise was loud and the beer was cheap; and that sitting at a corner table near the fire, with a mug in front of him and a pale, neat, clerkish face that Fenrick vaguely recognised from the quays, was a man who looked up as he came in, and saw the blue coat and the sword and the look on Fenrick's face, and smiled, very slightly, as if he had been waiting for him.
