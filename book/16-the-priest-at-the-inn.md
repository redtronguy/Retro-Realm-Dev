## Chapter 16: The Priest at the Inn

The marsh road ran north from Saltmere for forty miles, through flat wet country of reeds and dykes and lonely farms, before it climbed into the hills and joined the great king's road to Solace. It was not a busy road. The traffic that mattered went by sea. But carters used it, and drovers, and pilgrims walking to the shrines in the hills, and every so often along its length there was an inn, a low, mean, mud-coloured building squatting by the roadside with a sign over the door and a yard for the horses.

The worst of them, all the carters agreed, was the Dry Well, eleven miles out of Saltmere, where the road crossed the old Sallow dyke. The beer was sour and the beds had fleas and the stew was mostly turnip, and the landlord was a fat, soft, wet-eyed man who talked too much and drank most of his own stock, and his wife was a thin, yellow, shaking woman with a cough who looked at every guest as if she were working out what was in his purse. Most carters, if they could, drove the extra five miles to the Crossed Keys at Fendle, and the Dry Well stood mostly empty, with its sign creaking in the wind.

It was there, late on a wet afternoon in early autumn, that a priest came walking out of the south.

---

He was a Brother of the Lantern: one of the wandering mendicants who went from village to village through Valemont with nothing but a staff and a begging bowl and a little brass lamp hung on a cord round their necks, preaching the mercy of the Sun to anyone who would listen and sleeping wherever they were given a bed. There were hundreds of them. Nobody looked at them twice. That was why he had chosen it.

His robe was grey and patched. His feet were bare and muddy. His head was shaved, as the Brothers' heads were, and his face under the shaved scalp was the kind of face you forgot as soon as you looked away from it: neither old nor young, neither handsome nor plain, with a mild, patient, faintly sorrowful expression, like a man who has heard every sin there is and has stopped being surprised by any of them.

It had taken him three days to make that face.

He had sat in his cabin on the *Slip* with a bowl of still water in front of him and the old book open beside it, *On the Discipline of Still Water, and the Art of the Mask*, and he had breathed, and found the stillness, and looked into the water, and tried to do what the book said. *Let the face become a pool. A pool shows only what is above it. So let thy face show only what the watcher brings to it.* For the first two days, nothing. On the third day, looking into the bowl, he had seen his own face begin to blur, very slightly, at the edges, like a reflection when a breath of wind touches the water. He had held it. He had felt the cold come into him, and taste the salt, and seen for a moment a woman's face, an old woman in a long robe in a city of white stone, smiling at a child, a face he had never seen before and knew the Deep remembered. And when he looked into the bowl again, the face looking back at him was not quite his own. It was softer. Vaguer. It was a face that slid off the eye.

It was not a disguise, exactly. Anyone who knew him well, who looked hard, who expected to see Edric Vane, would see him. But nobody expected to see Edric Vane. Edric Vane had been dead for fourteen years. And so, when people looked at him now, they saw what they expected to see: a wandering priest, mild and dull and forgettable. And forgot him.

He held it now as he walked up to the door of the Dry Well, the way you hold a breath. It cost him something every minute, a thin cold trickle of the sea into his blood, like a tap left running. But he could hold it for hours, if he had to.

He had come to find Cade Rusk. It had taken him a week of asking, in Saltmere, as a priest, to learn that Cade Rusk no longer lived on Ropewalk Lane. That his shop had failed, ten years ago. That he had taken what money he had left and bought the lease of an inn on the marsh road, and that it was failing too.

He had not let himself ask about anything else, in Saltmere. Not yet. He had walked past the end of Ropewalk Lane, once, and not looked up. He had walked past the end of the Weir, once, and not looked down. He had not trusted himself to.

---

Cade was in the taproom, behind the bar, polishing a mug that would never be clean. He looked up when the door opened and saw a priest, and his face fell, because priests never paid. Then he remembered that priests sometimes had money from the people they begged from, and his face brightened again, a little.

"A Brother of the Lantern!" he said. "Welcome, Brother, welcome. A wet day for walking. Come in, come in, come and dry off by the fire. What can I get you? We've beer, and a stew on, and there's a bed upstairs if you're stopping."

He had got fatter. That was the first thing Faro thought. Fatter and softer and older, his face like a pudding that had been left out for a week, the small wet eyes sunk further into it. His hair had gone thin and grey. His hands, on the mug, shook.

"Thank you, friend," said the priest, in a soft, grave voice, nothing like Edric Vane's. "A bowl of stew, if you'd be so kind. And a cup of water. The Lantern forbids me beer." He sat down by the fire. "Is this the inn of Cade Rusk? Formerly a tailor, of Ropewalk Lane, in Saltmere?"

Cade's eyes went wary at once. "It might be. Who's asking?"

"Only a poor priest, with a message."

"A message?" The wariness deepened. Then, slowly, something else came into the wet eyes, something hungry. "From who?"

"From a dead man," said the priest. "Will you sit with me a while, friend? It's a long story, and a sad one, and I've walked a great many miles to tell it."

---

Cade brought the stew himself, and the water, and a jug of beer for himself, and sat down across the fire from the priest, and his wife came out of the back room and stood in the doorway in a shawl, coughing, watching them both with her yellow eyes.

"A dead man," Cade said. "Which dead man?"

"His name," said the priest, "was Edric Vane."

He watched Cade's face as he said it. He had been trained, for thirteen years, by the best reader of faces in Valemont, and now, since the sea had come into him, he could do more than read. He could feel. He felt it the way he felt a current under a ship: the jolt in Cade Rusk, the sudden cold shock, like a man stepping into icy water. And then, under the shock, rising fast, something else. Something sour and frightened and squirming. Guilt.

"Edric," said Cade. He had gone grey. "Young Edric. Gods. Gods have mercy. Dead, you say? He's dead?"

"He died in prison," said the priest. "A year ago. In the Drowned Keep, on the Teeth. I was the chaplain there, for a time. The only one the governor allowed. I sat with him at the end."

"The Drowned Keep," whispered Cade. "The *Keep*. He was in the Keep, all those years. We never knew. Nobody knew. They said he'd been taken to Solace, for trial. They said he'd been transported. They said all sorts. But the Keep." He put his face in his hands. "Fourteen years. Oh, gods."

The priest waited.

"Was it," said Cade through his fingers, "was it bad? At the end?"

"He died of a fever," said the priest. "Quietly. He was not alone." He paused. "He talked about home, at the end. About Saltmere. About his father. About a girl in the Weir, called Mira. And about his friends. He asked me, when he was dying, to find them for him. To tell them what had become of him. And to give them something."

Cade's hands came away from his face.

The priest reached into the breast of his robe, and brought out a small leather pouch, and opened it, and tipped something out into his palm, and held it out in the firelight.

It was a diamond. A single stone, as big as a quail's egg, cut in the old style with a hundred tiny faces, and it caught the light of the fire and broke it into a hundred tiny rainbows that danced across the walls and the ceiling and the face of Cade Rusk, who stared at it with his mouth open, and the face of his wife in the doorway, who had stopped coughing.

"He had it from a man he was in prison with," said the priest. "An old man, a rich man once, who died there and left it to him. And when Edric was dying, he gave it to me, and he said: *Brother, sell this, and divide what you get for it among the five people in the world who loved me. My father, Tomas Vane. My betrothed, Mira Solen. And the three friends who sat at my table on the night of my wedding feast: Dorran Quell, and Fenrick Morrow, and my neighbour, Cade Rusk.*" He closed his hand over the stone, and the rainbows went out. "It's worth, I'm told, about fifty thousand marks."

Cade made a sound, a small, strangled sound, like a man who has been punched in the stomach.

"Fifty," he said. "Fifty *thousand*."

"Ten thousand each," said the priest. "That was his wish. So I've come to find them. I've already been to Saltmere. I went to Ropewalk Lane, to find his father." He looked into the fire. "They told me Tomas Vane is dead."

---

Cade told him.

He told it badly, in pieces, out of order, with many long swallows of beer and some tears, and the priest sat by the fire and listened to all of it, and held his face still, still as a pool, and did not let anything show on it at all.

Tomas Vane had gone looking for his son the morning after the arrest. He had gone up to the magistrate's house and been turned away. He had gone to Moren, and Moren had gone up to the magistrate's house and been turned away. He had written letters, dozens of letters, to the Ministry of Justice in Solace, to the High Justicar, to the King himself, and paid a scribe in the market to write them for him in a fine hand, and never had an answer. He had spent the money in the iron-bound box, the wages Edric had been saving for the wedding, on the scribe and the paper and the postage, and on a man in the market who said he had a cousin in the Ministry who could find out where the boy was, and who took the money and was never seen again.

"And then he stopped," said Cade. "He just. Stopped. He stopped going out. He stopped writing. He sat by that window, day after day, looking at the harbour. Like he was waiting for a ship. And he stopped eating." He swallowed. "I offered. I swear I offered. Agna made soup. I took it up. He'd thank me, very polite, and put it on the table, and it'd still be there in the morning. The girl came, the Weir girl, Mira. She came every day. She'd sit with him and try to make him eat. She'd cook for him. He'd eat a little, for her, to please her, and then stop. Moren came. Moren offered him money, a pension, a room in his own house. He wouldn't take it. Said he was waiting for his son. Said his son would provide for him when he came home."

"How long?" said the priest, very quietly.

"A year. Bit more. The winter after." Cade wiped his eyes. "She was with him at the end, the girl. Mira. She was holding his hand. They said it was hunger, the physician said. But I think it was waiting. I think a man can only wait so long."

The fire crackled. Outside, the rain came down on the marsh.

The priest sat very still. Under the grey robe, over his heart, the lines of the Tidemark had gone cold as ice, so cold it burned, and he could feel the sea in him, rising, heaving, like it heaved against the chains under the Keep. He could feel every drop of water in the room: in the beer in Cade's jug, in the stew in his bowl, in the rain on the window, in the blood in Cade Rusk's soft fat body, *in his blood*, and he thought, quite calmly, *I could stop it. I could stop his heart. I could make the blood in him stand still. It would be easy. It would be nothing.*

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. He found the stillness. He held it. Under the table, his hands were fists, and the water in his cup had begun, very faintly, to tremble.

*Cold head. Warm heart. Never the other way round.*

He breathed, and breathed, and after a while the water in the cup lay still.

"And the girl?" he said. His voice came out quite steady. "Mira Solen?"

---

"She waited too," said Cade. "Longer than the old man. Two years, nearly. Every spring tide, she'd go and sit at the landing in the Weir, where the boat was, the wedding boat. They kept it there, the Solens. Garlanded and all. She'd sit there all night, every spring tide, and watch the water come up, and go down. The whole Weir knew. They used to talk about it."

"And then?"

"Her mother got sick. Sicker. The damp in the marsh, it was killing her. The healer said she had to go somewhere dry or she'd not see another winter. They'd no money. The old man's money was gone, and Moren's house was in trouble by then." He shook his head. "And then Fenrick came back."

The priest said nothing.

"Fenrick Morrow. Her cousin. He'd gone back to his garrison after the wedding, after the arrest, and they'd promoted him. Sergeant, then lieutenant. Nobody knew why. He'd never been much of a soldier before. Some said he'd done the King a service." Cade's eyes slid away. "He came back to the Weir eighteen months after, in a lieutenant's coat, with money in his pocket, and he went to her mother, and he said he'd take them both, the girl and the mother, to his new posting, up north at Harrowgate, where it was dry, and get the old woman a proper physician, and a house with a fire. And all he asked was that Mira marry him."

"And she did," said the priest.

"She did. In the end. For her mother." Cade drank. "I was at the wedding. It was up at the Temple of the Noon, in town, not in the Weir. She wouldn't have it in the Weir. She wore grey. She never smiled once. Not once, the whole day." He was quiet a moment. "Her mother lived three more years, up at Harrowgate. So I suppose it was worth it."

"And Fenrick Morrow?"

Cade laughed, a short, wet, bitter laugh.

"You must have been in that Keep a long time yourself, Brother, if you don't know about Fenrick Morrow. Everybody knows about Fenrick Morrow. He went east, to the war in the Sardh. Ten years ago. Went as a captain and came back a general. They say he was at the siege of Qasr, where the Emir Tariq held out for two years against the King's army, and that it was Morrow who ended it. Something about him being in the Emir's confidence, an adviser, and then the gates being opened one night, and the Emir dead, and the city taken." He shrugged. "Nobody knows the whole of it. But the King made him a count for it. Count Morrow. And then Lord Marshal of Valemont. He's the head of the whole army now. Lives in a palace in Solace. And she's the Countess. The girl from the Weir. Countess Morrow, with a son of eleven, and diamonds in her hair." He spat into the fire. "Good luck to them."

"And Dorran Quell?"

"*Baron* Quell." Cade's lip curled. "The purser. He left Moren's service a month after the wedding, went to Solace, got himself a clerk's job in the bank of old Haverel. Within three years old Haverel was dead and Quell had married his widow and taken over the bank. Within ten, it was the biggest bank in Valemont. He lent the King the money for the Sardh war. Made a fortune out of it. The King made him a baron, and then Lord Treasurer. He holds the King's purse now. They say there's not a mark spent in Valemont that doesn't pass through Dorran Quell's hands first."

The fire popped. The rain beat on the window. The priest sat with his hands folded in his lap, and his face still as a pool.

"And the magistrate?" he said. "The one who examined Edric, the night he was taken. Corvan Valtor."

Cade looked at him sharply. "How do you know that name?"

"Edric told me. He told me everything."

"Did he." Cade's eyes slid away. "Well. Valtor went back to Solace the spring after, to marry some great lady. They say he's High Justicar now. The highest judge in the land, under the King. The King's own hand on the law." He laughed again. "They call them that now, you know, in Solace. Morrow and Quell and Valtor. The King's Three Hands. The sword, the purse and the law. The three most powerful men in Valemont, after the King himself."

He drank, and set down his jug, and stared into the fire.

"Funny, isn't it?" he said. "Funny how it all turned out. The three of them, at the top of the world. And him, dead in the Keep. And me." He looked around the dirty taproom, the empty tables, the rain on the window. "And me here."

---

The priest let the silence go on for a long time.

Then he opened his hand, and the diamond lay in his palm, and caught the firelight.

"Edric loved them," he said softly. "His friends. Dorran, and Fenrick, and you. He talked about you all, at the end. About his wedding feast, and how happy he was, and how he wished you could all have been happy with him. He wanted you to share this. Equally. As friends." He looked at Cade. "But you seem troubled, friend. When you speak of Dorran Quell and Fenrick Morrow, there's something in your voice. As if you were holding something back."

Cade stared at the diamond.

Faro felt him. He felt it as clearly as a current: the struggle in the soft, fat, frightened man across the fire. The fear, old and deep and settled, of the purser and the soldier, who had become the Lord Treasurer and the Lord Marshal of Valemont. The habit of forty years, of silence, of staying out of trouble. And against it, rising like a tide, pulling, pulling, the want. Ten thousand marks. Or fifty thousand, if the others were not friends after all. If the others did not deserve it. If the dying man had been wrong about them.

He did not push. He did not need to. He only had to wait.

"Brother," said Cade, very low. "Brother, if a man knew a thing. A terrible thing. About how young Edric came to be taken. And he'd kept it to himself, all these years, out of fear. Would it be a sin, to tell it now? Now he's dead, and can't be hurt by it any more?"

"It's never a sin to tell the truth," said the priest. "Only sometimes too late."

And Cade Rusk told him.

The Three Anchors. The purser, sitting in the corner with his small beer. The soldier, coming in out of the night with that look on his face. The black rum. His own voice, *between ourselves*, telling them about the message for Solace, the old man's words through the floor. The pen. The left hand. The blotted, childish letters. *To the Crown Magistrate, from a friend to the Throne.* The crumpled ball, tossed into the corner. The soldier's eyes on it. Waking at midnight, and the corner empty.

He told it all. And the priest listened, and did not move, and felt every word go into him like a nail.

He had known. He had known for nine years, since the day in the cell when Faro had laid it out for him, piece by piece, like a chart. But knowing a thing from a puzzle in the dark is not the same as hearing it from a man who was there. He saw it now. He saw Dorran's neat hand passing the pen to the left, smiling. He saw Fenrick on his knees in the sawdust behind the settle, groping for the paper. He saw Cade with his head on the table, waking, looking at the empty corner, and getting up, and going home to bed.

"And you said nothing," said the priest.

"I was frightened." Cade was weeping now, openly, his soft face wet. "They'd have killed me. The soldier had a sword. And the purser, you should've seen his eyes. And I thought, I told myself, it might come to nothing. It was only a letter. I didn't know they'd send him to the *Keep*. I didn't know he'd be there fourteen years. I swear I didn't know." He wiped his face on his sleeve. "I've thought about it every day since. Every day. Every time I heard the old man coughing upstairs. Every time I saw the girl going up the stair with her soup. I'd think: I could have stopped it. One word. I could have said one word." He looked up at the priest with his wet, pleading eyes. "Is that a sin, Brother? Is that the worst thing a man can do? To not say a word?"

The priest looked at him for a long time.

"It's not the worst thing," he said at last. "But it's not nothing."

Then he leaned across, and took Cade Rusk's soft, shaking hand, and opened it, and laid the diamond in the palm.

"Edric wanted this to go to the people who loved him," he said. "His father is dead. His betrothed is a countess and has no need of it. And the other two." He let the words hang. "I don't think he would want the other two to have it. Not now. Not knowing what you've told me." He closed Cade's fingers over the stone. "It's yours. All of it. Do some good with it, friend. You've a long way to go to balance the account."

Cade stared at his own closed fist as if it belonged to someone else.

The priest stood up, and took his staff from beside the fire, and his little brass lantern, and went to the door.

"Brother," said Cade behind him. His voice was a whisper. "Brother, wait. Your name. I never asked your name."

The priest stopped at the door, and turned, and for a moment, just a moment, he let the stillness go.

He felt it go out of his face like a tide going out. He felt the vagueness lift, the blur at the edges sharpen, and his own face come back, the face he had been born with, hard now, and lined, and fourteen years older, but his. He looked across the taproom at Cade Rusk with Edric Vane's grey eyes, and he saw Cade see him. He saw it hit the man like a wave: the colour draining out of the soft fat face, the mouth falling open, the eyes going wide, wider, with something that was not quite recognition and not quite terror but some sick, cold, impossible mixture of both.

Then he let the stillness come back, and the face blurred, and softened, and was only a priest's face again, mild and forgettable.

"Faro," he said. "My name is Brother Faro. Goodbye, Cade Rusk."

And he went out into the rain.

---

Behind him, in the taproom of the Dry Well, Cade sat by the fire for a long time with the diamond in his fist, shaking, telling himself he had imagined it. That it was the beer, and the firelight, and the story, and the dead man's name. That it was nothing.

In the doorway to the back room, his wife Agna stood in her shawl and did not cough. She was looking at his closed fist. She had been looking at it since the priest put the diamond into it, and she did not stop looking at it now. Her yellow eyes were very bright.

"Fifty thousand marks," she said softly. "He said fifty thousand."

"Agna—"

"There's a jeweller in Fendle," said Agna Rusk. "A Sardhi. He buys stones and doesn't ask where they came from. He comes through on the market road every month." She came across the room, and knelt beside her husband's chair, and put her thin yellow hand over his fat one. "He's due next week."
