## Chapter 21: Currents of Gold

Baron Dorran Quell, Lord Treasurer of Valemont, rose every morning at five, in the dark, in a bedroom of white marble in a house of white marble on the great terrace below the Sunspire, and the first thing he did, before he washed or dressed or said his prayers to the Sun, was look at the numbers.

He kept them in a small black ledger on the table by his bed. Not the bank's numbers; those were kept by forty clerks in the counting-house on the Street of Gold, in ledgers the size of tombstones. Not the Treasury's; those were kept by the Treasury. These were his own numbers. The real ones. The ones nobody else had ever seen. He sat on the edge of his bed every morning by the light of a single candle and turned the pages and ran his eye down the columns, and added them up in his head, and checked them against yesterday's, the way another man might check his pulse.

He was forty-five years old. He had been the richest man in Valemont, after the King, for eleven years. And every morning, for eleven years, when he looked at the numbers, his hands had shaken, very slightly, just for a moment, before they steadied.

Because the numbers did not balance. They never had.

---

It had started with the Sardh war. Everything started with the Sardh war.

He had been a clerk in the bank of old Haverel, then, three years out of Saltmere, quiet and neat and clever, and he had seen what nobody else in the bank had seen: that the King's war in the east was going to need money, a great deal of money, more than the Treasury had, and that whoever lent it would own the war. He had persuaded old Haverel to lend it. Then old Haverel had died, very suddenly, of a stomach complaint, and Dorran had married his widow, Hermione, a cold, handsome, bored woman ten years older than himself who had needed a man to run the bank and had not cared much which. And Dorran had run it. And lent the King everything. And when the war was won, the King had paid him back with interest, and a barony, and the Treasury.

That was the story everyone knew. It was a good story. It was mostly true.

What nobody knew was that the war had cost more than the King had paid. That the bank had lent more than it had. That the gap between the two, in the last year of the war, had been very large, and that Dorran had filled it, quietly, carefully, the way he had once filled casks of pepper with sand, by borrowing from the only place in Valemont that had more money than his bank. The Treasury itself.

He had always meant to put it back. He had put some of it back. But there had always been something else that needed money first: a new loan to the southern Empress, which would pay twenty per cent if it paid at all; a fleet of grain ships to break the northern merchants' hold on the harvest; a speculation in Sardh war bonds, bought cheap after the war and sure to rise. And so the hole in the Treasury had not shrunk. It had grown. It was the Blight Fund now, mostly, the great fund raised by special tax every year to pay for the sunfire beacons along the coast and relief for the fishing towns, six million marks of it, of which perhaps two million was actually where the Treasury's ledgers said it was. The rest was in Dorran's grain ships, and Dorran's bonds, and Dorran's loans. It was the foundation of everything. It was the ground under his feet.

And every morning, he sat on the edge of his bed and looked at the numbers, and told himself that this year, at last, he would fill the hole. And every morning his hands shook.

---

He went to see the Count of Cristal on the morning after the dinner.

He had been at the dinner, of course. Everyone had been at the dinner. But he had been at the far end of the table, beside some dull Radiant lord's wife, and had barely exchanged ten words with the Count. He had watched him, though. He had watched him all evening, with his clerk's eye, the eye that could find a weak seam in a ledger, and what he had seen had made him go home and lie awake until dawn doing sums.

The Count's fortune was real. That was the first thing. Dorran had spent the fortnight since the Count's arrival making inquiries, quietly, through the bank's agents in Port Jasmine and the southern cities and the Sardh, and the answers had come back by the mirror towers in less than a week. Thornbury and Wilde of Port Jasmine existed, and was solid as rock, and held funds on behalf of the Count of Cristal so large that the bank's agent had at first assumed there was a mistake in the coding. The Count had bought half a street in Port Jasmine. He had bought a fleet of ships. He owned, it was said, an island in the far west, beyond the Reach, which he ruled like a king.

And he did not seem to care about money at all. That was the second thing, and the more interesting. He spent it like water. He had paid three times the value for the house with the blue door. He had spent, Dorran estimated, at least two hundred thousand marks on that one dinner. He tipped servants in gold.

A man like that needed a banker. And whoever became his banker would never have to worry about a hole in the Treasury again.

---

The Count received him in a small study at the back of the house with the blue door, a room panelled in dark wood with a window looking out over the sea. He was sitting at a desk when Dorran came in, writing; he rose, and bowed, and offered him a chair and a glass of Sardhi wine, and sat down again, and folded his hands, and waited.

He had a very still face, the Count. Dorran found it unsettling. He was used to reading faces; it was how he had made his fortune. But the Count's face was like a closed ledger.

"My lord," said Dorran, smiling his small smile. "I'll come straight to the point; I know you're a busy man. You've arrived in Solace with a great fortune, and I'm told you intend to stay some time. You'll need a bank. I'd be honoured if it were mine."

"I'd heard," said the Count, "that you were the best."

"The largest," said Dorran modestly. "I couldn't speak to best."

"I could." The Count reached into a drawer of his desk and took out a letter, and pushed it across. "From my bankers in Port Jasmine. A letter of credit. I'd like to open an account with you on the strength of it. If you'll accept it."

Dorran picked up the letter and read it. He read it twice. Then he put it down very carefully on the desk, and looked at the Count.

"This is a credit without limit," he said.

"Yes."

"My lord, I don't think you understand. A credit without limit means that you may draw on my bank for any sum. Any sum at all. And my bank must pay it, on demand, and recover the money from Thornbury and Wilde in Port Jasmine, which may take months."

"I understand perfectly," said the Count. "Is it too much for you? I could go to Haverel's old rivals, the Vossens, I suppose. Or the Bank of the North."

"No," said Dorran quickly. "No, no. Not at all. It's only unusual. Very unusual." He picked the letter up again. A credit without limit, from a house as solid as Thornbury and Wilde. It was the kind of thing a banker saw once in a lifetime. With a client like this, he could refill the Blight Fund in a year. In six months. He could stop looking at the numbers in the morning. He could stop his hands shaking. "Of course. Of course, my lord. The house of Quell would be honoured. What sum would you like to draw, to begin with?"

The Count considered.

"A million marks," he said. "By the end of the week. In gold. I'll send my steward."

---

He drew a million by the end of the week. And another million by the end of the next. And half a million the week after that, and three hundred thousand two days later, and seven hundred thousand on the last day of the month, and every time, the Count's steward, a silent black-browed man with a scar across his throat, came to the counting-house on the Street of Gold with the Count's seal and a cart and four armed men, and took it away, in gold, and every time, the house of Quell paid it, on demand.

Dorran sent the drafts to Port Jasmine by the fastest ships. Thornbury and Wilde honoured every one. But Port Jasmine was a month away by sea, and the gold took a month to come back, and in between, it had to come from somewhere.

It came from the bank's reserves. And when the reserves were thin, it came from the deposits. And when the deposits were thin, it came from the only place left.

Dorran did not sleep much, that month. He sat on the edge of his bed every morning and looked at the numbers, and his hands shook, and did not stop.

---

Edric knew. He knew it the way he knew the tides.

He had felt the bank of Quell from the library at Cristal, five years ago, without knowing what it was: a great slow drain at the heart of Valemont, pulling money in from every corner of the kingdom. Now he was standing on top of it. In Solace, with the whole city around him, the currents were so strong that he could feel them without trying, the way you feel a river under a bridge. Money moved through the white city like blood through a body, in great slow pulses, through the Treasury and the banks and the merchant houses and the Chamber of Lords and the Sunspire. And at the centre of it, pulling, pulling, was Dorran Quell.

He sat in the study at the back of the house with the blue door, at night, with the lamp turned low and a bowl of still water on the desk in front of him, and let himself feel it. He had never used Undertow like this before, so deliberately, so long. It came easily. Frighteningly easily. He breathed, and found the stillness, and reached, and the currents of Solace opened up around him like a map.

He felt the bank of Quell. He felt its shape: vast, glittering, impressive, like a great house seen from the street at night with every window lit. And he felt, behind the lit windows, how much of it was empty. How the money flowed in and flowed out again, faster and faster, to cover other money that had flowed out before. How it was propped up, at its foundations, by something that was not the bank's at all. Something with the King's seal on it.

*The Blight Fund,* he thought, and saw it: six million marks, raised from the fishing towns and the coastal villages, from the poor, to pay for the beacons that kept the blighted things from the shore. Four million of it gone into Dorran Quell's grain ships and southern loans and speculations. Four million marks that should have been beacons and medicine and bread, for towns like Saltmere, where the fishing had failed and the Weir-folk were leaving.

He sat back from the bowl. He was cold, very cold, and the taste of salt was strong in his mouth.

He could do it now. He could feel exactly where to pull. One more draft on the bank, a large one, at the right moment, and the bank would not be able to pay. And when a bank cannot pay, everyone who has money in it comes running to take it out, all at once, and the bank falls, and when the bank fell, the hole in the Blight Fund would be found. And Dorran Quell would hang.

He did not do it. Not yet. It was too quick. It was too easy. And he could feel, under the cold, rising, the thing he had felt on the beach at Cristal: the thing that wanted it. That would enjoy it.

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. He reached for the bowl and laid his hand flat on the water until it was still.

*Not like that,* he thought. *Not by pulling. By letting him pull himself.*

---

There was a mirror tower at Grey Point, on the coast road twenty miles south of Solace: a tall, narrow tower of white stone on a headland, with a great mirror on its summit, twelve feet across, mounted on a frame of brass that could be turned and tilted to catch the sun and throw it north to the next tower on the cliffs below Solace, or south to the next tower on the road to Saltmere.

Its keeper was an old man called Hob Fennick, who had sat on top of that tower for thirty-one years, reading flashes of light from the south and passing them on to the north, and the other way, every day from sunrise to sunset. He had passed on news of wars, and births, and deaths, and the prices of grain in the Sardh, and the King's commands. He did not understand any of it. The messages were in code, and the keepers were not given the code; they only read the pattern of flashes and copied it, flash for flash, long and short, to the next tower. It was dull work, and badly paid, and Hob had done it faithfully for thirty-one years because it was the only work he knew.

He had a granddaughter, Nell, who was nine. She lived with him in the two rooms at the foot of the tower, because her mother was dead and her father was at sea. And she was dying.

It was the blight-cough. Everyone on the coast knew the blight-cough now; it had come up out of the sea in the last ten years, with the blighted things and the failing fish, and it took the children first. It started with a cough, and a fever, and then the lungs filled up with water, slowly, slowly, over months, until one day they were full.

Hob had spent everything on physicians. The physicians had told him there was nothing to be done. He sat by her bed at night, after his day at the mirror, and held her hand and listened to her breathe, and to the water bubbling in her chest, and prayed to the Sun, and the Sun did nothing.

On a night in early summer, there was a knock at the door at the foot of the tower, and when Hob opened it, a tall man in a black coat was standing there with a lantern, and behind him, on the road, a carriage with silver on the harness.

"Master Fennick," said the tall man. "I'm told you have a sick child."

---

Edric sat by the little girl's bed for three hours, that night, with his hand on her chest.

He had never done this before. The books had told him how. *On the Healing of the Waters*, from the twelfth stone chest, from a Warden of the House of Cristal who had been strong in Undertow and had spent her life in the hospitals of the city. *All sickness is a disturbance of the waters,* she had written. *A fever is a storm. A wasting is a drought. A drowning sickness is a flood. Feel the waters of the body as thou wouldst feel the sea, and find the current that is wrong, and draw it out, gently, as thou wouldst draw a splinter.*

He felt the water in the child's lungs. It was sick water, blighted water, the same sickness as the sea: grey and cold and heavy and wrong. He felt it clinging, filling, drowning her from the inside, a little more every day. And he reached for it, gently, as you put your hand into a pool, and drew.

It came slowly. It did not want to come. It clung to her like weed to a rock. He drew, and drew, and felt it coming up through her, out of her lungs, out of her blood, into his hand, and up his arm, cold, cold, into him. It was like drawing poison into his own body. It was the sea's sickness, the Deep's sickness, the thing that three hundred years of chains had done, and he felt it go into him, and felt the Tidemark on his chest flare and burn, and tasted salt and rot, and saw, behind his eyes, for a moment, the sea itself, grey and dying, with dead fish floating on it as far as the eye could see.

He held on. He drew until there was nothing left to draw, and the child's breathing, under his hand, was clear and quiet and slow.

Then he let go, and sat back, and put his head between his knees, and was very sick on Hob Fennick's floor: sick with seawater, grey and stinking, more of it than could possibly have been in him.

When he could sit up again, the old man was kneeling by the bed with his granddaughter's hand in his, weeping. And the child was asleep, quite peacefully, with colour in her cheeks for the first time in a year.

---

He did not ask the old man for anything that night. He went back to Solace in the carriage, and slept for a day and a night, and woke with the Tidemark spread a finger's breadth further across his cheek, and the taste of rot still in his mouth.

He went back to Grey Point a week later. Nell was up and running about the yard at the foot of the tower, chasing chickens. Hob Fennick met him at the door and fell on his knees in the dust.

"Anything," said the old man. "My lord, anything. Ask me anything. My life."

"I don't want your life," said Edric. "Get up, Master Fennick." He helped the old man to his feet. "I'm going to ask you to do one thing. And then I'm going to give you a purse of gold, and a letter, and the name of a ship that will be waiting for you at Harnmouth harbour, and you'll take Nell and go to a place called Cristal, in the west, where there are no mirror towers, and where nobody will ever find you. And you'll never sit on top of a tower again."

"What thing, my lord?"

Edric took a folded paper out of his coat.

"There's a message on this paper," he said. "A pattern of flashes. Long and short. I want you to send it north, to the tower below Solace, tomorrow at noon, as if it had come to you from the south. Just once. That's all."

The old man took the paper and looked at it. He could not read the code; no keeper could. But he could read the pattern, and he looked at it for a long time.

"It's not a true message," he said slowly. "Is it, my lord?"

"No," said Edric. "It's not true."

"Will anyone die of it?"

Edric thought about that. He thought about it carefully, as Faro had taught him to think: the whole of it, every current, every consequence.

"No one will die of it," he said. "One man will lose a great deal of money. Money that was never his. Money he stole from people like you, from towns like this, from children like Nell. Money that should have bought medicine for the blight-cough, and beacons for the coast." He looked at the old man. "But I won't make you do it, Master Fennick. If you say no, you'll still have the purse and the ship. You owe me nothing."

Hob Fennick looked at the paper for a long time. Then he looked across the yard, at his granddaughter, chasing a chicken round the well, laughing.

"Tomorrow at noon," he said.

---

At noon the next day, the mirror on the tower below Solace caught a flash of light from the south, and its keeper copied it, flash for flash, and sent it on to the Sunspire, and the clerks of the Ministry of War decoded it and read it, and went pale, and sent runners to the Treasury and the Chamber and the palaces of the Three Hands.

*FROM MAZRAH. URGENT. QASR RISEN. CITY TAKEN BY REBELS. SON OF EMIR TARIQ PROCLAIMED IN THE GREAT MOSQUE. ALL SARDH PROVINCES IN REVOLT. GARRISONS BESIEGED. MORE FOLLOWS.*

Baron Quell was in his counting-house on the Street of Gold when the Treasury runner reached him. He read the message once, standing up. Then he sat down, very suddenly, and read it again.

The Sardh war bonds. He held nearly two million marks of Sardh war bonds, bought cheap after the war, sure to rise. They were secured on the peace in the Sardh. On the King's hold on the provinces. If the provinces were in revolt, if the Emir's heir had been proclaimed, if there was going to be another war, the bonds would be worth nothing. Less than nothing. And everyone who held them, every bank and merchant house in Solace, would be trying to sell them the moment the news got out. Which would be in an hour. Two at most.

He did not hesitate. He had never in his life hesitated over money. He called his chief clerk and gave the order: sell. All of it. Every Sardh bond the house of Quell held, at whatever price anyone would pay, before the news reached the exchange.

By the close of the exchange that afternoon, the house of Quell had sold one million eight hundred thousand marks' worth of Sardh war bonds for a little under six hundred thousand. Dorran sat in his office with the door shut, and looked at the numbers, and felt sick, and told himself that he had saved six hundred thousand marks from a disaster, and that every other bank in Solace would be ruined by morning.

At nine o'clock the next morning, a second message came up the mirror towers from the south.

*FROM MAZRAH. CORRECTION. PREVIOUS MESSAGE IN ERROR. MIRROR FAULT AT GREY POINT. NO RISING AT QASR. SARDH PROVINCES QUIET. GOVERNOR SENDS LOYAL GREETINGS TO THE THRONE.*

By noon, the Sardh war bonds were trading higher than they had been before the panic, because so many of them had been sold so cheaply that everyone who had kept their nerve had bought more. The Vossens had bought most of Dorran's. They made a million marks on them, in a day.

And when the Ministry of War sent a rider to Grey Point to find out what had gone wrong with the mirror, they found the tower empty, and the two rooms at the foot of it empty, and the keeper and his granddaughter gone, and nobody on the whole of that stretch of coast had the faintest idea where.

---

Dorran Quell sat in his counting-house that night, long after his clerks had gone home, with a single candle burning, and his private ledger open on the desk in front of him, and looked at the numbers.

One million two hundred thousand marks. Gone. In a day. On a lie. On a mirror fault. And the Count of Cristal had drawn another three hundred thousand that afternoon, as if nothing had happened, and the bank had paid, and the reserves were empty now, and the hole in the Blight Fund was not four million marks any more. It was nearly five.

His hands were shaking. They had been shaking all day, and they would not stop.

He thought, for no reason, of a tavern in Saltmere. The Three Anchors. A corner table, by the fire, twenty years ago, and a pen passing from his right hand to his left. He had not thought of it in years. He had used to take the memory out and look at it, in the early days, the way another man might look at a medal. He had stopped, somewhere along the way. He could not remember when.

There was a knock at the door. His secretary, very apologetic: a letter had come, by hand, a boy had left it at the gate, marked *urgent, for the Lord Treasurer only*.

Dorran took it and broke the seal. It was a single sheet of cream paper, with nothing on it but one line of writing, in a hand he did not know.

*Every captain needs to keep good accounts.*

He sat and looked at it for a very long time. And then he got up, and went to the window, and looked out across the dark city to the sea, which was black and quiet under the stars. He stood there for an hour. He could not have said why. He only felt, very strongly, that someone was looking back.

---

On the other side of the city, in the study at the back of the house with the blue door, Edric took his hand off the bowl of still water, where the image of a pale, neat, frightened man standing at a window in a white marble house had trembled for a moment on the surface, and gone.

He sat back in his chair. He was very cold. And he was smiling.

He noticed it after a moment: the smile. It was on his face, and he had not put it there. A small, cold, satisfied smile, like the smile of a man who has just tasted something good.

He stopped smiling.

He sat for a long time in the dark study, very still, with his hands flat on the desk, and thought about an old man in a cell gripping his hands. *The moment it becomes sweet.*

It had been sweet. Just for a moment. It had tasted like honey.

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. And did not reach for the bowl again that night.
