## Chapter 29: Return to the Keep

He could not stand for two days. On the third, he stood, and walked to the window of the library, and looked out at the harbour, and saw the dead fish.

They were everywhere now. The whole harbour of Cristal was silver with them, a slow drifting carpet of dead fish from the burned quays to the harbour mouth and beyond, as far as he could see, rising and falling on a grey, sluggish swell. The Weir-folk had tried to clear them, on the first day, with nets. They had given up. There were too many. The smell came up through the broken dome of the library, sweet and rotten, and the gulls had stopped eating them; even the gulls knew something was wrong. They sat on the cliffs in their thousands, silent, watching the sea.

And under the smell, under the silence, under everything, he could feel it. All the time, every moment, like a toothache in the whole of the world. The Deep. Screaming.

It was not loud. That was the worst of it. It was getting quieter. Every hour, every day, a little fainter, a little thinner, the way a man's cries get fainter when he is bleeding to death. Three hundred miles to the east, at the bottom of the world, at the end of seven chains, a golden man was kneeling at the edge of a black pool with both his hands in the light, drinking. Not a cup a day. Everything. As fast as he could. And the sea was dying.

And as the sea died, so did Edric.

He felt that too. He reached for the sea, on the third morning, standing at the window, and it came, as it always came. But so faint. So slow. Like a voice from the bottom of a well. Like Faro's drop of water, trembling on the stone of a cell floor, barely able to rise. Whatever the King was taking from the Deep, he was taking from Edric too. Every hour, a little less of the sea in him. Every hour, the Tidemark a little fainter on his chest and arms and throat.

*He said it would take a month,* Cassia Vell had said. *And at the end of it, the sea would be dead, and the Warden would be nothing.*

Edric stood at the window and looked at the dead fish, and did the sums, the way Halloran had taught him, the way Faro had taught him. Three hundred miles to the Teeth. With a fair wind, ten days. He could not drive the *Faro* with the sea now, not as he had. There was not enough sea left in him to drive a rowing boat. Ten days, if the wind was kind. Twelve if it was not. And every day of it, the King drinking.

He would arrive at the Lock with almost nothing. And the King would arrive with everything.

He turned away from the window.

"Jac," he said. "Get the ships ready."

---

They sailed on the fourth day: the *Faro* and the *Slip*, together, east.

He had not wanted to take anyone. He had said so, in the library, on the night of the third day, to all of them: Jac with her shoulder bandaged and her arm in a sling, and Bastien with his black brows, and Hayda, and Cassia Vell in a plain grey coat that Tam had found for her, without her armour, without her sword. He had told them it was his to do. That it had always been his. That he had sworn it to an old man in a cell, and the old man was dead, and the oath was his alone.

Jac had laughed at him.

"You couldn't sail a bathtub across a pond right now," she said. "You can barely walk. You think I'm letting you go and face the bloody King of Valemont alone, at the bottom of the bloody sea, in that state? I'd never hear the end of it from Olaf." Her face changed, just for a moment, at the name, and then set. "I'm coming. That's an end of it."

"So am I," said Bastien.

"So am I," said Hayda. She did not explain. She did not need to.

And Cassia Vell, who had been sitting by the fire saying nothing, looked up and said: "You'll need me. You'll need someone who knows the Hundred. Who knows the Keep, the garrison, the way in. Who knows the King's sunfire, how it's woven, where the joins are." She looked at her hands, big and square and scarred, lying in her lap. "I've been the King's sword for six years. I know every working he's ever taught the Order. I don't know if I can break any of them. But I know where they're weakest."

Edric looked at her for a long time.

"He'll kill you," he said. "If he sees you there. With us."

"Yes," said Cassia Vell. "I expect he will." She looked into the fire. "There was a man singing in that temple. I want to be able to tell him I tried."

---

Maxim wanted to come too. He stood in the library with Lyse's hand in his and his jaw set, his father's jaw, and said he owed the Warden his life, and his father's, and Lyse's, and that he would not stay behind.

"You'll stay," said Edric. "Both of you. You'll stay here with Tam, and the council, and the people of Cristal, and you'll help them rebuild. Somebody has to." He looked at Lyse. She was still pale, still thin, but there was colour in her face now, and her grave dark eyes were steady. "And your grandfather. He'll need you."

Severin Valtor had been carried up from the *Faro* to the library on the day they arrived, in his chair, and set by the window where he could see the sea. He had sat there ever since. He did not eat much. He did not sleep much. He sat in his chair by the window with the rug over his knees and his ruined face turned towards the harbour, towards the grey water and the dead fish, and his dark eyes open.

Edric went to him on the last night, and knelt beside the chair, and picked up the board.

*G-O*, said the old man.

"I'm going."

*F-R-E-E. I-T.*

"I'll try."

The dark eyes held his for a long time.

*I. W-I-L-L. W-A-I-T.*

Edric put down the board, and took the old man's big useless hand, and held it for a while, and did not say anything.

---

They put in at Saltmere on the seventh day, because the wind failed off the Hound's Teeth and they had to wait for it, and because Edric, standing at the bow of the *Faro* in the grey dawn, looking at the white houses climbing their hill and the light tower on the harbour wall, found that he could not sail past.

He went ashore alone, in a skiff, and walked through the town. It was very quiet. The harbour was full of dead fish here too, a silver carpet of them lapping against the quays, and the fishing boats lay idle at their moorings, and people stood in doorways, silent, looking at the sea. Nobody looked at him. He wore no face; he did not have the strength for it. He was only a thin, grey, tired man in a black coat, and they did not see him.

He went to the Weir.

It was half empty now. Half the stilt-houses stood dark, their walkways sagging, their landing stages bare. The families who had lived in them had gone west, to Cristal, over the last five years, as Old Ysolde had told them to; and now their houses on Cristal were burned too. The ones who were left sat on their landing stages with their feet in the grey water, very still, listening. When he passed, they looked up, and something went across their faces: not recognition, exactly. More like the look of people who hear thunder a long way off, and know what it means.

He went to the end of the walkway. Where the little platform of old planks ran out over the open water of the channel, where Old Ysolde had sat in her chair, blind, listening, for fifty years.

There was someone sitting there.

She was sitting on the edge of the planks, with her feet in the grey water, in a plain brown dress, with a grey shawl round her shoulders, an old grey shawl that had been kept in a chest for nineteen years and never worn. Her black hair was loose down her back. There were no diamonds in it. Her face, turned towards the sea, was brown again, a little, from three weeks in the sun and the wind, and across her nose, very faint, he could see the freckles coming back.

She had heard his step on the walkway a hundred yards away, as Weir-folk always did. She did not look up.

"You're late," said Mira.

---

He sat down beside her on the edge of the planks. After a moment, he took off his boots, and put his feet in the water, the way the Weir-folk did, the oldest way of sitting.

It was cold. And grey. And very still. And under it, so faint he could barely feel it, so faint he would not have felt it at all if he had not known it was there: the screaming.

"I can hear it," said Mira, very quietly. "Every night. Since I came back. Gran always said I had the ear for it, a little. I never could, before. Not in Solace. Not in Harrowgate. But here, on the end of the walkway, at night." She did not look at him. "It's crying, Edric. The sea. It's been crying for three weeks. Every night it's quieter. Like a baby that's cried itself out." She pressed her lips together. "What's happening?"

He told her.

He told her everything. The Sunspire, and the white cup, and the King's offer, and the fleet. The Long Day. Olaf, singing. The twelve white ships set down on the Long Bank in the dark. The fish. Cassia Vell, kneeling on the shingle. The King, gone to the Teeth, kneeling at the Lock, drinking, drinking, until there was nothing left.

She listened without interrupting, with her feet in the grey water and her eyes on the sea. When he had finished, she was quiet for a long time.

"And you're going there," she said. "To the Teeth. To the Keep. Back to the place where they kept you. To fight the King."

"Yes."

"Can you win?"

He thought about lying to her. He had lied to a great many people in the last five years. He had been very good at it. But he had never been able to lie to her, not once, not when he was nineteen and not now, and he did not try.

"No," he said. "I don't think so. I'm nearly empty. He's nearly full. By the time we get there, I'll have almost nothing left, and he'll have three hundred years of the sea in him, and the Hundred around him. I don't think I can win." He looked out at the grey water. "But I have to go. I swore it. To Faro. To the sea. I can't let him drink it dry."

Mira did not say anything for a long time.

Then she reached down beside her, among the folds of the grey shawl, and brought up something wrapped in an old grey cloth, and put it in her lap. And unwrapped it.

It was the crown. The circlet of woven sea-lavender, dried and brittle and faded to the colour of dust, threaded with small white shells, two of them missing. He had given it to her six weeks ago, before the Desert Witness, in the house with the blue door. He had left it on the table in the study, and she had found it there, the night she came to him, and taken it when she left, and he had not known.

"Gran made this," said Mira. "For our wedding. Our real one." She turned it in her hands. "I gave it to her to keep, the night before I married Fenrick. I told her to give it to you if you came back, and to the sea if you didn't." She looked down at it. "You brought it back to me. I've been sitting here every night for three weeks with it in my lap, trying to decide which."

He could not speak.

"I'm coming with you," said Mira.

"No."

"I'm coming with you," she said again, quite calmly, the way she used to say things on the landing stage when she was nineteen and had already decided. "Aren too. He's been working the boats since we came back, and he's good, Edric, he's very good, he's got your hands for a line, I don't know where he got them, not from his father. He'll want to come. He'll want to do something." She looked at him, at last, with her dark green eyes, the colour of deep water in shade. "I sat on that landing stage every spring tide for two years, watching the light on the Teeth, not knowing you were under it. I'm not going to sit here on the end of this walkway and watch it again." She wrapped the crown back up in its grey cloth. "Besides. You'll need someone who can hear the sea. When yours is gone."

---

So there were nine of them, in the end, in the *Faro*'s longboat, in the dark of the moon on the twelfth day, rowing towards the Teeth.

Edric in the bow. Jac at the tiller, one-armed, cursing softly under her breath. Bastien and Aren Morrow at the oars, side by side, pulling together, the black-browed man who had hated Corvan Valtor for twenty-five years and the boy whose father had died by his own sword in a study in Solace, and neither of them said a word to the other the whole way, and neither of them missed a stroke. Hayda in the waist, with Bastien's long knife across her knees. Cassia Vell beside her, in her white and gold armour again, which she had brought from Cristal in a sea-chest, with her sword on her hip and her big scarred hands folded in her lap, very still, as if she were praying. Mira, in the stern beside Jac, with her grey shawl round her shoulders and something wrapped in grey cloth in her lap. And two of the *Faro*'s crew, Sardhi brothers who had once sailed with Jac on the *Slip* and had come back to her, to row the boat home if anyone was left to row it.

The Teeth rose out of the sea ahead of them, black against the black sky.

He had not seen them for six years. Not since the night he had come up out of the water beside them, laughing, and turned his back on them, and let the sea carry him away. He had thought, sometimes, in the years since, about what it would be like to see them again. He had thought it would be terrible.

It was terrible. But not in the way he had thought.

The rock was the same: a great black mountain rising out of the sea, its cliffs falling sheer into the water, and on its summit the walls, and the tower, and on the tower the red light that had burned there for three hundred years. But the sea around it was not the same.

It was dead.

There was no other word for it. The water around the Teeth was grey and still and flat as a sheet of slate, for a mile in every direction, without a ripple, without a swell, without a sound. No waves broke at the foot of the cliffs. No spray rose. The longboat slid across it as if across oil, and the oars, when they came out of the water, came out slick and grey and smelling of rot. There were no fish, not even dead ones. There was nothing at all.

And down through the grey water, as they came closer to the cliffs, he could see them.

The chains. Seven lines of gold light, going down through the water from the foot of the rock, taut and burning. He had seen them before only in the temple, in the dark, under the Keep. He had never seen them from above. They were so bright now that they lit the water from beneath, a fierce shimmering gold glow coming up out of the depths, so bright he could see the flank of the mountain going down beside them, far down, into the dark. And they were not shuddering any more. Not heaving. Not *booming*, every few heartbeats, the way they had heaved against the floor of his cell for fourteen years.

They were still.

Whatever had been heaving against them, for three hundred years, did not have the strength to heave any more.

---

"There," said Cassia Vell, very low, and pointed.

At the foot of the cliffs on the south side of the Teeth, where the rock came down to meet the water, there was an arch: a great opening in the rock, half submerged, like the mouth of a tunnel. And across it, from the top of the arch down into the water, bars of gold light, a dozen of them, a hand's width apart, burning. The sea-gate. The way into the temple, in the old days, when the Tidewardens came by boat at low tide to listen to their god.

He had looked at it from the inside, twelve years ago, with Faro sitting on the ledge beside him weeping. *Nothing passes it. Not a fish, not a man. Touch it and it'll cook you like a crab.*

"The Hundred will be inside," said Cassia. "In the temple. Around him. The garrison of the Keep is on the walls above; they won't come down, they never come down, they're not allowed. The King doesn't trust them." She looked at the burning bars. "Nobody's watching the gate. Nobody needs to. Nothing can open it but the King."

"Can you?" said Edric.

Cassia Vell looked at the gate for a long time.

"No," she said. "Not open it. It's his. His own working, the oldest there is, the same as the chains. I can't unmake it." She held up her big scarred hands. "But every working has joins. Places where the threads cross. I've been studying his workings for twenty-two years, Warden. I know where his joins are. I can't open the gate. But I can take hold of it, at a join, with my own fire, and hold it. Bend it. A little. For a moment." She was quiet. "Long enough for a boat, maybe."

"And you?"

"It'll burn me," said Cassia Vell simply. "His fire against mine. His is stronger. It'll burn me, through my hands, into me. I don't know how badly." She looked at him. "I know what I'm doing, Warden. I chose."

Edric looked at her for a long time, at her plain square face, and her pale eyes, steady in the gold glow coming up out of the water.

"Thank you," he said.

---

They rowed the longboat up to the gate, as close as they dared, until the heat of the bars was like standing at the mouth of an oven, and the dead grey water around them steamed, very faintly.

Cassia Vell stood up in the bow, beside Edric, in her white and gold armour. She closed her eyes. She held out her hands.

Light came into them. Edric had seen sunfire before, from the Radiants on the stair at Cristal, from the mirrors on the white ships, from the shaft in the Sunspire. But he had never seen it come into someone's hands this close: gold light, gathering in her palms like water filling a cup, brighter and brighter, until her hands were two small suns, and he had to look away, and the Tidemark on his chest flared cold against it, and he gritted his teeth.

She reached out, with her eyes closed, and took hold of two of the bars of the gate, at a place near the waterline where, Edric saw, if he looked very closely, the gold light of the bars was not quite even: a knot, a crossing, a join.

There was a sound like a forge when water is thrown on it. A hiss, and a roar. Light exploded off the gate, gold and white, and the dead water around the boat boiled. Cassia Vell made a sound, a small, sharp sound, through her teeth. Her face went white. But she did not let go.

And slowly, slowly, with a sound like iron being bent in a smithy, the two bars began to part.

They bent apart at the join, the way two bars of an iron gate bend when a strong man pulls them. A hand's width. Two. A foot. Cassia's arms were shaking. Smoke was rising from her gauntlets. Her face was running with sweat and something else, tears or blood, Edric could not tell in the light. Three feet. Four.

"*Now*," said Cassia Vell, through her teeth. "Now. Go."

Bastien and Aren pulled. The longboat slid forward, into the gap, between the bent bars, with the heat of them on either side like two open furnaces. Edric felt it scald him through his coat. He heard Mira gasp behind him. He heard the wood of the boat's gunwales begin to char and smoke. And then they were through, into the dark of the tunnel beyond, and the heat was behind them.

Behind them, Cassia Vell let go.

The bars snapped back together with a sound like a bell, and she fell backwards into the bottom of the boat, and lay there, with her hands held up in front of her. They were black. Her gauntlets had fused to them. Smoke rose from them in thin grey threads.

Hayda was beside her at once, kneeling, with water from the cask. Cassia shook her head.

"It doesn't matter," she said. Her voice was quite steady, though her face was grey. "It doesn't matter. Go on. He'll have felt that. Go on."

---

The tunnel went in under the rock for a hundred yards, low and black and dripping, and the gold glow grew brighter ahead of them, and the air grew warmer, and the sharp strange lightning smell came to meet them. And then the walls of the tunnel opened out, and they came out of it into the temple.

He had last seen it with Faro, twelve years ago, by the light of the chains: the vast cave, the ribbed and fluted walls like the inside of a shell, the tiers of ledges stepping down to the black water, the carvings of the Tidewardens with their hands raised to the sea. The rope still hung from the hole in the roof, he saw, far above, the rope they had plaited from their blankets, rotted now to a few grey threads.

It was not dark any more. It was blazing.

The chains filled it with light: seven lines of gold coming down out of the roof and plunging into the water in the middle of the cave, so bright now that they lit every corner, every carving, every ledge, like a cathedral at noon. The black water was not black. It was grey, and still, and dead, and lit from within by the gold going down through it into the depths. And it was lower. Much lower than he remembered. The water had gone down, Edric saw, ten feet, twenty, as if something underneath it had shrunk; the lowest ledges, that had been at the waterline when he knelt there twelve years ago and reached out his hand, stood high and dry, crusted with salt.

On the ledges, all round the cave, in a great ring, stood the Hundred.

They were old. That was the first thing he noticed. Not old the way the King was old, hidden behind beauty. Old the way men are old: grey, and lined, and stooped, in white and gold armour that hung on them. The oldest knights of the Radiant Order, the ones who never left the Sunspire, the ones who had stood at the King's side for fifty years, sixty, seventy. They stood on the ledges all round the cave with their hands held out towards the water in the centre, and sunfire burning in their palms, and the light going out from their hands to the chains, feeding them. Holding them. A hundred small suns, in a ring, around a pool of dead grey water.

And on the lowest ledge, at the very edge of the pool, a figure knelt.

It was kneeling with its back to them, with both hands plunged into the light where the chains entered the water. It was golden. It was blazing. It was so bright that it was hard to look at: brighter than the chains, brighter than the Hundred, brighter than the noon sun, a figure of pure gold light kneeling at the edge of the dead water, drinking. And Edric felt it drink. He felt it through the chains, through the water, through the rock, through the soles of his feet: a vast steady draining, like the tide going out of the whole world at once. And far below, at the bottom of the chains, in the dark, something so faint now that he could barely feel it at all.

Not screaming. Not any more. Only a whisper. A breath. A heartbeat, very slow, very far apart.

The golden figure lifted its head.

It took its hands out of the light, slowly, and stood up, on the lowest ledge, at the edge of the pool. And turned.

It was the King. It was still the King: the fine bones, the grave gentle face, the face of a young saint in a temple painting. But it was so much more now. The gold had soaked through him. It was in his skin, his hair, his eyes, blazing out of him, the way the Tidemark had blazed out of Edric in the stillroom of the grey house. He looked like a statue of the Sun come to life. He looked like a god.

He looked across the cave, across the dead grey water, at the longboat coming out of the tunnel mouth, at the nine people in it, at the thin grey tired man standing in the bow. And he smiled: the grave, gentle, beautiful smile, the smile of a host whose guest has finally arrived.

"Edric," said Aurelian, Eternal. His voice filled the cave like music. "You came. I hoped you would."
