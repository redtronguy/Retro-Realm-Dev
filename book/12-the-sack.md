## Chapter 12: The Sack

Faro lived for five more years.

He got back some of what the first stroke took. Within a month, his face had mostly come back to itself, and he could talk again, though some words came slowly, and he would stop in the middle of a sentence and frown and tap his temple as if knocking on a door to see who was home. His left leg came back enough that he could limp about his cell with one hand on the wall. But his left arm never came back at all. It hung at his side, thin and useless, and he carried it around like a parcel he had been asked to look after for someone else.

The digging stopped. There was no point in it now. The tunnel ended in a hole above a temple with a gate of fire across its door, and there was no other way. They both knew it, and neither of them said it, and for a while they simply did not go down the tunnel at all.

Instead, Faro taught.

He taught as if the stroke had lit a fire under him. Everything, all at once, faster than before, as if he were trying to pour himself into Edric before the vessel cracked. He taught the way to the Isle of Cristal, every rock and current and landmark, over and over, until Edric could close his eyes and sail it in his head from Saltmere to the hook-shaped harbour without a single wrong turn. He taught the way through the ruined citadel to the sea-cave, and the marks on the walls that showed the path, and the words in the old tongue that were carved over the door. He taught the histories of the noble houses of Valemont, which ones were rich and which were pretending to be, which ones owed money to whom, who had married whom and why. He taught the workings of the Chamber of Lords, and the Ministry of Justice, and the Treasury, and the great merchant banks of Solace, and how money moved through all of them like blood through a body.

"Why do I need to know all this?" Edric asked, once.

"Because when you get out," said Faro, "you're going to go after four men who've had fourteen years to climb. And men like that don't stay where you left them. They rise. They'll be in places you've never been and don't understand. And you'll need to understand them better than they understand themselves." He tapped his temple. "I can't give you a sword. I can give you this."

And he taught Tidecraft.

---

"There are three disciplines," Faro said, on a grey day in the tenth year, when the light through the slit was the colour of pewter and the sea was quiet. "Three ways a Tidewarden can work with the water. Every Warden could do a little of all three, in the old days. Most were strong in only one. The very greatest were strong in two. My mother said that in the whole history of the order, there were only five who were strong in all three, and every one of them was remembered for a thousand years."

He held up one thin finger.

"The first is called Riptide. It's the simplest. Force. Moving water. A wave, a current, a spout. The water in your body, too: a Warden strong in Riptide could make himself fast, and strong, stronger than any man, by moving his own blood. In the old days, the Riptide Wardens were the ones who turned back storms and pulled ships off reefs. And in the Drying, they were the ones who fought." He was quiet a moment. "They were very terrible, when they fought. That was how the Radiants justified the Drying, afterwards. They pointed at what the Riptide Wardens did in the last battles."

A second finger.

"The second is called Undertow. That's subtler. Undertow is pull. Currents. A Warden strong in Undertow could feel every current in the sea for a hundred miles: where the fish were running, where a storm was building, where a ship was going down. But it's not only water. All things flow, Edric. Money flows. Rumour flows. Fear flows, and desire, and debt, from person to person, through a city, like a tide through a harbour. An Undertow Warden could feel those currents too, if they were strong enough. Could feel where the money in a market was going before it went. Could feel what a man wanted, underneath what he said. And could pull. Draw things down. Drain a well. Sink a ship. Drag a man under." A pause. "The Undertow Wardens were the advisers, in the old days. The counsellors. Every king in the world wanted one."

A third finger.

"And the third is called Still Water. That's the rarest, and the strangest. Still Water is reflection. When the water is perfectly still, you can see yourself in it. Or anything. A Still Water Warden could make their own face into a still pool, so that whoever looked at them saw only what they expected to see. A servant. A stranger. A friend. They could look into a bowl of water and see things far away. They could hear the lie in a man's voice, because a lie makes ripples. And they could make a stillness in someone else: a sleep so deep it looked like death, and wake them from it, days later, unharmed." Faro lowered his hand. "My grandmother was strong in Still Water. She used to frighten me half to death when I was small, turning up at the dinner table wearing my mother's face."

"Which were you strong in?" said Edric.

"Undertow," said Faro. "A little. Once. It's how I knew so much about people, before I ever came here." He smiled faintly. "Not that it helped me much in the archives."

"And which will I be strong in?"

Faro looked at him for a long time.

"I don't know," he said. "With the Deep chained, I don't know if you'll ever be strong in any of them. You can raise a drop now. In a year, perhaps a cupful. In ten years, perhaps you'll be able to do what I could do at forty: sense the weather, find a spring, calm a sick man's fever. That's all. That's the most any of us can do now, the most any of us have been able to do for three hundred years." He hesitated. "But I'll tell you something strange. When I had my stroke, on the rope, and you pulled me up. The water in the temple rose. All of it. The whole cave. I felt it."

"I felt it too."

"I've never seen anything like it. Not in thirty-eight years." Faro shook his head slowly. "I don't know what it means. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps you were frightened, and fear is like anger, and it reached the Deep. Perhaps the Deep was simply heaving against its chains, as it always does, and it was chance." He looked at Edric with his very blue eyes. "Or perhaps not. Perhaps it was listening. And perhaps, for a moment, it decided to help."

---

The second stroke came in the twelfth year, in the middle of a lesson.

Edric was reading aloud, from the wall, a passage of High Sardhi poetry about a man who crossed the desert to find a well his father had told him about, and found, when he got there, that the well was dry, and sat beside it and wept, and his tears filled it. He finished the passage and looked up, waiting for Faro to correct his accent. And the old man was lying on his side on the pallet, with his eyes open, not moving.

It was worse than the first. Much worse. When he came back, two days later, it was slowly, and not all the way. He could barely speak. He could not walk at all. He lay on his pallet, and Edric fed him porridge one spoonful at a time and held the cup to his lips, and wiped his face, and moved his body every few hours so the sores would not come. He did it the way his father had cared for his mother, in the last winter, when Edric was very small: patiently, gently, without thinking about it, because there was nothing else to do.

Faro hated it. He would lie there with his good eye blazing and make a sound in his throat, a furious rasp, and try to push the spoon away. And Edric would wait, and then try again.

"Stop," Faro said, one night, weeks after. It came out slow and thick, each word dragged up from somewhere deep. "Stop. Wasting. Time."

"It's not wasted."

"Go. Get out. Without me."

"There's no way out, Faro. You know that. The tunnel ends at the gate."

"Then. Find. Another." The good hand reached out and found Edric's wrist and gripped it, with a strength that was astonishing in a body so wasted. "Don't. Wait. For me. I won't. Be long."

"Don't say that."

"*True*," said Faro, with terrible effort. "Third one. Soon. I can. Feel it. Coming. Like weather." The grip tightened. "When it comes. Don't grieve. Too long. Go on. Live. Do. The work."

Edric could not answer. He held the old man's hand in both of his and bowed his head over it.

After a long time, Faro spoke again. His voice was very faint, but clearer, as if he had gathered all his strength for it.

"Do you know," he said, "what my mother told me. When I was a boy. All the wisdom. Of the Tidewardens. A thousand years of it. All the books. She said. It comes down. To two words."

"What words?"

The good side of Faro's mouth lifted, very slightly, in something like his old smile.

"Wait," he said. "And hope."

---

The third stroke came in the spring of the thirteenth year, at night, while Edric was asleep in his own cell.

He did not hear it. There was nothing to hear. He woke in the grey dawn and crawled through the tunnel, as he did every morning, with a cup of water in one hand, and came out behind the pallet, and knew at once. The cell was too quiet. The breathing was gone.

Faro lay on his back on the pallet with his eyes closed and his good hand resting on his chest. His face was smooth and peaceful, both sides of it, as if whatever had pulled it out of shape for five years had finally let go. He looked younger. He looked, Edric thought, like the man he must have been at forty, clever and proud and brave, walking into the archives under the Sunspire in the dark.

Edric sat down on the floor beside the pallet. He did not cry. He had thought he would; he had dreaded it, for months, the moment and the grief. But it did not come. There was only a great, cold, still emptiness, like the sea on a windless night, stretching away in every direction to the edge of the world.

He sat there for a long time, holding the old man's cold hand.

Then he heard the guard's feet on the stairs, for the morning porridge, and he put the hand down, and crawled back through the tunnel, and put the block back in the wall, and lay on his pallet with his face to the stone. He heard the guard push the bowl through Faro's slot, and walk on, and push the bowl through his own. He heard the guard go away.

At dusk, the guard came again with the evening porridge, and found the morning bowl still full in the slot, and opened the door.

---

They did not take the body away at once.

Edric lay against the wall at the head of his pallet with his ear to the stone and listened to all of it. He heard the guard's lantern swing in the doorway. He heard the guard swear, and call down the corridor, and another guard come. He heard them grunting and talking as they moved Faro off the pallet, and he heard what they said.

"About time. Thirty-eight years. Must have been the oldest thing in the place."

"Governor'll be glad. One less number."

"Get the sack. And a weight. Doesn't need much, there's nothing to him."

"Leave it till night, then. Throw him off at the change of watch. I'm not carrying a corpse up four hundred stairs on an empty stomach."

He heard them bring the sack: the rough rustle of sailcloth, the clank of an iron weight. He heard them lift the body, and drop it in, and the scrape of a needle as one of them sewed up the end with big, quick, careless stitches. He heard them drop it on the floor of the cell with a thump. And he heard them go out, and the door close, and the key turn.

*At the change of watch.* Midnight. Four or five hours.

He lay very still, with his cheek against the stone, and listened to the sea booming beneath the floor.

*When it comes,* Faro had said. *Don't grieve too long. Go on. Live. Find another way.*

*Throw him off at the change of watch.*

*The sea is the only grave the Keep allows.*

Edric sat up.

---

He worked faster than he had ever worked in his life.

He took the block out of the wall and crawled through the tunnel for the last time. He came out behind Faro's pallet into the dark cell, and found the sack by feel, lying in the middle of the floor. It was a long, heavy sailcloth bag, sewn shut at one end. The iron weight, a ball the size of a man's head, had been tied to the outside with a short length of rope, around what must be the feet.

With Faro's chisel, he cut the stitches. He was careful: he cut each one cleanly, so that the thread came out in one piece. He opened the sack, and reached in, and lifted the old man out.

There was nothing to him. Faro had always been thin; at the end, he had weighed no more than a child. Edric held him for a moment in his arms in the dark, the way he had held him on the rope, in the tunnel, five years ago.

"I'm sorry," he whispered. "I'm sorry, Faro. You'll be in my bed. You won't mind. You always said my pallet was softer."

He carried the body back through the tunnel. It was the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than the digging, harder than the years: forty yards on his belly in the dark, dragging the body of the only friend he had ever really had. He laid Faro on his own pallet, in his own cell, with his face to the wall and the blanket pulled up over his head, the way Edric always slept. In the dark, through a slot at the bottom of a door, it would pass. For a day, perhaps two. Long enough.

He kissed the old man's forehead. He put the chisel in his belt. Then he crawled back through the tunnel, and pushed the block back into the wall from Faro's side, and smeared the joints with damp grit until they looked as they always had. If anyone ever looked closely, they would find it. But no one had looked closely at anything in the Drowned Keep for three hundred years.

Then he went to the sack, and climbed inside it.

---

He had kept a needle. Faro had made it years ago, from a fish bone in a rare bowl of soup, and kept it in the hem of his robe for mending; Edric had taken it from the hem before he moved the body. He had the guard's thread, all of it, unpicked in one long piece. Lying inside the sack, in the absolute dark, with the rough sailcloth against his face, he pulled the open end closed over his head and sewed it shut again from the inside: big, quick, careless stitches, through the same holes, as near as he could feel them. It was clumsy work. He pricked his fingers a dozen times. But when it was done, it was done, and he lay on the floor of the dead man's cell, sewn into a dead man's shroud, with an iron ball tied at his feet, and waited.

He did not know how long he waited. Hours. He lay perfectly still, as Faro had taught him, breathing in for eight and out for eight. He made himself go over the way to the Isle of Cristal, every turn, every rock. He made himself think about the water: how cold it would be, how deep, how far from the foot of the cliffs to the nearest shore. He made himself think about the knife.

He had Faro's chisel in his right hand, held flat against his thigh. When he hit the water, he would have perhaps a minute before the weight dragged him too deep. In that minute, he would have to cut his way out of the sack, and then cut the rope that tied the ball to it, and then swim up. In the dark, in the cold, with the breath knocked out of him by the fall.

He did not let himself think about how high the cliffs were.

---

At midnight, the key turned in the lock.

He heard the door open, and the lantern, and two men's voices, low and bored. He heard them come in, and stand over him.

"Light, isn't he?"

"Told you. Nothing to him."

"He feels heavier than this morning."

"It's the weight. Get his feet."

Hands seized him through the sailcloth, at the shoulders and the knees, and lifted. He made himself go limp. He made himself be dead. They carried him out of the cell, and along the corridor, and to the foot of the stair, and up.

Four hundred stairs. He counted every one. He felt the air change as they climbed, from the warm wet air of the depths to the cooler air of the upper tower, and then, at the very top, as a door opened, to the cold. The wind. Real wind, sea wind, on the other side of the sailcloth, for the first time in thirteen years. It came through the weave and touched his face like a hand, and smelled of salt and night and open water, and he nearly wept.

They carried him across a stone courtyard, slipping on the wet. He heard the sea now, not under him but all around him, the roar and crash of waves against rock, very loud, very close. He heard the wind howling. They stopped.

"Here?"

"Here. Usual place."

"Gods, it's a long way down tonight. Tide's out. Rocks."

"He won't mind."

They swung him. Once, back, and forward. The wind rushed past the sailcloth.

"One."

Twice.

"Two."

Edric tightened his grip on the chisel. *Wait,* he thought. *And hope.*

"Three."

And they let go, and he fell.

He fell for a long time. Longer than he had believed possible. The wind screamed past him, and the sailcloth flapped and cracked around his face, and the iron ball at his feet dragged him down, feet first, faster and faster, through the dark, and he could not breathe, and he could not think, and there was nothing in the world but falling.

Then the sea struck him like a wall, and he went into it, and down.
