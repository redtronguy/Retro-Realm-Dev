## Chapter 7: The Drowned Keep

The governor of the Drowned Keep was a fat, grey, wheezing man named Ostric Pell, who had held the post for twenty-two years and who had long ago stopped thinking of the men in his charge as anything other than numbers in a ledger. He met the boat at the top of the landing stairs, read the Sun Warrant with his lips moving, and looked Edric up and down with no more interest than a farmer looking at a calf.

"Treason," he said. "No trial. Natural life." He folded the warrant and put it inside his coat. "Number four hundred and eleven. Lower east. Put him in the empty one, next to the mad priest."

"I want to see the magistrate," said Edric. His head was splitting where they had struck him; there was dried blood stiff in his hair, and his voice came out cracked and strange. "I want to see Magistrate Valtor. There's been a mistake. He said he would clear my name. He burned the letter. Ask him. Send to him and ask him."

The governor looked at him for a moment longer.

"They all say that," he said, not unkindly, and turned and went back inside, out of the wind.

---

The Drowned Keep had been built, people said, in the first years of the Sun King's reign, before there was any town at Saltmere at all. No one knew who had built it, or why they had chosen the Teeth, which was a barren, wave-lashed spur of black rock half a day's sail from the coast, where nothing grew and the gulls would not nest. Some said it had been a watchtower once, against pirates. Some said it had been a temple, to some god that nobody remembered. Some said the King had raised it in a single night with sunfire, during the war against the Drowned Heresy, and that the stones were fused together so tightly that no tool could ever chip them.

Edric did not know any of this, then. He only knew that it was very old, and very large, and very cold, and that it went down.

They took him down. Through a gate in the outer wall, across a courtyard where nothing grew, through a second gate into the tower itself, and then down: down a spiral stair worn hollow in the middle by centuries of feet, past landings where iron doors stood shut on either side, down and down, until the light from the courtyard was gone and there was only the guard's lantern, swinging, and the stone, and the sound of their footsteps, and a sound underneath the footsteps that Edric did not at first recognise.

It was the sea. He could hear the sea. Not outside, not beyond the walls, but under them. Under his feet. A deep, slow, booming rhythm, like a great heart, like a vast slow breath, coming up through the stone.

"The lower levels go under the waterline," said the guard, a squat, scarred man with a ring of keys at his belt, when Edric asked. He seemed to find the question funny. "Down here you're under the sea, my lad. Twenty feet of rock and then fathoms of water. You'll hear it all day and all night, for as long as you live. Some of them go mad from it." He grinned. "You'll get used to it. Or you won't."

They stopped at last on a landing where the stone walls sweated and dripped, and the lantern light showed a long low corridor running away into darkness, with iron doors set into its walls at intervals. The guard walked along it, counting, and stopped at one, and unlocked it with a key from his ring, and swung it open on a darkness so complete that the lantern light seemed to go into it and not come back.

"Lower east," he said. "Number four hundred and eleven. In you go."

---

The cell was eight paces long and five paces wide. Edric learned that in the first hour, walking it in the dark with his hands on the walls. Eight paces. Five paces. A stone floor, slick and cold. Stone walls, wet. A stone ceiling, too low for him to stand straight under at one end. A wooden pallet against one wall with a mouldy straw mattress on it. A bucket in the corner. An iron door with a slot at the bottom for food. High up in the far wall, so high that he could only touch it by standing on the pallet and stretching, a single narrow slit, no wider than his hand, through which came, at certain hours, a thin grey light, and the sound of the wind, and sometimes, when the waves were high, a spatter of salt spray.

That was all. That was the world.

He did not believe it, at first. For the first day, and the second, and the third, he did not believe it. He sat on the pallet and waited for the door to open and someone to come in and say *there has been a mistake, Master Vane, you're free to go.* He rehearsed what he would say to them. He rehearsed what he would say to Mira. He thought about the magistrate, who had looked at him with such careful eyes and burned the letter and called it a kindness, and he told himself that the magistrate was a good man, that there had been some error in the paperwork, that the magistrate had been called away, that the governor had misread the warrant. He told himself a hundred stories, and he believed each one of them for a few hours, and then stopped believing it, and told himself another.

When the guard came with his food, a bowl of grey porridge and a cup of water pushed through the slot at the bottom of the door twice a day, Edric threw himself at the door and shouted through the slot. He asked for the governor. He asked for the magistrate. He asked for Master Moren, for his father, for anyone. He told the guard the whole story, the anonymous letter, the dead captain's packet, the magistrate's fire. He begged the guard to send word to Saltmere, just a single line, just to tell them where he was.

The guard listened, sometimes. Sometimes he laughed. Once he said, quite kindly: "Lad, everyone on this landing has a story like yours. Every man in this keep is innocent, if you ask him. The governor doesn't care, and the magistrate doesn't care, and the King, gods bless him, has never heard your name and never will. Eat your porridge. It's the only thing in this place that'll do you any good."

---

On the eighth day, he asked to see the governor, and to his astonishment, the governor came.

It was the annual inspection, the guard told him afterwards; once a year, by law, the governor had to walk every corridor and look into every cell and ask every prisoner if he had any complaint. It was a formality, like everything else in the Keep. The governor did it as quickly as he could, with a handkerchief pressed to his nose against the smell.

The door opened, and the lantern light came in, and behind it the governor's grey, bored face.

"Number four hundred and eleven," said the governor. "Any complaints?"

Edric was on his feet. He had been waiting for this for eight days, rehearsing it, and now that it had come he found that his voice shook, and he made it stop.

"Sir," he said. "My name is Edric Vane. I'm a sea-captain of Saltmere, in the service of Master Pieter Moren. I was arrested on the night of my wedding on a false charge of treason, made in an anonymous letter. I was examined by the deputy Crown Magistrate, Corvan Valtor, who told me he believed me innocent, and who promised to clear my name. And then I was brought here, without a trial, without being charged, without being told why. I don't ask to be set free. I only ask to be tried. Let me stand in front of a court, any court, and answer the charge. If I'm found guilty, hang me. But let me be tried."

The governor looked at him for a long moment, with something that might, in a better man, have been pity.

"You were taken under a Sun Warrant," he said. "Do you know what that means?"

"No, sir."

"It means there will be no trial. Not now. Not ever. It means the King himself, through his magistrate, has decided that you are too dangerous to be tried. It means your name is not written in any court roll in Valemont. As far as the law is concerned, you were never arrested, because you do not exist." He shrugged. "I can't help you, four hundred and eleven. Nobody can. The only man who could release you is the one who signed the warrant, and if he'd wanted you released, he wouldn't have signed it. I'm sorry."

"Then write to him," said Edric. "Please. Write to the magistrate. Ask him why. Just ask."

The governor sighed. "I'll make a note of it," he said, and the door swung shut, and the lantern light went away, and Edric was alone in the dark.

He found out much later that the governor had, in fact, made a note of it, in the margin of the prison ledger, beside the number 411: *Claims innocence. Asks to see the magistrate.* It was the same note that was written beside nearly every number in the book.

---

The days began to run together.

He kept count, at first. He scratched a mark on the wall by the pallet every morning when the grey light came through the slit. Ten marks. Twenty. Thirty. He measured his life by the porridge coming through the slot and the light coming through the window and the sea, booming, booming, under the floor.

He talked to himself, to keep from going mad. He talked to Mira, out loud, in the dark, telling her about his day, which was always the same. He talked to his father. He went over every moment of the Night of Lamps, again and again, trying to understand. Who had written the letter? Who hated him enough? He went through every face in the Three Anchors and could not find one. He had no enemies. He had never hurt anyone.

He thought about Fenrick's face at the door: white, and wet, and frightened. He thought about it a great deal. But Fenrick had come to apologise. Fenrick had shaken his hand. And Fenrick had not known about the letter. Nobody had known about the letter, except his father.

He thought about the magistrate. That was the hardest thing, the thing he kept coming back to, like a tongue to a broken tooth. The magistrate had believed him. Edric had seen it in his face. The magistrate had read the letter, the dead captain's letter, and gone white, and put it in the fire, and called it a kindness. And then he had sent Edric here, to the Drowned Keep, forever, without a trial.

*Why?*

He did not know. He could not imagine. And not knowing was worse than the dark, worse than the cold, worse than the sea.

---

By the hundredth mark, he had stopped shouting through the slot.

By the two hundredth, he had stopped talking to Mira, because it had begun to hurt too much to hear his own voice say her name and get no answer.

He began to think, instead, about how long a man could live in a place like this. He did the sums, the way Halloran had taught him to do sums for navigation. He was nineteen. Twenty, now: his birthday had come and gone, marked by nothing but another scratch on the wall. If he lived to be sixty, he would spend forty years in this cell. Fourteen thousand days. Twenty-eight thousand bowls of porridge. He could not make the numbers mean anything. They were too large, like the distance to the stars.

He thought about Mira waiting. He thought about how long she would wait. A year? Two? And then her mother would get sicker, in the damp of the Weir, and there would be no house on the hill, no fire in every room, and someone would come along, someone kind, someone with money, and she would have to choose. And he would want her to choose it. He would want her warm, and safe, and her mother well. He made himself want it. It was the hardest thing he had ever done.

He thought about his father, alone in the rooms on Ropewalk Lane, with the iron-bound box of wages that were supposed to be for the wedding. He hoped the old man would spend them now. He hoped he would buy coal, and meat, and keep a fire. He prayed it, at night, to the Sun, to whatever gods there were, to the sea booming under the floor. *Let him spend the money. Let him eat.* It was the only prayer he had left.

---

By the three hundredth mark, he had stopped scratching marks on the wall.

He lay on the pallet most of the day now, with his face to the stone. The porridge came through the slot, and sometimes he ate it, and more often he did not. The guard stopped bothering to talk to him. The grey light came and went. The sea boomed.

One day, he did not know which, he realised that he had stopped hoping. It was not a decision. It was more like a tide going out: he simply looked one day for the place in himself where the hope had been, and found it empty, wet sand and stranded weed, and the water a long way off and still retreating.

He found that he did not much mind. It was easier, without hope. Hope was the thing that hurt.

And then, on a day when the grey light had not come at all, because a storm was blowing outside and the slit in the wall showed nothing but darkness and flung spray, he lay on the pallet and listened to the sea booming under the floor, louder than he had ever heard it, booming and booming like a great fist on a door, and he thought, quite calmly, quite clearly, as if it were a sum in navigation:

*I could stop eating.*

He thought about it for a long time. It would not be difficult. He had barely been eating anyway. It would take, he thought, a few weeks. It would be quiet. Nobody would notice. Nobody would stop him. And at the end of it, the guards would sew him into a sack and carry him up all those stairs into the air, and throw him off the cliff into the sea, and the sea would take him, and that would be the end. He would be in the sea. He had always loved the sea. It seemed a better grave than this.

The next morning, when the porridge came through the slot, he pushed it back.

---

On the fourth day without food, the hunger stopped hurting, and he felt light, and clear, and strangely peaceful.

On the sixth day, he could no longer stand up, and the walls of the cell began to move, very slightly, when he looked at them.

On the eighth day, he was lying on his back on the pallet in the dark, drifting in and out of something that was not quite sleep, listening to the sea, when he heard another sound under it.

It was very faint. He thought at first he was dreaming it. A scratching. A small, patient, regular scratching, like a mouse in a wall. Then a pause. Then the scratching again. *Scrape. Scrape. Scrape.* Then a pause.

He lay and listened to it, and did not understand it, and did not much care. It was a rat, he thought. Rats in the walls. It would stop.

It did not stop. It went on all that day and into the night. *Scrape. Scrape. Scrape.* Pause. It was coming from the wall at the head of his pallet, the wall he shared with the next cell along. The cell of the mad priest, the governor had said, on his first day; Edric had heard nothing from it in all this time, not a voice, not a cough, nothing.

*Scrape. Scrape. Scrape.*

And then, very faintly, so faintly he was sure he had imagined it, a voice. An old man's voice, muffled by the stone, muttering to itself, irritable, and precise.

"*Wrong.* Wrong, wrong, wrong. Thirty-one degrees, I said, not thirty-four, you fool, you blind old fool. Now look. Now we'll have to go round."

Edric lay in the dark, and turned his head very slowly towards the wall.

It was the first human voice, other than the guard's, that he had heard in more than a year, and it was talking about degrees. Angles. Measurements. The way a navigator talks. The way Halloran used to talk, bent over a chart.

Something moved in him, then, at the bottom of the empty place where the hope had been: something small and stubborn and very nearly dead, which lifted its head.

The next morning, when the guard pushed the bowl of grey porridge through the slot in the door, Edric Vane dragged himself off the pallet, and crawled across the floor, and ate it.
