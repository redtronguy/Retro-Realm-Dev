## Chapter 15: The Isle of Cristal

They raised it on the ninth day out of Corrow, in the grey hour before dawn: a darker grey on the grey sea, low at first, then rising, as the light came, into a great humped shape of rock with cliffs that went straight down into the water on every side, white with the droppings of a hundred thousand seabirds. Cloud sat on its summit like a cap. And under the cloud, as the sun came up behind them and lit the island gold, they saw the ruins.

It had been a citadel. Even from three miles out, even broken, you could see that. Walls ran along the cliff tops and down into the clefts between them, walls of a pale stone that glittered faintly in the sun, as if there were crystals in it. There were towers, or the stumps of towers, rising in tiers up the slope of the island to its peak. There were great arched gateways with no gates in them, and long colonnades open to the sky, and on the highest point of all, under the cloud, the shell of a vast domed building whose dome had fallen in, leaving only a ring of pillars standing against the sky like the ribs of a whale.

Nobody on the *Slip* spoke. Even Jac, who had an opinion on everything, stood at the rail with her mouth slightly open.

"Gods below," said Big Olaf at last, in a whisper. "Who built that?"

"Tidewardens," said Faro.

He was standing in the bow. He had been standing there since the island first rose out of the sea, and he had not moved. He had sailed this way a thousand times, in his head, in the dark of the Keep, with Faro's thin finger tracing the route on the wet stone floor: *past Hook Point, then due west for two days, then north-west until you see the Bird Rocks, and then the island is in front of you, and you'll know it, Edric, you'll know it the moment you see it.*

He knew it. He felt it, through the water, before he saw it: something about the sea around the island that was different. Deeper. Older. It was like coming into a church.

"There," he said, and pointed. "On the south side. A harbour, shaped like a hook. You'll see it in a moment, when we come round the point. There's a channel in, between two reefs. Very narrow. The reefs are marked with stone towers, or were. Steer for the left-hand tower and then come hard to starboard when you're abreast of it."

Jac looked at him.

"You've been here before," she said.

"No," said Faro. "Never."

---

The hook-shaped harbour was exactly where Faro had said it would be, and so were the reefs, and so were the stone towers that marked them, though one had fallen and the other leaned like a drunk. Jac took the *Slip* in herself, at the wheel, with Faro beside her calling the channel, and they came through between the reefs with a yard to spare on either side and into a perfect natural basin of still green water, sheltered on three sides by the curve of the cliffs.

There had been quays here, once. Long stone quays, running along the inside of the hook, with steps going down into the water and iron rings for mooring, rusted now to lumps. There had been warehouses behind the quays, and houses behind the warehouses, climbing the slope in terraces: hundreds of them, all roofless, all empty, their doorways dark, their windows looking out at the harbour like the eye-sockets of a skull. Weeds grew in the streets. Trees grew in the houses. Seabirds nested on every ledge. And over all of it, running up the slope in wide, shallow steps from the harbour to the citadel at the summit, was a great processional stair of the pale glittering stone, broken and overgrown but still magnificent, like the road to a king's throne.

They anchored in the middle of the basin. In the sudden silence when the chain stopped running, they could hear the sea breathing against the cliffs, and the cries of the birds, and something else, very faint, that might have been the wind in the ruins and might not.

"Singing," said Tam, very quietly. His face was white. "Can you hear it? Like they said in Corrow. Voices. Coming up out of the ground."

Faro could hear it. It was not voices. It was the sea, moving through caves and tunnels under the island, making sounds like a great slow organ. It was the sea, singing to itself, in the dark. He had heard it once before, in a dream that was not his own, on the night of the squall.

"It's all right," he said. "It's only the water. Stay with the ship. All of you. I'll go alone."

"Like hell you will," said Jac Teague, and buckled on her cutlass.

---

They went up the processional stair in the morning sun, the two of them, with lanterns and rope and a pick and a crowbar, and a bag of food, and the island watching them.

It was a long climb. The stair went up in a series of wide terraces, each one opening out into a broad paved square with buildings round it, and then narrowing again to climb to the next. In the squares there were fountains, dry now, carved in the shapes of fish and waves and curling sea-things. On the walls of the buildings there were carvings: the same carvings Faro had seen in the temple beneath the Keep. Men and women in long robes with their hands raised to the sea. Ships. Fish. Storms turned away. And over every doorway, the same sign, cut deep into the stone: a single curling wave.

The same sign that had been on the disc of green sea-glass in the dead captain's packet, fourteen years ago, on the magistrate's desk. He had seen it for a moment, by lamplight, before the magistrate closed his hand over it. He had not known what it meant.

He knew now.

"You're very quiet," said Jac, on the fifth terrace, while they rested.

"I'm listening."

"To what?"

"Everything." He looked up at the ruins above them, at the colonnade of broken pillars against the cloud. "This was a city, Jac. A real city. Thousands of people lived here. They had markets and schools and libraries and temples and children, and they talked to the sea every day, and it talked back. For a thousand years." He was quiet a moment. "And then one day the King came, with his Radiants, and burned it. And killed every one of them. And wrote in the histories that they were witches who drowned children."

Jac looked at him, and then at the empty, sunlit square around them, the dry fountain, the dark doorways.

"How do you know all that?"

He did not answer at once. He was not sure how to. Because he did know. He knew it the way he knew the harbour channel, without ever having seen it. As he had climbed the stair, he had felt the memories rising around him, like water rising in a well: not his memories, not Faro's, but the sea's. The Deep's. It had known this place. It had known these people. It had loved them, if a god could love. And it remembered the day they died.

He had felt it, on the third terrace, so strongly that he had had to stop and hold onto a wall. He had seen it. Ships in the harbour, a hundred of them, white sails with gold suns. Fire falling out of a clear sky, not from the ships but from the men on them, men in white and gold with their hands raised, and light pouring out of their hands like water from a burst dam. The houses burning. The people running down to the harbour, to the sea, the way children run to their mother, and standing in the water up to their waists with their hands raised, calling it, calling it, *help us, help us*. And the sea trying to rise. Trying and failing. Because it had already been chained, eleven years before, in a cave under a black rock far to the east, and it was weak, so weak, and it could only lift a little, only a little, a wave that broke on the quay and fell back. And the fire came down on the people standing in the water.

He had tasted salt, and wept with tears that were not his, and Jac had stood beside him on the third terrace with her hand on his shoulder, saying nothing at all.

"I just know," he said now. "Come on. It's not much further."

---

The citadel at the summit was even larger than it had looked from the sea. They walked through it for an hour, through great halls with fallen roofs and long galleries open to the sky, through courtyards where trees had grown up through the paving and pushed it aside, through a library, he knew it was a library, though there was nothing in it now but empty shelves of stone and a drift of ash in the corners, centuries old, where the books had been burned. Faro walked through it all without hesitating, turning left, turning right, going down stairs and up others, as if he had lived there all his life.

*Through the great hall,* Faro's voice said in his head, thin and patient. *Past the library. Through the Court of Tides, the one with the tree in the middle. There'll be a tree, there's always a tree. Down the stair on the north side, the narrow one, not the wide one. Count the doors. The seventh door.*

The seventh door on the narrow north stair was not a door at all. It was a blank wall of the glittering stone, with a single wave carved on it at the height of a man's chest.

Jac looked at it. "Well, that's that," she said. "Unless you've a mason in your pocket."

Faro put his hand flat on the carved wave.

*Not with a pick, Edric. Not with a bar. It was made by Tidewardens, for Tidewardens. There's water in the stone. There's water in everything. Ask it.*

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. He found the stillness. He felt the stone under his palm, and the damp in it, deep down, in the tiny spaces between the crystals: a breath of water, from the sea air, from the rain, from a thousand years of cloud sitting on the summit. He felt it, and he reached, gently, without anger, the way you put your hand into a pool. *Open.*

The stone sighed. It was the only word for it. A long, soft, grinding sigh, like a very old man settling into a chair. Water ran out of the carved wave, a thin trickle, then a stream, as if the stone were weeping. And then the whole slab of wall, ten feet high and five wide, slid slowly back into the hill and to one side, on a groove that had been cut for it a thousand years ago and had not been used in three hundred, and behind it was darkness, and a stair going down, and a smell of salt and stone and something else, something dry and dusty and sweet, like old paper.

Jac Teague said a word that Faro had not heard even Big Olaf use.

He lit his lantern, and went down.

---

The stair went down for a long way, through solid rock, and the sound of the sea grew louder as they descended, the slow singing organ-sound, until it was all around them. And then the stair ended, and they came out onto a ledge above a cave, and Jac's lantern light went out into it and did not reach the far side.

It was a sea-cave: a great natural cavern carved out of the island's heart by the sea, with a floor of still black water and a roof lost in darkness. It was like the temple under the Keep, and nothing like it, because there were no chains here, no gold light, no terrible taut heaving. The water here was calm, and clear, so clear that when Faro held his lantern over it he could see the bottom, twenty feet down, pale sand and rock. Light came into it from somewhere, a faint blue-green glow from beneath the surface on the far side, where a tunnel must open to the sea. And the sea moved through it gently, in and out, breathing, singing.

All around the walls of the cave, at the waterline and above it, there were alcoves carved into the rock. Dozens of them. Hundreds. And in every one, the lantern light caught something that glittered.

Jac lifted her lantern higher, and higher, turning slowly, and her face in the light was the face of a child on Midwinter morning.

There were chests. Iron-bound chests, stone chests, chests of wood so old it had turned black and hard as iron. There were jars, great clay jars sealed with wax. There were heaps of things that had once been in chests and jars, when the chests and jars had rotted away: heaps of coins, gold and silver, in every shape and size, from every kingdom and empire that had ever been, stamped with the faces of kings no one remembered. There were cups and plates and bowls of gold. There were ropes of pearls, black and white and rose. There were jewels, loose, in drifts, rubies and sapphires and emeralds and stones he did not know the names of, catching the lantern light and throwing it back in a thousand colours. There were swords with gems in their hilts, and crowns, and chains of office, and things whose use he could not guess, all piled together in the alcoves around the singing water, as if a thousand years of treasure had been thrown into the cave in a single desperate night.

Which, he supposed, it had.

"Faro," said Jac, in a strange, small voice. "Faro. I think. I think I need to sit down."

She sat down, on the ledge, with her lantern in her lap, and stared.

---

He did not go to the gold first. He walked along the ledge, past the alcoves glittering with more wealth than he had imagined existed in the world, to the far end of the cave, where Faro had told him to look. *At the far end. Above the water. In the oldest alcoves, the ones cut by hand. That's where the real treasure is.*

The oldest alcoves were deeper than the others, and drier, and the chests in them were of stone, sealed with a black pitch that had set as hard as glass. He broke the seal of the first one with the crowbar. Inside, wrapped in oiled silk, and the silk wrapped in waxed cloth, and the cloth wrapped in lead foil, were books.

Dozens of them, in that one chest. He lifted one out with shaking hands and unwrapped it. It was bound in sealskin, and its pages were vellum, and the ink on them was as black and clear as the day it was written. The writing was in the oldest form of the Weir-tongue, the one Faro had taught him, the one the Deep had spoken in the dark. He turned the pages, and read a line, and then another.

*On the Three Disciplines, and the Ways of Their Teaching. Being the Book of the Masters of the House of Cristal, set down in the nine hundred and fortieth year of the Order.*

There were twelve stone chests in the oldest alcoves. Every one of them was full of books.

He sat down on the ledge beside the open chest, with the book in his lap, and put his hand over his eyes. *The last books. Everything the Drying didn't burn.* The old man had known. He had known, and kept it in his head for forty years, and carried it down into the dark with him, and given it to Edric on the rope with half his face fallen, because it was the only thing he had to give.

"Thank you," he said, to no one, to the dark, to an old man sewn into a sack. "Thank you, Faro."

The sea moved in the cave, in and out, singing softly. And somewhere very far away and very deep, at the bottom of the world, something turned, and listened, and he thought, though he could never afterwards have said why, that it was pleased.

---

They stayed on the island for a month.

They brought the *Slip* round to the sea-tunnel on the far side of the island, where the cave opened to the sea, and found that at the lowest tides there was room enough for a longboat to row in under the arch. They brought the crew. Faro made them swear, every one, an oath he took from one of the old books, an oath in the Weir-tongue that he translated for them line by line, by the light of their lanterns, standing on the ledges above the singing water. And when they had sworn it, he showed them the cave.

Big Olaf fell on his knees. Pike laughed until he cried. The Sardhi brothers stood very still and said a prayer in their own language that Faro understood, a prayer of thanks to the Thirst, which was what their grandmothers had called the sea. Tam, the boy, simply walked up to the nearest heap of gold and lay down on it, on his back, with his arms spread, and stared up at the roof of the cave, and did not say a word for an hour.

Faro gave each of them a share. Not a share of the gold: a share of the gold they could carry. Which was, it turned out, enough to make every one of them richer than any lord in Valemont.

"You'll want to go home," he said to them, on the beach below the citadel, on the last night, with a fire burning and the stars overhead. "Buy land. Buy ships. Marry. Live like kings. I won't blame you. You've earned it. You found me on a reef and gave me a life. This is the least I can give back."

There was a long silence round the fire.

"And you?" said Jac Teague. "What'll you do?"

He looked up at the citadel on the summit, black against the stars, with its broken dome.

"I'm going to stay," he said. "I'm going to rebuild it."

They stared at him.

"This was the last city where people listened to the sea," he said. "The King burned it, and wrote lies about it, and made sure no one would ever come here again. I want to bring it back. Not the same. It can never be the same. But a harbour, and a town, and people. A place for people the King's forgotten, or doesn't want, or would rather were dead. Smugglers. Runaways. Outcasts. The Weir-folk, if they'll come. A place outside his law, where the sea's still listened to." He was quiet a moment. "And then, when it's strong enough, when I'm strong enough, I'm going to go east. To the mainland. To Solace. There are some people there I need to see."

"What people?" said Tam.

Faro looked into the fire.

"Old friends," he said.

Nobody asked anything else. They were a crew who had learned not to.

But in the morning, when the *Slip* was ready to sail, with her hold full of gold and her crew rich beyond their dreams, Jac Teague came and found him on the quay, where he was standing looking at the empty houses, and stood beside him for a while without speaking.

"Pike's going," she said at last. "He's got a sister in the north he hasn't seen in twenty years. And the Sardhi lads, they've got family. Olaf's staying. He says the Sun's been very good to him and he wants to build it a temple, here, to say thank you, and I haven't the heart to tell him it wasn't the Sun. Tam's staying, of course. He'd follow you into the sea if you asked." She paused. "And me."

He turned and looked at her.

"I've got enough gold in that ship to buy half of Corrow," said Jac Teague. "And I've thought about it. And I've decided I'd rather see what you do with the other half of the world." She held out her hand. "If you want a captain."

He took it.

"I want a captain," he said.

And that was the beginning of the city of Cristal.
