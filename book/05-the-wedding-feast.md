## Chapter 5: The Wedding Feast

Among the Weir-folk, a wedding took a night and a morning.

On the night before the spring tide came the Feast of Lamps: the families of the bride and groom, and their friends, and anyone else who could squeeze in through the door, would eat and drink and dance until midnight, and at midnight the bride's grandmother would bring out the crown she had made, and show it to the guests, and hide it away again. Then at dawn, when the tide was at its highest and the sea came furthest into the marsh, the bride and groom would go down to the water in a boat with no oars and let the tide carry them out into the channel, and there, with the whole Weir watching from the walkways, they would speak their vows to the sea, and the grandmother would crown the bride, and it would be done.

"The sea has to agree," Mira had explained to Edric once, years ago, when they were sixteen and he had laughed at it. "That's why there are no oars. If the sea doesn't want you married, it won't take you out. Sometimes it doesn't. Sometimes the boat just sits there at the landing, and everybody goes home, and the couple never marry."

"What happens to them?"

"Nothing. They go and marry somebody else. Or they don't." She had shrugged. "The sea knows things."

He had stopped laughing at it after that. He was not superstitious, particularly; sailors were supposed to be, but Halloran had cured him of most of it. But there was something in the way the Weir-folk talked about the sea that made the back of his neck prickle: not as a thing, but as a someone. An old someone, who was listening.

---

He had hired the whole of the Three Anchors for the Feast of Lamps, as he had promised, and the whole of Saltmere seemed to have come.

The crew of the *Gull's Promise* were there, every man of them, scrubbed and combed and squeezed into their shore clothes, roaring at every toast. Master Moren was there, beaming, with his daughter Julie, who was twelve and solemn and had brought Edric a present of a brass compass she had saved for since she heard he was captain. Half the Weir had come up the hill, the old women in their black shawls and the young men in their bright sashes, and some of them had indeed spat in the beer at the door, ceremonially, and then drunk it anyway. The harbour master was there, and the rope-maker, and the baker from Mill Street, and a great many people Edric had never met in his life who seemed to know exactly who he was.

Tomas Vane sat in the place of honour by the fire, in a new coat that Edric had bought him, with a plate that the potboy had been ordered to keep full. Every so often he would look across the room at his son, and his face would do something complicated, and he would look down at his plate.

Mira's mother sat beside him, coughing a little into a handkerchief, wrapped in three shawls. Mira's grandmother sat beside her, a tiny, ancient, nut-brown woman with eyes like black beads, who had been introduced to Edric as Old Ysolde and who had looked at him for a long, long time when he bowed to her, with her head on one side, like a bird looking at a worm.

"You've the sea in you," she said at last, in the Weir-tongue. "Your mother's blood. It's quiet in you. But it's there."

"Thank you, Grandmother," said Edric, uncertain whether it was a compliment.

"Don't thank me," said the old woman. "It's not always a gift." And she had turned away to her plate and not spoken to him again all evening.

And Mira. Mira, in a dress of sea-green that her mother had sewn over the winter by lamplight, with her black hair loose down her back as the Weir-folk brides wore it on the Night of Lamps, and her freckles, and her wide mouth laughing. Mira, who danced with every one of the crew in turn and with Master Moren and with the harbour master and, gravely, with Tomas Vane, who could barely stand but stood anyway. Mira, who came back to Edric between every dance and took his hand under the table, as if to make sure he was still there.

"Are you happy?" she said in his ear, once, under the noise.

"I didn't know you could be this happy," he said. "I didn't know it was allowed."

"It's allowed," said Mira. "Tonight it's allowed." And she kissed him, and the crew roared, and somebody struck up a reel.

---

Dorran Quell was there.

Edric had invited him, of course; he had invited the whole crew. And Dorran had come, in a neat dark coat, and shaken Edric's hand, and wished him every happiness, and given him a present: a fine leather-bound ledger with his initials stamped on the front in gold. "For the accounts," Dorran had said, with his small smile. "Every captain needs to keep good accounts."

Edric had thanked him warmly and meant it. He had been a little puzzled by Dorran on the voyage, by the business with the pepper in the aft hold, which he had looked into one night and found sand where there should have been spice, and had not been able to make sense of. He had meant to ask Dorran about it, quietly, and then the captain had fallen sick, and there had been the fever and the storms and the burial, and somehow he had never found the moment. He had decided, in the end, that it was something to do with the Ember Coast merchants, some swindle they had worked on the ship before the casks were ever loaded, and that Dorran was probably as embarrassed about it as anyone and would rather not have it raised. He would mention it to Moren, in time, as a thing to watch for on the next voyage. There was no hurry.

So he shook Dorran's hand, and thanked him for the ledger, and thought no more about it, and Dorran went and sat in a corner and drank small beer and watched.

Cade Rusk was there too, of course, because he had never in his life missed a free drink, and he sat at the end of the long table nearest the beer barrel and drank a great deal. But he was quieter than usual. He did not stand up and make speeches about how he had always known the boy would go far. He did not try to dance. He sat with his shoulders hunched and his eyes sliding, and every time the door opened, he jumped.

And Fenrick Morrow was there.

Nobody had expected him. He came in late, after the first dances, in his blue Coastal Guard coat, freshly brushed, and stood in the doorway until the noise around him faltered and died. Mira's hand tightened in Edric's under the table.

Then Fenrick crossed the room, and stopped in front of them, and bowed. A proper bow, this time: stiff, correct, sober. His face was very pale.

"Cousin," he said to Mira. "Captain Vane." He held out a hand to Edric. "You asked me to come. I've come. I wanted to say I'm sorry. For the other night. I'd been drinking. I said things I shouldn't have said."

Edric stood up and took the hand. It was cold, and damp, and it held his for barely a moment before letting go.

"Thank you, Fenrick," he said. "I mean it. Thank you for coming. Sit. Eat. Drink to us."

"I'll drink to you," said Fenrick. He did not sit. He went to the bar, and took a cup, and stood with his back against the wall near the door, and drank, and did not take his eyes off the door all night.

"He's sorry," Mira said, wonderingly, in Edric's ear. "I've never known him say sorry in his life."

"People change," said Edric.

Mira looked across the room at her cousin's pale face, and his eyes on the door, and did not answer.

---

It was nearly midnight. The fire had burned down and been built up again. The noise had reached the stage where it no longer rose and fell but simply went on, like the sea. Old Ysolde had got up from her chair, slowly, leaning on Mira's mother's arm, and the room was quietening around her, because everyone knew what came at midnight. She had a bundle in her hands, wrapped in an old grey shawl.

"Children," she said, in her cracked old voice, in the common tongue, so that the hill-folk could understand. "Children, it's time. Time to show the crown. Time to ask the sea."

A hush fell. Mira stood up, flushed and laughing, and went to her grandmother. Edric stood up too. The whole room turned towards them.

The old woman began to unwrap the shawl.

And the door of the Three Anchors opened.

It did not bang. It did not crash. It simply swung open, wide, and let in the cold night air, and with it a sound that every person in that room had heard before and never wanted to hear again: the measured, ringing tread of soldiers' boots on cobbles.

Six of them came in, in the white and gold of the Crown Guard, with the sun of Aurelian blazing on their breastplates and their hands on their sword hilts. An officer led them, a lieutenant with a lean, tired face and a paper in his hand. They stopped just inside the door and spread out, and the guests nearest them shrank back against the walls, and a jug fell from somebody's hand and smashed on the floor, and nobody bent to pick it up.

Old Ysolde stood with her bundle half unwrapped, and a glimpse of white shell and sea-lavender showing through the grey wool, and did not move.

"I am looking," said the lieutenant into the silence, "for Edric Vane."

---

For a moment, Edric genuinely did not understand. He stood there in his good coat with his bride beside him and his hand still half-raised from clapping, and the words seemed to arrive from a great distance, like sounds heard underwater.

Then Master Moren was on his feet, red-faced and blustering. "What is the meaning of this? Lieutenant, this is a private gathering. A wedding. The boy is my captain. What could you possibly want with him?"

"I am Edric Vane," said Edric. He heard his own voice, very calm. "What do you want?"

The lieutenant looked at him. It was not an unkind look. It was the look of a man who had done this many times, and did not enjoy it, and had stopped expecting to.

"Edric Vane," he said, "master of the merchant ship *Gull's Promise*. I have a warrant for your arrest, signed by the deputy Crown Magistrate of Saltmere, on a charge of treason against the Throne. You'll come with us now, please, quietly. Your sea-chest has already been taken from the ship. Your father's rooms are being searched as we speak."

There was a sound in the room like a wave pulling back over shingle: a hundred people drawing breath at once.

"Treason," said Moren. "*Treason?* This is absurd. This is a mistake. The boy's never been north of Saltmere in his life. He's never so much as—"

"Then the magistrate will find that out, sir," said the lieutenant, "and he'll be home by morning. But I have my orders." He nodded to two of his men, who came forward.

"No," said Mira. She stepped in front of Edric. She was white to the lips. "No. You can't. It's our wedding. It's the Night of Lamps. The tide's at dawn. You can't take him tonight."

"I'm sorry, miss," said the lieutenant, and he sounded as if he meant it. "Truly. But I can."

Edric put his hands on her shoulders, gently, and moved her aside.

He was not afraid. That was the strange thing: he would remember it later, in the dark, and not understand it. He was not afraid at all. He had done nothing. He had committed no crime, carried nothing, said nothing, been nothing but a sailor and a son and a man in love. It was a mistake, plainly, some confusion of names or ships, and the magistrate would see it at once, and he would be back by dawn, for the tide. He was as sure of it as he was of the sunrise.

The only thing that troubled him, at the bottom of his mind, was the oilskin packet at the bottom of his sea-chest. *Nothing is ever just a letter.* But that was nothing: a dead man's private message, sealed, unopened. He would explain it. He would tell the magistrate the truth. The truth was always enough.

"It's all right," he said to Mira. "It's a mistake. You'll see. I'll be back for the tide."

"Edric—"

"I'll be back for the tide." He kissed her forehead. He turned to Old Ysolde, who still stood with the half-unwrapped crown in her hands, and bowed to her, as gravely as if nothing had happened. "Grandmother. Keep it for me. Till dawn."

The old woman looked at him with her black bead eyes. She did not say anything. She wrapped the shawl back over the crown, slowly, with her twisted old fingers, and held it against her chest.

Edric turned to the lieutenant and held out his wrists.

"I'm ready."

---

They did not bind him. The lieutenant shook his head at the shackles one of his men held out, and they simply walked him to the door, one guard on either side, through a room full of people who had stopped breathing.

At the door, he looked back.

He saw his father, trying to stand, and failing, and falling back into his chair by the fire with his face grey, and Master Moren bending over him. He saw Mira, held back by her mother and two of the crew, her face streaked with tears, not making a sound. He saw Old Ysolde with the bundle clutched to her chest. He saw the crew of the *Gull's Promise*, his crew, standing helpless with their fists clenched. He saw Cade Rusk, at the end of the long table, with his face buried in his hands.

He saw Dorran Quell in his corner, with his small beer in front of him, and an expression of polite, sorrowful concern, exactly the face a man might wear at the funeral of an acquaintance.

And he saw Fenrick Morrow by the door, not three feet away, with his back against the wall and his cup in his hand. Their eyes met, for a moment. Fenrick's face was white as paper, and wet with sweat, and there was something in his eyes that Edric could not read, something that looked almost like terror.

Then the guards moved him on, out into the night, and the door of the Three Anchors swung shut behind him on the warm yellow light and the fire and the smell of the feast, and he was walking up the dark hill between two men in white and gold, with the cold stars overhead and the sea whispering on the shingle behind him.

He did not look back again. He thought: *I'll be back for the tide.*

---

At dawn, the tide came up the channels of the Weir, higher than it had come in years: the great spring tide of the full moon, the luckiest wedding tide of the year.

It filled the reed-beds and lifted the boats at their moorings and lapped at the very edges of the walkways. It came all the way up to the Solens' landing stage, where a small boat with no oars sat waiting, garlanded with sea-lavender and white shells.

Mira sat on the landing stage beside it, in her sea-green dress, with her feet in the water, and watched the tide come in, and did not move.

It rose. It reached its height. It hung there for a while, silver and still under the pale dawn sky.

And then it turned, and began to go out, and the little boat with no oars rocked gently at its rope, and went nowhere at all.
