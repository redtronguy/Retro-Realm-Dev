## Chapter 13: The Eye in the Dark

The cold was the first thing. It was not like any cold he had ever felt. It came through the sailcloth and through his skin and into his bones all at once, a cold so total that for a moment it was not even painful. It was simply everything, the way the dark was everything.

The second thing was the pull. The iron ball at his feet was dragging him down, feet first, fast. He felt the pressure building in his ears, in his chest. He felt the water rushing up past the sacking.

The third thing was the knife.

He had held onto it. Somehow, through the fall and the impact, his fingers had stayed locked around the handle of Faro's chisel. He brought it up, blind, and drove the point into the sailcloth above his face, and ripped. The cloth was old and wet and tore more easily than he had dared hope. He ripped again, sideways. Water flooded in, and he got his head and shoulders through the hole, and then his arms, and he was out, kicking free of the sacking as it fell away below him in the dark.

And he was still going down.

The rope. The rope that tied the iron ball to the sack. He had meant to cut it. But in the fall it had twisted, and when he kicked out of the sack, it had come with him, a loop of it wrapped tight around his left ankle. He felt it bite. He felt the weight hanging from it, dragging him down and down.

He doubled over in the water and groped for his foot. Found the rope. Got the chisel against it and sawed. It was thick, tarred rope, hard as wood, and the chisel was blunt from thirteen years of stone. He sawed and sawed, with his lungs beginning to burn, with the pressure in his ears building to a scream. He felt fibres part. Not enough. He sawed. The rope was half through.

And then the chisel slipped out of his numb fingers, and was gone.

He grabbed for it. His hand closed on black water.

He kept going down.

---

He would remember, later, that he was not afraid.

He had been afraid all his life of drowning, the way every sailor is: the one death that waits under every voyage. He had dreamed of it as a boy, after his first storm. He had watched men drown, twice, in the years on Moren's ships. He knew what it was. He knew how it went.

But now that it was happening, he was not afraid. He was only very cold, and very tired, and very sorry. Sorry for Faro, who had given him everything and asked only one thing in return, and would never know he had failed. Sorry for his father, and for Mira, whose faces he could not see. Sorry that he would never see the sky again. He had felt the wind on the other side of the sailcloth, at the top of the Keep, and smelled it, and that would have to do.

The water was getting darker, if that were possible. And it was getting warmer.

He noticed that, dimly, through the cold and the burning in his chest. It was getting warmer as he went down, not colder. And it was getting lighter. Not lighter, exactly. But there was something below him, a long way below, that was not quite dark. A faint glow. Gold.

He looked down.

He was falling past the foot of the Teeth. He could see the rock, a black wall going down beside him into the depths, much deeper than the cliffs above, and he understood that the Teeth were not an island at all but the top of a mountain that went down and down into the sea. And below him, far below, so far it was like looking down from a mountaintop into a valley at night, there was light. Seven lines of gold light, coming down out of the rock of the mountain, straight and taut, and going on down, into a darkness so deep that the light itself grew faint and failed before it reached the bottom.

The chains. He was falling towards the chains.

And around them, below them, in the vast dark where they disappeared, something was moving.

It was too big to see. That was the thing he would never be able to explain afterwards, to anyone, even to himself. It was not that it was hidden, or far away. It was simply too big: the way the sky is too big to see, or the night. It was the darkness itself, moving, slowly, all of it at once, the way the whole sea moves in a swell. It went down further than he could see and out further than he could see. And it was turning, very slowly, at the bottom of the world, towards him.

His lungs gave out. He breathed in, and the sea came into him, cold and salt and absolute, and filled him up, and the gold light went out, and there was nothing.

---

And then there was something.

He was not in the water any more. Or he was, but it did not matter. There was no cold, and no pressure, and no pain, and no need to breathe. There was no up or down. He was simply there, in a darkness that was not empty, but full: full the way a held breath is full, full of a vast and patient attention, all of it turned on him.

And in front of him, in the dark, an eye opened.

It was not an eye. He knew that, even as he saw it. It was something that was using the shape of an eye because that was the only shape he would understand. It was as large as the sky. It was the colour of the sea at the very bottom of the world, where the light has never been, a blue so deep that it was almost black, and in its depths, very far down, things moved: shapes, currents, lights, like a whole ocean seen from above. And it was looking at him. Not at his face, or his body, but at him. At everything he was. It looked at him the way Faro had looked at a puzzle, turning it over, examining every side, and he felt it go through him, through all his years, through the Weir and the quays and the *Gull's Promise* and the Feast of Lamps and the cell and the dark and the old man's hand going cold in his, and he could not have hidden one thing from it if he had tried.

Then it spoke.

It did not use words. It was more like a tide coming in: a great slow pressure of meaning, rising through him until he was full of it. But when he remembered it afterwards, it came back to him in words, the way dreams do, and the words were in the oldest form of the Weir-tongue, the one Faro had taught him, the one his mother had never known.

*Little one.*

He could not answer. He did not need to.

*You fell to me. As I fell. They threw you into the dark, as they threw me. Bound you, as they bound me. Took from you, as they take from me. Every day. Every hour. For a span of time your kind cannot count.*

The eye moved. Below it, far below, the seven gold lines shuddered, and he felt it, a pain like a fishhook in the throat of the world.

*The old one taught you. The last of the listeners. I heard him. Thirty-eight of your years, on the stone above me, listening. And then you. You, who heard me through the stone, and wept with my tears. You, who reached for me once in anger and found me. You, who lifted my waters when the old one fell.*

*I know you, little one.*

*And I am dying.*

He felt it then, as he had felt it in the temple, but a thousand times worse: the grief and the fury and the terrible patience. Three hundred years of being drunk from. Three hundred years of growing weaker, and sicker, while the fish thinned and the weed died and the storms went wrong. It was not a person dying. It was a world.

*They will kill me, in time,* said the Deep. *The golden one drinks and drinks, and does not stop, because he fears to stop. He will drink until there is nothing left. And then the sea will be only water. Dead water. Salt and silence, forever. And he will find another thing to drink. He always does.*

The eye drew closer. Or he drew closer to it. It filled everything.

*I cannot break the chains. Not alone. They were made to hold me, and they hold. But a listener, one with my waters in him, one who stood at the Lock, one who carried my strength into the place where the chains are fixed. Such a one could break them.*

*Be my tide, little one. Carry me. I will give you back your life. I will give you strength such as no listener has held since the old days. And you will take back what was taken from you. And then, when you are ready, you will come back to the Lock and break the one who chains us both.*

---

He thought of Faro.

He did not decide to. The old man was simply there, suddenly, in his mind, in the dark, as clear as if he were sitting on the pallet in his ragged grey robe with his useless arm in his lap. *It's not kind, Edric. It's not cruel either. It's hungry.* And: *Hate is a tide. It will carry you anywhere. And it will never bring you home.*

And: *Something that was mostly sea. Mostly anger. Mostly the Deep, looking out through a human face.*

He found that he could speak. Not with his mouth. With something else. He made the words in the old tongue, slowly, carefully, the way Faro had taught him, and pushed them out into the dark.

*If I say yes,* he said, *what happens to me?*

The eye considered him. He felt something move through the vast dark around him that might, in something smaller, have been surprise. Or amusement.

*The old one taught you well,* said the Deep. *You ask the question the young never ask.*

*Answer it.*

*You will live,* said the Deep. *You will be stronger than any of your kind. My waters will move at your asking. And every time you ask, a little more of me will come into you. More and more, the more you take. You will feel what I feel. You will remember what I remember. And if you take enough, little one, in the end, there will be more of me in you than there is of you. That is the price. It is the only price there is. The sea does not bargain.*

*And if I say no?*

*Then you drown,* said the Deep, quite simply. *Here, now. And the old one's last listener dies with him, and I go on dying, slowly, in the dark, and the golden one goes on drinking. And the men who put you here go on living, and eating, and sleeping in their beds, until they are old, and die of nothing, and no one ever knows what they did.*

He was quiet for a long time, in the dark. Or perhaps it was no time at all.

*I won't do it for revenge,* he said at last. *I promised him. For justice. Not for revenge.*

And the Deep laughed.

He felt it go through the whole of the sea, through the whole of the dark, a vast slow tremor, like a swell rolling across an ocean. It was not a cruel laugh. It was not a kind one. It was the laugh of something unimaginably old hearing a child explain the difference between two words for water.

*Justice,* said the Deep. *Revenge. Those are your words, little one. Words for the land. The sea does not know them. A wave does not ask whether the cliff deserves to fall.* The eye drew back a little. *But if you want to keep your words, keep them. Take only what you need. I will not force more on you than you ask for. I have been patient for longer than your kind has had a name. I can be patient a little longer.*

*Choose.*

He thought of Saltmere. He thought of a girl on a landing stage with her feet in the water and a boat with no oars. He thought of an old man in a cell, weeping because a drop of water had risen. *Wait, and hope.*

He thought: *I've waited fourteen years.*

*Yes,* he said.

---

The eye closed.

And the sea came into him.

It came in over his heart. That was where he felt it first: a burning, like a brand, like a hot iron laid flat against his chest, except that it was cold, cold beyond anything, the cold of the very bottom of the world. It spread. He felt it go out from his heart in lines, branching, like rivers on a map, like the veins of a leaf, across his chest, down his arms, up his throat. He felt it in his blood. He felt his blood *move*, all of it at once, as if it had suddenly remembered that it was water, and that all water is one water, and that it belonged to the sea.

He opened his eyes.

He was in the water again. The real water, cold and black, falling past the dark flank of the mountain under the Teeth, with his lungs full of sea and the iron ball still dragging at his ankle. But he was not drowning. The water in his lungs did not hurt. It simply was, the way air is. And he could see. Not with light; there was no light, except the faint gold glow of the chains far below. But he could feel the water around him, all of it, every current and eddy and swirl, for a long way in every direction, the way a man in a dark room can feel the shape of the room around him. He could feel the rock of the mountain. He could feel a shoal of small fish, half a mile off, turning all together. He could feel the surface, far above, heaving in a storm. He could feel the rope around his ankle, and the weight on the end of it, and the frayed half-cut place where the chisel had sawed.

He thought: *Let go.*

The water around his ankle moved. It moved like a blade, a thin hard edge of current, sharper than any knife. The rope parted. The iron ball fell away into the dark, towards the chains, and he did not watch it go.

He thought: *Up.*

And the sea took him up.

---

He came out of the water like a breaching whale.

He shot up out of the depths faster than he had fallen, with the water carrying him, pushing him, a great column of current rising beneath him like a hand. He burst through the surface into a howling night of wind and rain and towering black waves, and went up into the air, a man's height, two, and fell back, and went under, and came up again, coughing. The sea came out of his lungs in a great gout, and the air went in, cold and wet and real, the first breath he had taken under the sky in fourteen years.

He laughed. He could not help it. He floated on his back in the heaving black water, with the rain on his face and the wind screaming over him, and laughed and laughed until he choked.

Behind him, a long way off, he saw the Teeth: a black shape against the black sky, and on top of it, a single red light.

He turned his back on it.

He did not know where he was going. He did not care. He thought only: *Away. Away from there. Somewhere with land.* And the sea heard him. He felt the currents gather around him, beneath him, like a team of horses taking up the slack. He felt the swell lift him. And then he was moving: through the water, across the water, faster than any man could swim, faster than any boat could row, riding the face of the waves like a gull rides the wind, with the storm all around him and the Teeth falling away behind.

He did not know how long it went on. An hour. Three. The storm blew itself out. The rain stopped. The clouds tore apart and showed stars, and he lay in the water and looked up at them, and they were the same stars, the ones Halloran had taught him, the ones Faro had named. He could have wept at them. Perhaps he did.

And then, as the sky began to grey in the east, the sea set him down.

It did it gently. He felt the currents slow, and ease, and let go of him one by one, and he was floating in shallow water over pale sand, with small waves pushing him towards a long low shape that was land: a reef, a scatter of rocks and sand and scrub, barely higher than the waves. He crawled up out of the surf on his hands and knees, and fell forward onto the wet sand, and lay there, with his cheek against it, feeling it. Sand. Real sand. Under the sky.

And then the strength went out of him all at once, the way a wave goes out, and he slept.

---

He woke to a boot in his ribs.

"*Oi.* Oi, you. Dead or alive? Make your mind up, I haven't got all morning."

He opened his eyes. It was full day, bright and blue and blinding, and he flinched from it and threw an arm across his face. Fourteen years. He had not seen the sun in fourteen years.

"Alive, then," said the voice, sounding faintly disappointed. "Typical. Get up. Can you get up?"

He got up. Or tried to. He got as far as his knees, and stayed there, swaying, squinting at the shape standing over him against the glare.

It was a woman. A short, stocky, broad-shouldered woman of about thirty-five, with a sailor's tan and a sailor's squint and a mass of curly red hair tied back with what looked like a piece of fishing line. She wore a man's shirt and breeches and boots, and a cutlass on her hip, and a pistol in her belt, and she was looking down at him with her hands on her hips and an expression of deep professional suspicion.

Behind her, in a little cove in the reef, a sleek black two-masted cutter rode at anchor. Its sails were patched in grey and brown, and there was no name on its stern, and no flag at its masthead. On its deck, half a dozen men in a similar style of dress were leaning on the rail, watching with interest.

"Who are you?" said the woman. "And how in the nine hells did you get out here? This is Gull Reef. There's nothing for thirty miles. And there was a storm last night that'd sink a warship."

He opened his mouth. Nothing came out. He had not spoken to anyone but Faro and the guards in thirteen years, and his voice, when it finally came, was a dry, cracked croak.

"Shipwreck," he said. "Last night. The storm. I don't. I don't know where the others are."

The woman looked at him for a long moment. He knew what she was seeing: a man with hair down past his shoulders and a beard down to his chest, both tangled and matted with salt. A man as pale as a fish's belly, as thin as a rake, in the rags of what had once been a prisoner's shirt. A man who had come out of a storm that sank warships with not a mark on him.

"Shipwreck," she said. "Right." She looked at the empty horizon, and then at him again, and something in her face changed: a sort of resigned shrewdness, as if she had seen a great many things washed up on beaches and had long ago stopped expecting them to explain themselves. "Can you work a sail, Shipwreck?"

"Yes."

"Haul a line? Splice? Steer?"

"Yes. All of it. I was. I was a captain, once."

"Course you were," said the woman. "Everybody was a captain once." She held out a hand. "Jac Teague. That's my ship, the *Slip*. We're bound west, for the Reach, with a hold full of things the King's customs men would very much like to know about and never will. We're a man short since the storm took Benno off the bowsprit. You can come with us, and work your passage, and not ask questions, and we won't ask you any. Or you can stay here on Gull Reef and eat seaweed. Your choice."

He took her hand. It was hard and calloused and very warm, and it pulled him to his feet as if he weighed nothing.

"What's your name, Shipwreck?" said Jac Teague. "Your real one. Or the one you want me to call you. I don't much care which."

He stood on the sand of Gull Reef in the blinding sun, swaying, and thought about it.

Edric Vane was dead. Edric Vane had died fourteen years ago, at the Feast of Lamps, when a door opened. Edric Vane had been sewn into a sack and thrown off a cliff last night, and gone down to the bottom of the sea, and not come back.

"Faro," he said. "Call me Faro."

---

The men on the *Slip* gave him water, and a biscuit, and a shirt that did not fit, and a corner of the hold to sleep in. Nobody asked him any questions. Jac Teague ran that kind of ship.

But before he slept, he went up on deck alone, to the stern, where nobody was, and leaned over the rail, and looked down at his reflection in the still water of the cove.

A stranger looked back. A gaunt, wild, bearded stranger, with long hair the colour of wet rope and grey eyes that seemed to belong to someone much older. He would not have known himself. No one in Saltmere would know him. He thought about that for a long time.

Then he opened the borrowed shirt, and looked down at his chest.

The lines were there. They spread out from the centre of his chest, over his heart, in a branching pattern like the veins of a leaf, or rivers seen from a great height. They went across his ribs and over his shoulders and down his arms to the elbow. They went up his throat to just under the jaw. They were very faint in the daylight, a pale shimmering blue just under the skin, like the colour you see at the bottom of clear water. When he moved, they seemed to move too, very slightly, like light on the sea.

He touched them. They were cold.

From the bow of the *Slip*, Jac Teague was calling out the order to weigh anchor, and men were moving to the capstan, and the sails were going up. He closed the shirt and buttoned it to the throat.

Then he asked the nearest man, as casually as he could, what the date was. And when the man told him, the year and the month and the day, he went very still, and gripped the rail, and stood there for a long time, looking east, towards a coast he could not see.

Fourteen years. He was thirty-three years old.

The *Slip* came round into the wind, and her sails filled, and she leaned over and began to run, west, towards the Shattered Reach, and the man who called himself Faro stood at her stern with his shirt buttoned to the throat, and watched her wake, and under his collar, over his heart, the sea burned cold.

*End of Part Two*
