## Chapter 26: The Price of Bread

The bank of Quell fell on the third day after the trial, and it fell the way great houses always fall: slowly, and then all at once.

It began on the morning after the Great Court, when the *Courant* printed every word the prisoner had said in the dock, including the part about the lady in the third gallery who had fainted, and everyone in Solace knew exactly who she was. By noon, the Baroness Quell had left the city, in a closed carriage, with her maid, for her family's house in the north, and the *Courant*'s correspondent had it from her coachman that she did not intend to return. By evening, it was being said in every coffee house on the Street of Gold that the Lord Treasurer's wife had been the High Justicar's mistress, and borne him a son in secret, and that the son was the murderer they called the Pale Knight; and that the Lord Treasurer's daughter had run away to Port Jasmine rather than marry him; and that the Lord Treasurer himself had not been home in three days, and was sleeping in his counting-house, and had not been seen to eat.

None of that, by itself, would have brought down the bank. Scandal is a weather, in a city like Solace; it blows through, and is forgotten. But money is a tide, and a tide can be frightened.

On the morning of the second day, a merchant of the Street of Gold who had kept three hundred thousand marks in the house of Quell for eleven years walked into the counting-house and asked to withdraw it. All of it. In gold. He did not give a reason. He did not need to. The clerks paid it, slowly, with many apologies, out of the reserves. By noon, the story had gone round the Street of Gold that the merchant had been paid, but that it had taken an hour, and that the clerks had looked frightened. By three, there were eleven more merchants in the counting-house. By five, there were forty, and a queue in the street outside, and the clerks had stopped apologising and started to look sick.

On the morning of the third day, the doors of the house of Quell did not open.

---

Edric did not watch it. He had not touched the bowl of still water since the day in the grey house. He did not need to. He could feel it, without trying, the way he felt the weather: the currents of money in the white city turning all at once, like a shoal of fish, and rushing out of the great drain at the centre of Solace in a single panicked flood, until there was nothing left in it but mud.

He did not pull. He did not push. He did not draw on the bank again; he had not drawn on it since the night of the mirror-tower. He sat in the study at the back of the house with the blue door, with the cuts on his hands bandaged and the Tidemark cold on his face, and let it happen. And felt nothing. Not sweetness. Not anything.

*Enough*, an old man had said, with his eyes.

On the evening of the third day, Bastien came in and said that the Ministry of the Treasury had announced an audit of the Blight Fund. That the King himself had ordered it, after the storm, because so many of the beacons on the coast had failed in the rain, and the Radiant priests who tended them were asking why the money for new ones had never come. That the auditors were to begin in the morning.

And that the Lord Treasurer had not been seen since noon.

---

Dorran Quell left Solace an hour after midnight, alone, on horseback, by the north gate.

He had not ridden a horse in fifteen years. He rode badly, hunched and stiff, in a plain brown cloak with the hood up, with a pair of saddlebags strapped behind him that were so heavy the horse kept stumbling. In the saddlebags, wrapped in oilcloth, were letters of credit drawn on banks in Port Jasmine and the southern empires, and a quantity of gold, and the deeds to a house in a city in the far south where no one knew his name. All together, they came to a little under two million marks. It was everything that was left of the Blight Fund. It was everything the auditors would find missing in the morning.

He had told himself, in his counting-house that afternoon, with the doors shut and the crowd hammering on them outside, that he was not stealing it. That it had been his for eleven years, to all intents and purposes. That he had only ever borrowed it, and meant to put it back, and that the King and the Treasury and the beacons and the fishing towns would never miss it now, because the bank was gone and the fund was gone and nothing he did could change that. That a man had a right to save something from the wreck.

He had sat at his desk for a long time after he told himself that, with the black ledger open in front of him, looking at the numbers. And for the first time in eleven years, his hands had not shaken. They had been quite still. As if they had finally given up.

He rode north through the night, along the king's road into the hills, towards the Greyhills and the passes and the border, and did not look back at the white city on its cliffs, or the gold light burning at the top of the Sunspire.

---

They took him on the second night, in the Greyhills, in the same ravine where they had taken Aren Morrow.

He did not even see them come. One moment he was riding along the stony road between the rocks in the dark, half asleep in the saddle, with the horse stumbling under the weight of the saddlebags; and the next there were men all round him, in leather and rags, with crossbows and long knives, and a hand on his bridle, and a voice, cheerful, deep, from behind a scarf: "Good evening, my lord. A cold night for travelling. Let's get you somewhere warm."

They took him up into the hills by tracks that were not tracks, to the ruined monastery in the ravine, and put him in a cave in the side of the rock, the same cave where a frightened boy had sat with his back against the wall and decided not to cry. They took his saddlebags. They took his cloak and his boots and his purse. They gave him a pallet of straw and a blanket and a jug of water, and left him there, with a guard at the door.

And in the morning, the big man with the black beard and the single gold earring came and squatted in front of him, and looked at him by the light from the cave mouth, and grinned.

"So this is the Lord Treasurer," said Vasco Rook. "You don't look like much, my lord. I thought you'd be fatter."

"How much?" said Dorran. His voice was hoarse. "How much do you want? There's a great deal of money in those bags. Take it. Take half. Take all of it. Only let me go."

Rook looked at him with his head on one side, and something in his face that Dorran did not like.

"Oh, I'm not going to take your money, my lord," he said. "I've been told not to touch a mark of it. It's all quite safe. It's all still yours." He stood up. "No, you're my guest. You can stay as long as you like. And we'll feed you, of course. We're not savages. Whatever you like to eat. Only," and his grin widened, "it's very expensive, food, up here in the hills. Very hard to come by. I'm afraid we'll have to charge."

---

The first meal cost a hundred thousand marks.

Dorran had refused to believe it, at first. He had laughed, a thin, cracked, frightened laugh, when the boy with the squint and the missing front tooth came in at noon with a plate of bread and cheese and a cup of wine, and named the price. He had said it was absurd. He had said he would not pay it. He had sent the boy away.

By evening, he was very hungry. By the next morning, he was hungrier than he had been since he was a child in the back streets of Saltmere, the son of a drunken cooper, who had learned to count by counting the coppers his father drank. By noon on the second day, when the boy came in with a plate of bread and a bowl of soup, Dorran signed a letter of credit for a hundred thousand marks in favour of a bank in Corrow, with shaking hands, and the boy took it away, and left the soup.

The second meal was another hundred thousand. So was the third.

He tried to bargain. He tried to eat less, and less often: once a day, then once every two days. He tried to make the bread last, hiding the crusts in the straw. He tried to drink only water, which was free, and lie still on the pallet, and not think about food. It did not work. By the fourth day, he was signing a letter of credit every time the boy came in, without even asking what was on the plate.

By the fifth day, there was very little left.

He lay on the straw in the cave with the blanket over him and looked at the rock ceiling and did the sums in his head, the way he had done them every morning for eleven years, sitting on the edge of his bed by the light of a single candle. He did not need the ledger. He knew to the mark what was in the saddlebags, and what he had signed away, and what was left. He had always known. It was the one thing he had ever been good at.

Two million marks. The Blight Fund. The money for the beacons on the coast, that had failed in the storm. The money for the fishing towns, where the nets came up empty. The money for medicine for the blight-cough, that took the children first. Two million marks, and he had stolen it, and run with it into the hills like a thief, and now he was trading it, a hundred thousand at a time, for bread.

On the sixth day, he signed away the last of it for a heel of bread and a cup of water. The boy with the squint took the letter, and looked at it, and looked at Dorran, lying on the straw, and something in his face changed. For a moment, it almost looked like pity.

"That's all of it, then," said the boy. "Isn't it, my lord?"

"Yes," said Dorran.

The boy went away. He did not come back with food the next day, or the day after.

---

Edric came on the eighth day.

He had not meant to let it go so long. That was the truth, and he made himself look at it. He had arranged it all months ago, in the spring, before he ever came to Solace, sitting in the library at Cristal with Rook's letter in front of him: *if the Lord Treasurer ever comes north through the Greyhills alone, at night, with heavy saddlebags, keep him. Don't hurt him. Don't touch his money. Feed him, for a price. Let him buy his bread with what he stole. Every mark.* He had written the price himself. A hundred thousand marks a meal. He had smiled, writing it. He remembered the smile.

And then the grey house. And the boy on the floor, with the wooden horse in his hand. And the old man's eyes. *Enough.*

He had sent a rider to Rook the day after, with new orders. *Let him go. Give him back his money. All of it.* The rider's horse had gone lame in the hills, and he had come back to Solace on foot, ten days later, with the letter still in his pocket. And Edric, who had been sitting in the dark in the house with the blue door all that time, counting the days on the thread beside his heart, had not known.

When he did know, he rode north himself, through the night, without stopping, and came up the ravine to the ruined monastery at dawn on the eighth day, alone, and walked into the cloister, and Rook saw his face and went pale.

"My lord. I didn't know. Your letter never came. I've done just as you said, my lord, I've not touched a hair of his head. He's had food. He's paid for every bite. He paid the last of it two days ago."

"And since then?"

Rook looked at the ground.

Edric went past him, across the cloister, to the mouth of the cave.

---

Dorran Quell was lying on the straw at the back of the cave, under the blanket, curled on his side, with his face to the wall. He was so thin that the blanket barely seemed to cover anything. He did not turn round when Edric came in.

"Go away," he said. His voice was a thread. "I haven't any more. You've had it all. Go away."

Edric knelt down beside him. He took the jug of water from the floor, and found it empty, and filled it from the flask at his own belt, and held it to the man's lips.

Dorran drank. He drank like an animal, choking, with the water running down his chin. When the jug was empty, he lay back on the straw and looked up, for the first time, at the man kneeling over him.

"You," he said. His eyes were sunk deep in his face, and very bright, and not quite sane. "You. Cristal. The Count. Of course it's you. Of course." He laughed, a dry, papery sound. "I knew. I knew it was you. The drafts. The mirror tower. The note. *Every captain needs to keep good accounts.* I knew someone was watching me. All this time. I could feel it. Every night, at the window. Someone looking back." He closed his eyes. "Who are you? What did I ever do to you? I never saw you before the spring. I never even heard your name."

Edric took a heel of bread out of his coat and broke off a piece and put it in the man's hand.

"Eat," he said. "Slowly."

Dorran ate. His hands were shaking so badly he could barely get the bread to his mouth. Edric watched him. He felt the water in him: thin, and slow, and weak, and very frightened. He felt, under it, the old currents, the ones he had felt in the white marble house: the envy, and the fear, and the cleverness, and the cold. They were all still there. But they had gone quiet. Like a river in a drought, when the water has gone down so far that you can see the stones at the bottom.

"I'm sorry," said Dorran Quell suddenly.

Edric looked at him.

"I don't know what I did to you," said Dorran. "I don't know who you are. But I'm sorry. For whatever it was. I've had eight days, lying here, to think. Nothing to do but think. And I've thought about all of it. Everything. Every mark. Every column." He looked at the bread in his hand. "I stole it. The Blight Fund. Four million marks of it, over eleven years, and the last two million in those saddlebags. Money for the beacons. Money for the fishing towns. I knew what it was for. I always knew. I told myself I'd put it back. I never put it back. I never would have." He was weeping now, without seeming to notice, thin dry tears on his sunken cheeks. "Every morning, for eleven years, I sat on the edge of my bed and looked at the numbers. And my hands shook. Every morning. Every single morning. I've been afraid every day for eleven years. For longer. For nineteen years. Since the day I left Saltmere." He closed his eyes. "I'm so tired of being afraid."

Edric sat back on his heels and looked at him for a long time.

Then he let the stillness go.

---

Dorran saw it. He opened his eyes, and looked up, and saw the Count of Cristal's face change: saw it blur, and clear, and become another face, harder and older and scarred, with grey eyes, and the faint blue lines of the Tidemark on the throat and the cheek. He looked at it for a long moment, without understanding.

"Every captain needs to keep good accounts," said Edric quietly. "You gave me a ledger, Dorran. At my wedding feast. Leather-bound, with my initials on it in gold. I never got to use it."

Dorran's eyes went very wide.

"There was sand in the pepper casks," said Edric. "In the aft hold. On the *Gull's Promise*. I went down and looked, one night before Port Jasmine, and found the sand. And I thought it was the merchants on the Ember Coast. I thought someone had cheated *you*. I was going to tell Moren to watch out for them on the next voyage." He was quiet a moment. "I never even asked you about it. It never occurred to me that you'd done it. I trusted you."

Dorran Quell lay on the straw and looked up at Edric Vane, and his face did something that Edric would remember for the rest of his life. It did not go white; it was already as white as it could go. It did not crumple. It simply opened, all at once, the way a ledger falls open at a page that has been read so many times the spine has broken there, and what was underneath was not fear, or guilt, or shame, but a kind of terrible, weary recognition. The look of a man who has finally, after nineteen years, been caught.

"Edric," he whispered. "Edric Vane."

"Yes."

"I thought you were dead. Valtor said. Years ago. At a dinner. He said the boy died in the Keep." Dorran laughed, or tried to; it came out as a cough. "I was glad. I'm sorry. I was glad. I went home and sat on the edge of my bed and thought: there, that's balanced, that's the end of it. And then I looked at the numbers. And my hands still shook." He closed his eyes. "They never stopped. Even then."

"I know," said Edric.

Dorran opened his eyes and looked at him.

"I know," said Edric again. "I was in a cell under the sea for fourteen years, Dorran. Every morning I woke up in the dark. And every morning I said four names. Yours was the first." He looked down at his own hands, bandaged, on his knees. "I've been afraid too. For nineteen years. Not of being found out. Of what I'd become, if I ever got out. Of what I'd do." He was quiet for a long moment. "I found out, two weeks ago. In a house on the Street of Lanterns. A little boy is dead because of me. Because I wanted to see what someone would do, and I told her how to do it."

Dorran stared at him.

"So I'm not going to do anything to you," said Edric Vane. "I came here to watch you starve. I wrote the price myself. A hundred thousand marks for a heel of bread. I smiled when I wrote it." He took the rest of the bread out of his coat and put it in Dorran's hands. "And I'm not going to. I forgive you, Dorran. Not because you deserve it. Because I need to be forgiven too, and I don't know how else to start."

---

He sent Dorran south three days later, when he could stand, with a horse and a cloak and a purse.

There were fifty thousand marks in the purse. It was the only money Edric gave him. The rest of the two million, every letter of credit Dorran had signed in the cave, Rook brought down out of the hills in a strongbox, untouched, and Edric sent it to Solace, to the Ministry of the Treasury, with an anonymous letter, and a list, in his own hand, of every beacon on the coast that had failed in the storm, and every fishing town between Harnmouth and Saltmere where the nets came up empty, and every hospital where children were dying of the blight-cough. The letter said only: *For the purpose for which it was raised. The Tide remembers.*

Dorran did not ask where he was going. He sat on the horse in the cloister of the ruined monastery, thin as a rail, in a borrowed cloak, with the purse in his lap, and looked down at Edric for a long time.

"Fifty thousand," he said. "It's more than I had when I came to Solace."

"It's what Cade got for my diamond," said Edric. "It seemed right."

Dorran looked at the purse. Then he looked up again.

"What will you do now?" he said. "Now we're all done. Fenrick, and Valtor, and me. And Cade." He hesitated. "What's left?"

Edric looked past him, east, towards Solace, and the white tower on the cliffs, and the gold light burning at its summit, that he could see from here, from the hills, even in daylight. Under his coat, over his heart, the Tidemark had gone very cold.

"One more," he said.

Dorran followed his gaze. He looked at the far, small, burning point of light for a long time. Then he looked back at Edric, and something went across his thin face that was almost, Edric thought, admiration. The look of a clerk seeing a sum so large it was beautiful.

"Gods," said Dorran Quell softly. "You're mad."

"Probably," said Edric.

Dorran almost smiled. Then he turned his horse and rode out of the cloister, south, down the ravine, towards the passes and the border and a city in the far south where nobody knew his name. He did not look back.

---

That night, Edric went down to the sea.

He rode back from the Greyhills to the coast, alone, through the dark, and came out on a headland of white chalk north of Solace where the cliffs fell sheer to the water. The Pale Cliffs, where the boy had walked across the turf at dawn and withdrawn his challenge. He left the horse at the top, and climbed down the face of the cliffs by a goat-path, in the dark, without a lantern, feeling his way by the water in the rock, to a narrow strip of shingle at the bottom, where the waves came in, black and slow, and broke, and drew back, hissing.

He stood at the edge of the water and took off his coat, and his shirt, and stood there in the cold night wind, and looked down at himself.

The Tidemark covered him. Chest, and shoulders, and arms, down to the wrists; throat, and jaw, and the left cheek, almost to the eye. In the dark, it glowed: a pale, shimmering, branching blue, like light at the bottom of clear water, pulsing slowly with his heart.

He thought about the face in the cracked mirror, in the stillroom. The face that had smiled at him, out of his own mouth.

He thought about the book in the library at Cristal, the one he did not open any more. Three tide-taken Wardens who became the greatest of their order. Four who walked into the sea. Two who burned out. And three who became something the books did not like to name.

He thought about an old man in a chair by a window, blinking.

He walked into the sea.

---

It was very cold. It was always very cold, even in summer. He walked out until it was at his waist, and then his chest, and then he stopped, and stood there with the swell lifting and dropping him, and closed his eyes, and breathed.

In for eight. Out for eight.

He found the stillness. He went down into it. And from the stillness, he reached out, not for the sea, but for the part of the sea that was in him.

He could feel it. He had always been able to feel it, since the night under the Teeth: a great cold weight inside him, coiled around his heart and running out along the lines of the Tidemark into every part of him, like water filling the channels of a marsh at high tide. He had never looked at it directly. He had been afraid to. He looked at it now.

It was vast. Far more than he had thought. Five years of drawing on it, of reaching for it, on the beaches at Cristal and in the study in Solace and in the stillroom of the grey house, a little more each time. And each time, a little had stayed. It had not gone back. It had settled in him, like silt settling in a harbour, layer on layer, until the harbour was half full. Until there was more sea in him than he had ever let himself know.

*On the Return*, one of the books had said. A short chapter, near the end, in the Book of the Masters of the House of Cristal, in a hand that shook. *Of the giving back of what has been taken. It is said that a tide-taken Warden may, if he will, return to the Deep a part of what the Deep has poured into him, as a river returns its waters to the sea. It is said to be very painful. It is said that few have ever tried. For the Deep does not ask for its waters back. And the Warden who gives them is the less for it, ever after, in strength, and does not get them again.*

He reached for the cold weight in his chest, the silt of five years, and took hold of it, and *pushed*.

It did not want to go.

He had known it would not. It clung to him the way the blight had clung to the child's lungs at Grey Point, the way weed clings to a rock. It had been in him a long time. It had grown into him. It was in his blood and his breath and his bones. When he pushed at it, it pushed back, cold and heavy and immense, and something underneath it woke, and turned, and looked at him.

*What are you doing, little one?*

The voice. Not in his ears. In the water. In him. Vast and slow and deep, like a swell rolling across an ocean.

*Giving it back,* he said.

*Why?*

He did not answer at once. He stood in the black water with the swell lifting and dropping him, and pushed, and felt it begin, very slowly, to move. To come up out of him, out of the channels of the Tidemark, out of his blood, out of his heart, the way the blight had come up out of the child, and run down his arms and out of his fingers into the sea.

It hurt. The book had been right. It hurt more than anything had ever hurt him, more than the chisel slipping, more than the fall from the cliff, more than the sea in his lungs at the bottom of the world. It was like tearing out his own veins. It was like being flayed.

*Because I killed a child,* he said, through the pain. *Because I looked in a mirror and saw you looking out. Because an old man told me enough.*

The Deep was silent for a long time.

*They poison their own,* it said at last. *They bury their children in the dark. They chained me, little one, and drank me, for three hundred years. You saw. You felt. And you would give back the strength to punish them?*

*I've punished them,* said Edric. *All of them. Fenrick's dead. Valtor's mad. Dorran's gone south with fifty thousand marks and his hands shaking. Cade's dead in an alley.* He pushed, and the cold came out of him, a little at a time, through his fingers, into the black water. *And I'm not sorry, for them. I'll never be sorry, for them. But the boy didn't do anything. Lyse didn't do anything. The old Marquis and the Marquise didn't do anything. Old Noll didn't do anything. And they're dead, or as good as, because I wanted to see what would happen.* He gasped. *That's not justice. That's the sea. A storm that falls on the guilty and the innocent alike. You said it yourself. A wave doesn't ask whether the cliff deserves to fall.*

*No,* said the Deep. *It does not.*

*Well, I do,* said Edric Vane. *I have to. Or I'm nothing. Or I'm just you, wearing a face.*

---

He did not know how long he stood there. Hours. The tide came in, and the water rose to his shoulders, and then his chin, and he did not move. The cold went out of him, a little at a time, through his fingers, into the sea. He felt himself growing lighter. Weaker. Emptier. Like a harbour when the tide goes out, and the water drains away, and leaves the mud and the stones and the weed, bare and cold and naked to the sky.

When he could not push any more, he stopped.

He stood in the black water up to his chin, shaking so hard that his teeth rattled, and opened his eyes.

*You are the first,* said the Deep.

He did not understand.

*The first who ever gave back,* said the Deep, slowly, as if it were thinking about it. *In all the time I have poured myself into your kind. Thousands of your years. They took, and took, and took. Some took a little. Some took a great deal. Some took so much that there was nothing left of them but me. But none of them ever gave back.* The vast slow voice was silent a moment. *Interesting.*

He waded out of the sea.

He fell twice on the shingle and lay there, and got up, and fell again, and crawled the last few yards to where his clothes were, and lay on the stones in the dark with his shirt clutched to his chest, shaking. He was colder than he had ever been, and weaker, and so tired that he could barely lift his head.

But he lifted it, and looked down at himself, in the faint light from the stars.

The Tidemark was still there. It covered his chest, and his shoulders, and his arms. But it had gone back. On his arms, it stopped at the elbow, now, not the wrist. On his throat, it stopped below the jaw. And on his face, he put up a shaking hand and touched it, and felt only skin. Cold skin, wet with seawater. Nothing else.

Beside his heart, very faint, very far away, across the sea, a single thin thread of life lay still and quiet. Holding.

*Twelve,* he thought. *Twelve days left.*

He lay on the shingle at the foot of the Pale Cliffs and looked up at the stars, the same stars, the ones Halloran had taught him and Faro had named. And to the south, along the line of the cliffs, very small, very far, he could see the white tower of the Sunspire rising into the dark, and at its summit, the gold light burning.

It was brighter than usual. He was sure of it. It had been brighter since the storm.

And as he watched, lying there on the cold stones, it moved.

It did not flicker. It did not flare. It simply turned, the way a lantern turns in a watchman's hand, the way an eye turns in a face. And for a moment, just a moment, across the miles of dark sea and white cliff, he felt it look at him.

He felt it find him. He felt it know him. He felt something old and golden and beautiful and very, very hungry, at the top of a white tower, turn its attention towards a man lying on a beach, the way a man turns his attention towards a sound in the next room.

Then the light turned back, and was only a light again.

Edric lay very still on the shingle, with his heart hammering, for a long time.

"There you are," he whispered, to the dark, to the tower, to the light. "There you are."

---

*End of Part Four*
