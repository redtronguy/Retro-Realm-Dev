## Chapter 25: The Trial

Cade Rusk had not done well with the diamond.

Edric had known it would happen. Perhaps not exactly how; but he had known, on that wet afternoon at the Dry Well five years ago, when he put the stone into the fat soft trembling hand and saw the yellow eyes of Cade's wife in the doorway, that nothing good would come of it. He had given it anyway. He had told himself it was a test: that if there was anything in Cade Rusk worth saving, the diamond would find it. He had told himself a good many things, in those days.

He learned the rest of it in pieces, over the years, from Bastien's agents and from the court records of Fendle. The Sardhi jeweller had come through on the market road, the week after the priest's visit, as Agna Rusk had said he would. He had looked at the diamond and offered forty-five thousand marks for it. Cade had taken the money. And in the night, Agna had gone into the jeweller's room with a carving knife, because forty-five thousand marks was a great deal, and the jeweller had ninety thousand more in his strongbox. Cade, waking at the screams, had come in to find the jeweller dead and his wife covered in blood, counting money on the floor.

He had run. They had caught him in a ditch two miles away with the jeweller's strongbox in his arms. Agna had hanged herself in her cell before the trial. Cade had sworn that he had not done it, that he had been asleep, that it was all his wife. Nobody had believed him. He had been sentenced to the King's galleys for life.

And in the galleys, chained to the same oar, he had met a young man with a southern accent and a smile like the sun.

They had escaped together, two years later, when the galley went aground on a sandbar in a storm off the northern coast. They had parted on the beach. And Cade Rusk had drifted from town to town, as men like that do, thieving and begging and drinking, until one day, in a gin-shop near the docks in Solace, he had seen a handsome young man in a fine coat get out of a carriage across the street, with a crest on its door, and heard someone say, in a tone of envy, *that's Lord Andric Cavell, the southern heir, the one who's to marry Baron Quell's daughter*.

Cade had looked at Lord Andric Cavell for a long time. And then he had smiled.

---

"He's been paying Cade," said Bastien, in the study, on the third night after the funeral. His face was like a closed door. "Andric. For three months. Fifty marks a week. To keep his mouth shut about the galleys." He was quiet a moment. "Now Cade wants more. He's told Andric about this house. About the gold. He's heard, in the gin-shops, that the Count of Cristal keeps a fortune in the house with the blue door, in a strongroom at the back, behind the study. He says if Andric helps him get in, they'll split it, and he'll go away, and never bother Andric again."

"And Andric said yes."

"Andric said yes." Bastien looked at him. "Tonight. After midnight. Over the back wall, by the old fig tree, and in through the study window. Cade goes in. Andric waits in the alley below, to take the gold out."

Edric sat at his desk and looked at the bowl of still water, and did not touch it.

"Andric's not going to split anything," said Bastien quietly. "You know that."

"I know."

"He's going to wait in the alley until Cade comes out. And then he's going to kill him. Because Cade's the only man in Solace who knows who he really is." Bastien leaned on the desk. "Shall I stop it?"

Edric looked at the water for a long time.

"No," he said. "I'll deal with Cade myself. Go to bed, Bastien."

Bastien looked at him for a long moment with his black eyes. Then he went out, and did not go to bed. Edric heard him, later, sitting on the stairs outside the study door, in the dark, with a knife in his hand.

---

Cade came over the wall a little after one.

Edric heard him. He heard the fig tree creak, and the grunt as a heavy man dropped into the garden, and the scuffle of feet on the gravel, and the window of the study, which had been left unlatched, being eased open. He was sitting in the dark, in the chair by the cold fireplace, in a grey robe, with a little brass lantern on a cord round his neck, unlit, and his face soft and blurred and forgettable.

Cade came through the window, puffing, and stood on the floor of the study in the dark, and fumbled for his dark-lantern, and opened the shutter.

The thin beam of light swung across the room. Across the desk. The shelves. The door to the strongroom, which was exactly where the gin-shops had said it was. The fireplace. The chair. The figure in the chair.

Cade froze.

"Good evening, friend," said Brother Faro.

---

Cade did not run. Afterwards, Edric thought it was the only brave thing he had ever seen the man do. He stood in the middle of the study with the lantern shaking in his hand and the light jittering all over the walls, and stared at the priest in the chair, and did not run.

"You," he said. His voice was a whisper. "You. The priest. From the inn. From the Dry Well."

"Yes."

"What are you doing here?"

"I live here," said Brother Faro. "Sometimes."

Cade stared at him. Then something went across his face, a slow, sick, dawning understanding, the same look Edric had seen five years ago in the taproom of the Dry Well, when he let the stillness drop for a moment at the door. The lantern began to shake so hard that the light was a blur.

"No," said Cade. "No. No, no, no. You're not. You can't be. It was the beer. It was the firelight. You're a priest. You're Brother Faro. He's dead. You said he was dead. You sat with him. You *said*."

Edric let the stillness go.

He did not do it suddenly. He let it go slowly, the way a tide goes out over sand, so that Cade could watch it happen: the soft vague priest's face growing clearer, sharper, harder, the lines coming into it, the scar through the eyebrow, the grey eyes, until it was Edric Vane's face looking at him out of the dark, by the light of his own trembling lantern.

Cade dropped the lantern. It hit the floor and rolled, and the light went spinning, and he went down on his knees on the floorboards, heavily, like a sack of meal, and put his hands over his face.

"Edric," he said through his fingers. "Edric. Edric. Gods have mercy. Gods have mercy on me."

Edric sat in the chair and looked at him.

"I gave you a diamond, Cade," he said quietly. "Five years ago. Fifty thousand marks. Enough to live on for the rest of your life. Enough to do some good with. I told you to balance the account." He was quiet a moment. "What did you do with it?"

Cade wept.

"I didn't kill him," he said. "The jeweller. I swear I didn't. It was Agna. I was asleep. I swear it, Edric, I swear on my mother's grave, I never touched him. I never touched anyone in my life." He lifted his wet, sagging, terrified face. "I never did anything. That's the thing. That's always been the thing. I never *did* anything. I just. I just didn't stop it. Any of it. Not the letter. Not the jeweller. Not anything." He wiped his face on his sleeve. "Is that so bad? Is that the worst thing a man can do?"

"You asked me that before," said Edric. "At the inn."

"And you said it wasn't the worst. But it wasn't nothing." Cade laughed, a horrible wet sound. "Well, it's not nothing, is it? It's never been nothing. It's been everything. My whole life." He looked at the floor. "What are you going to do to me?"

Edric looked at him for a long time: at the fat, soft, broken man kneeling on the floor of the study in the light of his own fallen lantern. He felt the water in him. Weak, and sluggish, and frightened, and tired. So tired. A man who had been running from trouble for fifty years and had finally run out of places to go.

He thought of Andric, waiting in the alley below the window, in the dark.

He thought: *I should tell him. I should tell him not to go out that way. I should keep him here until morning, and send him out by the front door, with Bastien.*

He thought: *He stood in the Three Anchors and watched them write it, and went home to bed, and lay awake listening to my father snore, and said nothing, for nineteen years.*

He thought: *Let him go out the way he came.*

"Nothing, Cade," he said. "I'm not going to do anything to you. Get up."

Cade looked up at him, uncomprehending.

"Get up," said Edric. "Go back out of the window you came in by. Go back to whatever hole you crawled out of. Go away from Solace, and never come back, and never speak my name to anyone, and try, for whatever's left of your life, to do one thing, just one, that isn't nothing." He stood. "That's all. Go."

Cade got up, slowly, staring at him. He backed towards the window. At the sill, he stopped.

"Edric," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. For all of it. I want you to know that. I've been sorry every day for nineteen years."

"I know," said Edric. "Go."

And Cade Rusk climbed out of the window of the study of the house with the blue door, puffing, and lowered himself down the wall by the old fig tree, and dropped into the dark alley behind the garden.

Edric stood by the window. He did not look out. He stood with his back to the glass, and his eyes closed, and his hands at his sides, and listened.

He heard Cade land in the alley, grunting. He heard a voice, low, young, pleasant, with a southern accent. *There you are. Where's the gold?* He heard Cade's voice, shaking, saying something about a priest, a ghost, the house was haunted, they had to get away. He heard the young voice laugh.

And then he heard the knife.

It was a very small sound. Three small sounds, quick, like a man punching a sack of flour. And then a long, soft, bubbling sigh, and the sound of something heavy sliding down a wall.

And footsteps, light and quick, going away down the alley into the dark.

---

He was down in the alley before Bastien was off the stairs.

Cade was lying against the garden wall under the fig tree, half sitting, with his legs stretched out in front of him and his hands pressed to his belly, where the blood was coming out between his fingers, black in the starlight. His face was grey. His eyes were open.

He saw Edric kneel beside him, and something moved in the grey face, something that was almost a smile.

"Edric," he said. Very faint. Bubbling. "He was waiting. The boy. Andric. I should have known. I should have known."

"Don't talk." Edric put his hands over Cade's, over the wounds. He felt the blood: hot, and fast, and going. He felt the water in Cade's body running out of him through the three holes in his belly, like the tide going out through a broken sluice. He reached for it. He tried to hold it, the way he had held Lyse's, the way the books said, to make it still, to stop the bleeding.

It was too much. Too fast. It ran through his stillness like water through fingers.

"Andric," whispered Cade. "His name's not Cavell. It's not. We were on the galleys together. Chained to the same oar. Two years." He coughed, and blood came up. "He told me once. On the oar. At night. He said he was born in a garden. Buried in a box. Dug up by a stranger. He thought it was funny. He laughed." His hand moved, feebly, under Edric's. "He's the High Justicar's son. Valtor's. He found out. Years ago. From the woman who raised him, before he burned her. He's been waiting. Waiting for the right time to—to—"

He stopped.

"Cade," said Edric.

The grey face turned towards him. The small, wet, frightened eyes looked at him, and for a moment, just a moment, they were not frightened at all. Only very tired. And very surprised.

"I said something," said Cade Rusk. "Didn't I? Just then. I told you. I said something."

"Yes," said Edric. "You did."

"Well," said Cade. "Well." And the tired, surprised look stayed on his face, and the breath went out of him, very slowly, like a sigh, and did not come back.

Edric knelt in the alley with his hands on the dead man's belly, and the blood cooling between his fingers, and did not move for a long time.

Behind him, Bastien Corso stood in the mouth of the alley with his knife in his hand, looking at the dead man, and then at the dark end of the alley where the footsteps had gone. He did not say anything. His face, in the starlight, was the face of a man looking at a debt that has finally come due, twenty-three years late, and in the wrong coin.

---

The betrothal of Lord Andric Cavell to Mistress Elsabet Quell was to be signed three days later, at noon, in the great hall of the Lord Treasurer's house of white marble on the great terrace below the Sunspire.

All of Solace was there. Dorran had made sure of it. He had spent money he did not have on the flowers alone, and on the musicians, and the wine, and the gold leaf on the contract, which lay on a table of white marble at the end of the great hall with a quill beside it and a pot of gold ink. He needed this marriage. He needed it more than he had needed anything in his life. Lord Andric Cavell would come into his fortune in two years, a fortune held in trust in Port Jasmine, a fortune that would fill the hole in the Blight Fund twice over. Until then, the betrothal alone would steady the bank. People would see that the Lord Treasurer's daughter was to marry the richest young man in the south, and they would leave their money where it was, and stop asking questions.

He stood at the end of the great hall in a coat of gold silk, with his wife beside him in grey, and smiled his small smile at everyone, and his hands shook.

Elsabet stood beside the marble table in white, with her chin up and her face like stone. She had told her father, three times, that she did not want to marry Lord Andric Cavell, or anyone. She had told him she wanted to go south, to the music schools of Port Jasmine, and study the harp, and play in the great halls there, and never marry at all. He had told her not to be a fool. She had stopped telling him.

Lord Andric Cavell came in at a quarter to noon, in a coat of midnight blue, smiling. He bowed to the Baron, and kissed the Baroness's hand, and turned to Elsabet with his smile like the sun coming out, and said something charming. Elsabet looked at him as if he were a slug on a lettuce. The guests laughed indulgently.

The Count of Cristal was there too. He stood near the doors, in black, with Hayda beside him in white, and watched.

He was watching Baroness Quell.

She had been watching Andric since he came in. Watching him with an expression that she seemed unaware of, that no one else in the hall seemed to notice: a strange, fixed, hungry, frightened look, the look of a woman who has seen a face she knows in a crowd and cannot remember where. She had been watching him like that, Edric thought, since the night at Larkmere, under the chestnut tree. She did not know. She could not know. But something in her, some very old, very deep current that had been frozen for twenty-three years, knew something, and was trying to tell her.

At noon precisely, the notary rose, and read the contract aloud, and dipped the quill in the gold ink, and held it out to Lord Andric Cavell.

And the great doors at the end of the hall opened, and a captain of the Crown Guard came in, in white and gold, with six men behind him and a paper in his hand.

---

It was exactly like the Feast of Lamps. Edric would think that afterwards, and not be able to stop thinking it. The doors opening. The ringing tread of soldiers' boots on marble. The guests shrinking back. The sudden, total silence. The paper.

"I am looking," said the captain of the Guard, "for a man calling himself Lord Andric Cavell."

Andric did not move. He stood by the marble table with the gold quill in his hand and his smile still on his face, and looked at the captain with polite interest.

"I am Lord Andric Cavell," he said. "How can I help you, Captain?"

"I have a warrant for your arrest," said the captain, "signed by the High Justicar's deputy, for the murder of one Cade Rusk, an escaped galley convict, in an alley behind the Street of Lanterns, four nights ago. And for being yourself an escaped galley convict, under the name of Andric, no family, sentenced at Harrowgate six years ago for robbery and arson." He looked at the paper. "There's a witness. A man saw you in the alley. And Rusk named you, before he died."

There was a sound in the great hall like the sea pulling back over shingle.

Andric looked at the captain for a long moment. His smile did not change at all. Then he looked past the captain, down the length of the hall, to the doors, where the Count of Cristal stood in black.

Their eyes met.

And Edric saw it, then, for the first time: something in those laughing dark eyes. Not fear. Not anger. Understanding. The look of a clever boy who has finally worked out how the conjuror did the trick.

Andric smiled at him. A small, private, appreciative smile, like one gambler to another across a table.

Then he threw the gold quill in the captain's face, and vaulted the marble table, and went through the long window behind it in a shower of glass, and was gone, down the terraced garden of the Lord Treasurer's house, towards the cliffs.

---

They caught him on the road north two days later, at an inn in the hills, asleep, with a stolen horse in the stable and a great deal of money that was not his in his saddlebags.

Elsabet Quell did not wait to find out. She left her father's house of white marble on the night of the betrothal, with her harp and a small bag and a friend from her music lessons, a quiet girl called Louisa who played the viol, and took the public coach south to Harnmouth, and a ship to Port Jasmine. She left a note on her pillow. It said: *I will not be sold. Not even to a real lord. Goodbye, Father.*

Dorran read it standing in her empty room. Then he went back to his counting-house, and sat down at his desk, and looked at the numbers, and did not go home for three days.

---

The High Justicar of Valemont prosecuted the case himself.

He insisted on it. The High Justicar did not, as a rule, appear in court; he had deputies for that. But he went to the Chamber of Lords and to the King, and said that the murder of a man in an alley beside his own house, by a convict who had deceived the whole of Solace society, struck at the heart of the King's order, and that he would see justice done in person. Everyone agreed it was very fine of him. Some people, the ones who whispered, said it was because he needed to be seen doing something very fine, after the death of his daughter, and the rumours that would not go away about the deaths in his house.

The trial was held in the Great Court of the Hall of Justice, under the Sunspire, three days after his capture. The High Justicar would not wait. Half of Solace came. Edric came. He sat in the gallery, in black, with Hayda beside him, and watched Corvan Valtor stand up in his black robe and his gold chain of office and lay out the case against the prisoner in his clear, precise, terrible voice, every word placed like a stone.

He felt the thread of Lyse Valtor's life beside his heart, very faint now, very far away, across the sea, in the library at Cristal. Still. Holding.

*Twenty-eight,* he thought. *Twenty-eight days.*

The prisoner sat in the dock in chains, in a plain grey shirt, with his dark curls combed and his handsome face composed, and listened to it all with an expression of polite, faintly amused interest, as if he were watching a play. When the witnesses spoke, the man from the alley, the captain of the Guard, a gaoler from Harrowgate, he listened to them attentively, and nodded at the good bits. When the High Justicar described the murder, the three thrusts of the knife, the dying man in the alley naming his killer, Andric smiled slightly, as if at a fond memory.

At last, the presiding judge, an old man with a face like a walnut, turned to the dock.

"The prisoner will rise," he said. "And state his name, and his age, and his place of birth, and his parentage, for the record of this court, before he answers the charge."

Andric rose. His chains clinked. He looked around the Great Court, at the judges on their high bench, and the lawyers in their robes, and the packed galleries, and the High Justicar standing six paces away in his black robe and his gold chain. He looked at all of it with his laughing dark eyes. And then he smiled, the smile like the sun coming out, and Edric, in the gallery, felt the whole court lean towards it, the way a field of flowers leans towards the light.

"My name," said the prisoner, "is Andric. I have no other. The woman who raised me gave me that one, and I've never found a better. My age is twenty-three years. I was born at a country house called Larkmere, an hour's ride from this city, on a night in the spring, twenty-three years ago, a little before midnight."

There was a murmur in the galleries. Edric did not look at the galleries. He was looking at Valtor.

The High Justicar had not moved. He was standing quite still, six paces from the dock, with his hands clasped behind his back. But Edric could feel him. He could feel the water in him, every drop, gone suddenly, absolutely still. Frozen. The way it had frozen under the chestnut tree at Larkmere.

"I was born too early," said Andric conversationally. "I'm told I wasn't breathing. My mother was told I was dead. And my father, who was a young man then, very clever, very ambitious, with a great deal to lose if anyone ever found out about me, took me out into the garden at Larkmere, in a little oak box, in the dark, and dug a hole under the big chestnut tree at the end of the garden, and put me in it, and filled it in." He smiled. "I wasn't dead, as it turns out. A stranger dug me up. A man with a knife, who'd come to kill my father, and stayed to see what he was burying. He gave me to his sister-in-law, who raised me. She told me all about it, when I was fifteen. She thought I ought to know." His smile widened a little. "I burned her house down a week later. But I've always been grateful to her for that."

The court was utterly silent.

"The prisoner," said the old judge, in a strange voice, "will state the name of his father."

Andric turned his head. Slowly, gracefully, the way a dancer turns. And looked at the man in the black robe standing six paces away.

"My father," he said, "is the High Justicar of Valemont. Corvan Valtor." He made a small bow. "Good morning, Father. It's very good of you to prosecute me in person. I've always wanted us to spend some time together."

---

Nobody breathed.

Edric would remember it afterwards as the longest silence he had ever heard: longer than any night in the Keep, longer than the silence at the bottom of the sea. The whole of the Great Court, the judges, the lawyers, the guards, the galleries packed to the rafters with half of Solace, all of them frozen, staring at the thin grey upright man in the black robe and the gold chain, and waiting for him to laugh. To say it was a lie, a monstrous lie, the last desperate trick of a murderer. To have the prisoner removed. To go on.

Valtor did not laugh.

He stood six paces from the dock and looked at his son. And Edric, in the gallery, with the sea in him, felt the ice crack.

It did not crack all at once. It went slowly, from the edges in, the way ice goes on a river in spring: a long, deep, groaning splitting, somewhere far down, and then another, and another, and then the whole frozen surface of the man beginning to heave and buckle and break apart, and underneath, rushing up through the cracks, black and cold and twenty-three years deep, everything he had ever buried.

"Is there," said the prisoner pleasantly, "a mother, too? I'm told there was. A lady of this city. Married, at the time, to someone else. But I won't say her name. She at least never tried to kill me." His eyes moved, idly, up to the galleries. To the third gallery, the front row, where a tall, handsome, grey-haired woman in grey silk sat beside an empty seat where her husband should have been, with her hand pressed to her mouth, and her face the colour of chalk. "I think she might be here. I think she knows."

Hermione Quell stood up, and made a sound, and fell.

People were crying out now. People were on their feet. The old judge was banging his gavel. In the third gallery, ladies were crowding round the fallen Baroness. Guards were moving, uncertainly.

And in the middle of it all, in the well of the Great Court, Corvan Valtor raised his hand.

Not high. Only to the height of his shoulder. But something in the gesture, in the man, in his face, made the noise die. Made everyone stop. Made them turn, and look at him, and wait.

"It's true," said the High Justicar of Valemont.

He said it quite quietly. His voice did not rise. It was clear, and even, and precise, every word placed like a stone, exactly as it had always been. But something had gone out of it. Something had gone out of his face, too: the stillness. The thirty years of stillness. It was simply gone, like a mask taken off, and what was underneath was the face of a man of forty-seven who had not slept in a month.

"It's true," he said again. "All of it. I buried him. Under the chestnut tree at Larkmere. Twenty-three years ago. I thought he was dead." He looked at his son in the dock, and his mouth moved, as if he were trying to say something else, and could not find the words. "I think I knew he wasn't. I think I've always known."

He took off his gold chain of office, slowly, and laid it on the clerk's table in front of the judges' bench, and the sun at its centre caught the light from the high windows and flashed once.

"I resign my office," he said to the old judge. "I place myself in the hands of this court. I'll answer for it. For all of it." He turned towards the doors. "But not today. Not now. Forgive me. There's something I have to do at home."

And he walked out of the Great Court of the Hall of Justice, between the benches, through a crowd that parted for him in silence, and nobody tried to stop him.

In the dock, his son watched him go, with his laughing dark eyes, and smiled.

---

Edric followed him.

He did not decide to. He was simply on his feet, and out of the gallery, and down the stairs, and through the crowd, with Hayda behind him calling his name. He came out of the Hall of Justice into the bright cold noon, under the Sunspire, and the light hit him like a hammer, the way it always did at noon, at the foot of the tower, and he gasped and pressed his hand to his chest and kept going.

He could see Valtor ahead of him, a thin black figure walking very fast along the great terrace towards the Street of Lanterns. Then running. The High Justicar of Valemont, running through the streets of Solace in his black robe, bareheaded, like a madman, and people turning to stare.

*There's something I have to do at home.*

Edric knew. He knew before Valtor reached the grey house. He had felt it that morning, in the gallery, when the High Justicar stood up to prosecute: a current in him, under the ice, something cold and terrible and decided. He had felt it, and not understood it, and now he did.

Valtor had found the stillroom. He must have. Some time in the last month, after the funeral, after the rumours, after the physician, he had gone up to the room at the top of the house where his wife kept her copper stills and her glass jars and her bunches of dried herbs, and he had found the phials. And he had gone to her. And he had not called the Guard, or a judge, or a priest. Because a trial would have ruined him. Because the High Justicar's wife on the gallows would have been the end of everything. Because order mattered more than anything, more than truth, more than his daughter, more than his father, more than anything.

He had given her a choice. Edric knew it as surely as if he had heard it. *You have until I come home from the Great Court. If you're still alive when I come home, I'll take you to the Guard myself.*

And then he had gone to the Great Court to prosecute a murderer in the name of the King's justice, and his son had stood up in the dock and told the truth.

And now he was running home to stop her.

---

The door of the grey house was standing open when Edric reached it. There was a servant in the hall, a maid, standing quite still in the middle of the floor with her apron over her face. There was a sound coming from upstairs. A man's voice. Not words.

Edric went up.

The door of the stillroom at the top of the house was open. The room was full of the smell of alcohol and bitterness, and something sweet underneath. The copper stills gleamed in the light from the window. The glass jars stood in their rows on the shelves. And on the floor, among them, lay a small, pretty, plump woman in a pale grey gown, on her back, with her fair hair spread around her head and her pale eyes open, staring at the ceiling, and her lips blue, and a little froth at the corner of her mouth.

And beside her, curled up on his side, with his head on her arm, as if he had fallen asleep there, lay a fat, fair, solemn little boy of nine.

Valtor was on his knees between them. He had the boy in his arms. He was holding him, rocking him, the way you rock a baby, and making the sound.

On the table by the window, there was a note, in a neat, pretty, rounded hand. Edric read it without picking it up.

*You told me to choose. I have chosen. But I will not leave my son to you. Not to be the son of a murderess, and the son of a man who would let his own daughter die rather than face a scandal. Not to grow up in this house, with you. He is coming with me. You did this, Corvan. Remember that. You did this.*

Edric stood in the doorway and looked at the boy.

He was very small. Smaller than he had looked at the dinner table, staring at Edric with his mother's pale eyes and asking if it was true that he had killed a sea-monster with his bare hands. His fair hair was damp and curled on his forehead. His lips were blue. One of his hands had fallen open on the floor, palm up, and in it, for some reason, there was a little carved wooden horse.

*Show me,* he had said. And been sent to bed.

---

The sea came up in Edric Vane like a wall.

He had felt it rise before. On the beach at Cristal, killing the blighted things. In the taproom of the Dry Well, listening to Cade. In the study at night, with his hands on the bowl, watching Dorran stand at his window. It had risen in him, cold and eager, and he had held it, and breathed, and put it down.

This was not like that.

This came up from the very bottom of him, from the place where the Tidemark began, over his heart, and it did not rise. It *burst*. It came up through him like the sea through a broken dyke, all at once, the whole cold black weight of it, three hundred years of it, and there was nothing in the way. Nothing to hold it. Nothing at all.

The glass jars on the shelves exploded.

All of them, at once, every one: a sound like a hundred bells shattering. Liquid sprayed across the room. The copper stills burst at their seams and gushed. The water in the jug on the windowsill leapt out of it in a column and hung in the air, trembling. Below them, all through the grey house, he felt it, and heard it: the water in the pipes, the water in the cisterns, the water in the vases and the basins and the kitchen pots, all of it heaving, rising, bursting out. Windows cracked. Somewhere downstairs, a maid was screaming.

And outside, over the Street of Lanterns, over the great terrace, over the whole white city of Solace on its cliffs, the sky went dark.

He felt it go. He felt the sea, beyond the cliffs, rise up. He felt the clouds come pouring in off the water from nowhere, black and boiling, blotting out the noon sun. He felt the wind come, howling, off the sea, up the face of the cliffs, through the streets. He felt the rain begin, all at once, a hammering sheet of it, and the lightning, and the thunder rolling across the city like cannon.

He did not care. He did not feel anything at all except the sea, and under the sea, rising through it, a voice.

*Yes.*

*Yes, little one. This. This is what they are. Look at them. Look at what they do. They bury their children in the dark. They poison their own. They chain what they cannot own, and drink it, and call it order. And you would keep your little words? Justice. Revenge. Look at the child, little one. Look at the child and tell me what those words mean.*

*More. Take more. Take it all. I will give it to you. Drown them. Drown this house, and this street, and this city of white stone. Drown the golden one on his tower. Drown them all, the guilty and the innocent, as they drowned mine. And then come home to me.*

He took a step into the room. He did not know he had done it. There was a mirror on the wall, beside the door, a small square of silvered glass that Heloise Valtor had used to check her face, and he saw himself in it as he passed.

He did not know the face.

The Tidemark covered it. The whole of it. Pale blue branching lines like the veins of a leaf, like rivers seen from a great height, spreading across his cheeks, his brow, his jaw, glowing, pulsing, bright as lamps. And his eyes. His eyes had gone dark. Not grey any more. Not any colour he had a name for. The colour of the sea at the very bottom of the world, where the light has never been: a blue so deep that it was almost black, and in its depths, very far down, things moving. Currents. Shapes. A whole ocean, seen from above.

It looked out at him from the mirror, out of his own face. And it smiled.

---

He would never know, afterwards, what stopped him.

Perhaps it was the face in the mirror. Perhaps it was the thing smiling out of it, with his mouth.

Perhaps it was Valtor, still on his knees on the floor, still rocking the dead boy in his arms, who had not looked up once, not at the bursting jars, not at the storm, not at the water pouring down the walls. Who was still making the sound.

Or perhaps it was the thread.

He felt it. Very faint, very far away, beside his heart, under the flood. A single thin thread of life, still and quiet, going away from him across the sea to a room in a library on an island in the west, where a girl lay sleeping on a bed with her hands folded on her breast. *Forty days. If the Warden loses it, if he lets himself be carried too far, if the sea in him rises too high and sweeps it away—*

He felt the flood pulling at it. Felt it begin to slip.

*No.*

He reached for it. Not for the sea. For the thread. He reached down into the flood, into the black roaring cold of it, and closed himself around that single thin thread the way a drowning man closes his hand around a rope, and held on.

*Lyse.*

*Forty days. I swore it. The sea hears.*

*Not like this. Not like this.*

*Faro. Faro, help me.*

And somewhere, in the roaring dark, very faint, very far away, an old man's voice, thin and patient and cross, the voice that had come through a wall twenty years ago, saying: *Breathe, you fool. In for eight. Out for eight. Find the stillness. It's always there. You only have to go deep enough.*

He breathed.

In for eight. It was like breathing water. It was like breathing stone.

Out for eight.

In for eight.

He found the stillness. It was very small, and very deep, and very far down, at the bottom of everything, under the flood: a single still place, like the eye of a storm, like a pool in a cave. He went down into it. He held onto the thread, and he went down into the stillness, and he stayed there, and breathed, and breathed, and let the flood roar over him and around him and through him, and did not let it take him.

And slowly, slowly, it began to go down.

---

The storm over Solace lasted an hour.

Afterwards, people would talk about it for years: the day the sky went black at noon, and the sea rose up the cliffs, and every pipe and cistern and fountain in the Street of Lanterns burst at once. The priests of the Radiant Order said it was a portent. The fishermen in the harbour below the cliffs said they had never seen the sea do anything like it, not in a lifetime: rising up the face of the cliffs in great slow swells, as if something underneath it were trying to climb out. Some of the old people, the ones who remembered stories their grandparents had told, said nothing at all, and went home, and sat by their fires, and were afraid.

And then it stopped. As suddenly as it had come. The wind dropped. The rain thinned and ceased. The clouds broke apart and blew away out to sea, and the noon sun came back, pale and watery, over a city that was soaked and shaken and steaming. And at the top of the Sunspire, the gold light, which had burned all through the storm without flickering, flared once, very bright, as if something had noticed.

In the stillroom at the top of the grey house, Edric Vane knelt on the wet floor among the broken glass, with his hands flat on the boards and his head bowed, breathing.

In for eight. Out for eight.

Beside his heart, the thread held. Very faint. Very far. But it held.

He lifted his head, slowly, and looked at the mirror on the wall. The glass had cracked across, in the storm. In the cracked glass, a man looked back at him: grey-faced, soaked, shaking, with the Tidemark glowing on his throat and climbing his left cheek almost to the eye, as it had that morning. No further. His eyes were grey.

His own eyes. Grey.

He looked away from the mirror, at the boy on the floor.

He crawled across the wet boards, through the broken glass, and knelt by the small body, and put his hand on the boy's forehead, the way he had put it on Lyse's. He reached for him with Still Water, as gently as he could, the way you put your hand into a pool. He felt for the waters in him: the blood, the breath, a thread, anything.

There was nothing. Nothing at all. Only stillness. The real stillness. The one there is no waking from.

He knelt there with his hand on the dead boy's forehead, and for the first time in twenty years, since the night in Faro's cell when the old man put his thin arm across his shoulders, Edric Vane wept. He did not try to stop. He knelt in the broken glass beside the child he had killed, because he had killed him, as surely as if he had poured the phial himself, the night he stood in this room and told a smiling woman the name of a poison because he wanted to see what she would do. He wept, and could not stop, and did not try.

Valtor had stopped making the sound. He was looking at Edric now: really looking, for the first time, across his son's body, with his ruined grey face and his eyes red and wild.

"Who are you?" he said hoarsely. "Who are you? What are you? You did this. The storm. The water. I saw. It was you." He was trembling all over. "Who *are* you?"

Edric lifted his head and looked at him. He did not hold the stillness. There was no stillness left to hold.

"Look at me, Corvan," he said. "Look at me properly. A warm study, in Saltmere. A fire. A boy on the other side of your desk, nineteen years old, with a dead man's letter in his sea-chest, addressed to your father. *I've done you a kindness*, you said. And put it in the fire."

Valtor stared at him.

He saw it reach him. He saw it go into the man like a blade into a wound that was already open. He saw the High Justicar's face, which had broken that morning in the Great Court, break again, further, into something that was no longer a face at all.

"Edric Vane," whispered Corvan Valtor. "Edric. Vane." And then he began to laugh.

It was the worst sound Edric had ever heard. Worse than the sound he had been making before. Worse than Cade dying in the alley, or the boy on the floor, or the voice in the flood. It went on and on, high and cracked and breathless, while he knelt on the wet floor with his dead son in his arms and his dead wife beside him and the man he had buried alive kneeling across from him in the broken glass.

"Edric Vane," he said, through the laughter. "Of course. Of course. Who else? The one I buried. The one I put in the dark, to keep it all safe. To keep it all in order." He laughed and laughed. "And the other one. My son. My other son. I buried him too. Under the tree. I put them both in the dark. And they both climbed out." He looked down at the boy in his arms. "And this one. This one I didn't bury. This one I kept. This one I loved. And she took him. She took him into the dark." He bent over the small body, and the laughter turned into something else. "I have to find them. I have to find them. They're in the garden. They're under the tree. I have to dig. I have to find them before it's too late—"

He got up, with the boy in his arms, and stumbled past Edric, out of the stillroom, down the stairs. Edric heard him go out through the back of the house into the little grey walled garden behind it. And then, after a moment, a sound: a scraping, a tearing, a man digging in wet earth with his bare hands.

He did not follow. He knelt in the broken glass in the stillroom, beside the dead woman, with his face wet, and listened.

---

He found the old man's room on his way out.

He had not meant to go there. But the door was open, and the old man was sitting in his chair by the window, where the servants had left him that morning before the trial, in the sun that had come back after the storm. His rug had slipped off his knees. Nobody had been to him. Nobody had told him anything. But his dark eyes were open, and fixed on the door, and on Edric in it.

Edric went in and knelt beside the chair and picked up the rug and laid it back over the old man's knees.

Severin Valtor looked at him. At his wet face. At the Tidemark. At his hands, which were cut from the broken glass and bleeding.

There was no one to hold the board. Edric picked it up from the table beside the chair, and held it up himself, and ran his finger along the rows of letters, slowly, the way old Noll used to. And the old man blinked.

*E-N-O-U-G-H.*

Edric looked at him.

The old man's eyes held his. Very dark, very bright, swimming with tears that ran down his ruined face and that he could not lift a hand to wipe.

*E-N-O-U-G-H.*

*E-D-R-I-C.*

*E-N-O-U-G-H.*

Edric put down the board, and bowed his head, and put it against the old man's knee, under the rug, and stayed there, for a long time, while the sun came in through the window, and in the garden below, a madman dug in the wet earth with his bare hands, looking for his children.
