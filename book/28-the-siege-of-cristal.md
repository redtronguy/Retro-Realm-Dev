## Chapter 28: The Siege of Cristal

They raised Cristal at sunset on the tenth day, and the fleet was already there.

Edric did not see it at first. He did not see anything. He had been standing at the bow of the *Faro* for ten days and nine nights with his hands on the rail and the sea pouring through him, driving her west, and somewhere in the last two days, he had stopped being able to see. Not blind, exactly. But the world had gone grey and far away, like a world seen through deep water, and the only thing that was real was the sea under the keel, and the cold, and the thread beside his heart, very faint now, so faint he could barely feel it, holding, holding.

He heard Rashid shout from the wheel. He heard Hayda's voice beside him, very close, saying his name. He felt her hand on his arm.

"Edric. Edric, look. Look."

He made himself look.

The island rose out of the sea ahead of them, gold in the last of the sun: the cliffs white with birds, the terraces climbing to the citadel on the summit with its broken dome. He saw the harbour tower at the mouth of the hook-shaped harbour. He saw the walls along the shore, and the Weir-folk's stilt-houses at the inner end of the harbour.

And he saw, lying in a long curve across the mouth of the harbour, a mile out, twelve ships.

They were white. White hulls and white sails, with golden suns blazing on every sail, and on every deck, mounted on great swivels of brass, something that caught the last of the sunset and threw it back in blinding flashes: mirrors. Great curved mirrors, twelve feet across, like the mirrors on the towers that carried the King's word across Valemont. But these were not for messages. These were for gathering the sun, all day, and holding it, and pouring it out again as fire.

As he watched, one of them turned. It swung on its brass swivel, slowly, and tilted, and caught the very last of the sun as it touched the horizon, and threw it.

A beam of gold light leapt across the mile of water like a spear and struck the harbour tower at the mouth of the hook.

The stone glowed. Then it ran. He saw it: the white stone of the old tower, that the Tidewardens had built a thousand years ago and Jac's drunken mason had rebuilt, glowing red, then orange, then white, and then running down the face of the tower like wax, like water, in a long bright smoking stream into the sea, where it hissed and steamed. The light at the top of the tower went out.

Then the sun went down, and the beam faded, and the mirror went dark.

"They got here today," said Rashid hoarsely, from the wheel. "This afternoon. A few hours ago. We're too late."

"No," said Edric. "We're not."

---

They came into Cristal by the back way, through the sea-tunnel under the island where the cave opened to the sea, in the dark of the moon, with the tide nearly at the full and the arch of the tunnel barely clearing the *Faro*'s mastheads. Edric brought her in himself, with the sea, the way he had brought the *Gull's Promise* through the Elbow at Saltmere twenty years before: without a pilot, without slowing, with a yard to spare on either side. He brought her into the great sea-cave under the citadel, where the water sang, and the alcoves that had once held the treasure of the Tidewardens stood empty now, and the *Slip* was already lying at anchor in the black water, with lanterns at her stern.

Jac Teague was on the ledge above the water, with a lantern in her hand and a cutlass on her hip, waiting. She had felt him coming. Everyone on Cristal who listened to the sea had felt him coming, she said afterwards, for the last two days: like a storm on the horizon, getting closer.

She watched him come down the gangway. She watched him stumble on the ledge, and catch himself on the rock. She watched him straighten, and look up at her, with his grey face and his grey eyes and the Tidemark gone back to his throat.

"Gods," said Jac. "You look like a corpse that's been dug up twice."

"Lyse," said Edric.

"Upstairs. In the library. On your bed. Just like you left her." Jac's face changed. "She's cold, Edric. Colder every day. I've been keeping the fire going day and night, and wrapping her in every blanket on the island, and she's cold as ice. And this morning, I put a mirror to her lips, the way you said not to, because I couldn't stand it any more." She swallowed. "Nothing. Not a breath. I thought she was gone."

"She's not," said Edric. "Not yet. What day is it?"

Jac told him.

He had counted it on the thread every hour of every day since the night he put his hand on Lyse Valtor's forehead in the dark room on the Street of Lanterns. He knew what day it was. He had only needed to hear someone else say it.

*Forty.*

---

He went up the stairs from the sea-cave to the citadel at a run, and fell twice, and got up, and Maxim Moren was beside him by the second fall, white-faced, holding him up.

"My lord. My lord, what is it? What's in the library? Why are we here? You said thirty days, you said wait, you said hope, what is it?"

Edric did not answer. He did not have the breath.

The library at the top of the citadel was warm, warmer than it had ever been, with a great fire roaring in the hearth that Jac had built where the old Tidewardens' hearth had been, and the shelves of books glowing in the firelight, and the wind coming in faintly through the broken dome above. And on the bed in the corner, under a mound of blankets and furs, a girl lay on her back with her dark hair spread on the pillow and her hands folded on her breast, perfectly still, perfectly white, like a girl carved in marble on a tomb.

Maxim stopped in the doorway.

Edric heard him stop. He heard the breath go out of him, all at once, in a sound that was not a word. He did not turn round. He crossed the room to the bed, and knelt beside it, and put his hand on her forehead.

She was cold. Colder than anything living should be. Colder than the sea under the Teeth.

He reached for the thread.

It was there. He had been holding it for forty days, beside his heart, through Solace and the grey house and the storm and the Pale Cliffs and the Sunspire and ten days at the bow of the *Faro*, and it was there, under his hand: so faint, so thin, so very far down, like a single strand of spider-silk at the bottom of a well. But there.

*On the Discipline of Still Water. The Waking.* *As the Warden stilled the waters by his own stillness, so he must wake them by his own motion. Let the Warden be the tide that comes in. Let him move, and let the other's waters remember how to move with him.*

He was so tired. He had nothing left. He had poured everything he had into the sea for ten days to get here, and he knelt by the bed with his hand on her forehead and reached down into himself for the tide, and found only mud, and stones, and weed.

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight. He went down, under the mud, under the stones, looking. And at the very bottom, so deep he had never been there before, he found it: not the sea, not the Deep, not anything he had been given. Something of his own. Small, and warm, and very old. The thing Faro had called a heart. The thing Hayda had called a well. The thing that had laughed at a heron from a landing stage in the Weir, a lifetime ago, and been gone for twenty years, and had come back, in a small boat, when a gull stole Hayda's bread.

He reached down, and took hold of it, and moved.

He felt the thread move with him. Faint, so faint. He felt it tremble. He felt the still waters in her, frozen for forty days, feel the motion, and remember. Her blood, so cold, so still, beginning, very slowly, to stir. Her heart, under his hand, beating. Once.

A long pause. Twice.

He moved, and moved, the way the tide moves, in and out, in and out, and her waters moved with him. Her heart beat, and beat, slow and then faster. Her blood began to warm. And her breath, which had not moved in forty days, caught in her throat, and stopped, and caught again, and came: a long, shuddering, gasping breath, like a swimmer coming up from very deep water.

Lyse Valtor opened her eyes.

She looked up at him, at his grey face bent over her. Her lips moved. No sound came out. She tried again.

"You came," she whispered. "You said you'd come. You swore. Grandfather said not to swear." She smiled, very faintly. "Is it over? Am I free?"

"Nearly," said Edric. His voice was not working properly. "Nearly. There's someone here."

He moved aside.

---

He went out of the library and left them. He did not want to see it. He did not need to. He sat on the top step of the stair outside, with his back against the cold stone wall, in the dark, and listened to Maxim Moren make a sound that he had only ever heard once before, from Pieter Moren, on the quay at Saltmere, when the *Gull's Promise* came home from nowhere. And then Lyse's voice, faint and laughing and crying all at once. And then nothing, for a long time, except the fire crackling, and the wind in the broken dome, and two people breathing.

Hayda came up the stairs and sat down beside him.

She did not say anything. She put her small hand in his, and leaned her head against his shoulder, and they sat there together in the dark on the top step of the stair, and listened.

"You did it," said Hayda at last, very quietly. "You kept your word."

"Yes."

"How does it feel?"

He thought about it. He sat on the cold stone in the dark with her head on his shoulder and the sound of two people breathing behind the door, and reached down into himself, past the cold, past the mud and the stones and the weed, to the small warm thing at the very bottom.

"Like a well," he said. "With water in it."

Hayda did not smile. She never smiled. But she squeezed his hand, very hard.

---

The fleet attacked at dawn.

Edric had known it would. He had slept for three hours, on the floor of the library, by the fire, with Jac's cloak over him, and woken in the grey dark before sunrise to Jac's hand on his shoulder and the sound of the harbour bell ringing. And he had gone up to the roof of the citadel, to the broken ring of pillars where the dome had been, and stood there in the cold dawn wind looking down at the harbour and the sea beyond it.

The twelve ships had moved in closer in the night. They lay in a curve across the harbour mouth now, half a mile out, just beyond the reefs. And on every deck, the great mirrors were turning. Slowly, all together, like flowers turning towards the sun. Towards the east, where the sky was going pink, then gold.

"They're gathering," said Jac beside him. "They can't fire in the dark. The mirrors drink the light all day and pour it out again. The more sun, the more fire. That's what the Radiants say." She looked at him. "By noon, they'll be able to burn this whole island down to the rock."

"I know," said Edric.

"Can you stop them?"

He looked at the sun coming up over the edge of the sea. He felt it on his face, already, warm, and under the warmth, the faint pressing ache that the sun always gave him now, that would grow, through the morning, into a spike by noon.

"Not by day," he said. "Not in the sun. Not after the Pale Cliffs, and the *Faro*." He was quiet a moment. "Tonight. At high tide. In the dark. Then, perhaps."

"Tonight," said Jac Teague. "Right." She looked down at the harbour, at the walls and the terraces and the stilt-houses, at the four thousand people of Cristal who were already pouring up the processional stair towards the citadel with whatever they could carry, the way she had told them to. "Then we've got to hold till tonight."

---

They held till tonight.

Afterwards, the people of Cristal would call it the Long Day, and tell their children about it, and their children's children, for as long as there was a Cristal. They would tell how the mirrors on the white ships turned all morning, drinking the sun, and how at an hour past dawn the first beam came across the water and struck the walls along the shore, and the stone ran like wax. How the beams came faster as the sun climbed, two at a time, three, five, until the harbour was a crisscross of gold light and smoke and the hiss of melting stone falling into the sea. How the warehouses behind the quays burned. How the Weir-folk's stilt-houses burned, every one, a hundred of them, standing in the shallow water at the inner end of the harbour like candles, while the Weir-folk watched from the terraces above and did not weep, because the Weir-folk did not weep in front of their enemies. How the sun climbed to noon, and the beams came so thick and fast that the sea in the harbour boiled.

They would tell how the Radiants came ashore in the afternoon, in boats, two hundred of them, in white and gold, with sunfire burning in their hands, and came up the processional stair, terrace by terrace. How the watch met them on the first terrace, Jac's watch, forty men and women from the terraces with spears and nets and cutlasses, and fought them back down the stair, and lost the terrace, and took it back, and lost it again. How Jac Teague fought on the first terrace with a cutlass in each hand, cursing in a voice they could hear on the citadel, until a Radiant's sunfire took her across the shoulder and she went down, and Tam the schoolmaster dragged her back up the stair by her collar with the Radiants at his heels. How Bastien Corso held the narrow stair on the second terrace alone for a quarter of an hour, with a knife in each hand, and the Radiants could not get past him.

How Hayda bint Tariq, princess of Qasr, stood at the top of the stair on the third terrace, in white Sardhi silk with her black hair tied back and Bastien's long knife in her hand, beside the door of the little whitewashed temple with the golden sun painted over it. How she said, to the Radiants coming up the stair towards her, in a clear cool carrying voice, in the common tongue: *My father was betrayed by a man who swore on his sword. You swear on the Sun. Look at what you are burning in its name.* And how some of them stopped, and looked.

And they would tell about Big Olaf.

---

He would not leave the temple.

Jac had told him to. Tam had begged him. Hayda had stood in the doorway and argued with him for ten minutes in the middle of the battle, while the beams struck the terraces below and the smoke rose. He had stood at the altar of his little whitewashed temple on the third terrace, the one he had built with his own hands, stone by stone, in the first summer, as a thank-offering to the Sun, and polished the brass sun over the altar with his sleeve, and shaken his great fair head.

"They're burning us in the Sun's name," he said, in his slow deep voice. "Somebody has to stay and tell the Sun it isn't true."

He was still there when the beam came.

It came at an hour past noon, from the flagship, the biggest of the white ships, which had swung its great mirror round to find the citadel and found the third terrace in its way. Edric saw it from the roof. He saw the gold light leap across the water and up the face of the island, a spear of burning sun, and strike the little whitewashed temple full on, so that it glowed, and blazed, and ran.

And he heard, for a moment, before the roar of the melting stone drowned it, a voice. Very loud, and very bad, and very deep, singing a hymn to the Sun. The one the northerners sang, the one Big Olaf had sung every morning in the rigging of the *Slip*, and every morning since in his little temple, alone. *Light of the morning, light of the world, light that is mercy, light that is true.*

Then the temple was gone, and the voice with it, and there was only a smoking hollow in the face of the third terrace, and a lump of something gold, half-melted, lying in the rubble: the brass sun from over the altar, that Olaf had polished every day for five years.

Edric stood on the roof of the citadel, in the burning noon, with the sun pressing on him like a hand, and the Tidemark cold on his throat, and the sea in him rising.

He felt it come. He felt it surge up from the very bottom of him, the way it had surged in the stillroom of the grey house, cold and black and eager. He felt the Deep turn, at the bottom of the world, three hundred miles away, and look.

*Yes, little one. Yes. They burn your people. They burn your temple, your singer, your city. In the name of the golden one, who chains me. Now. Now. Call me. I am here. Drown them.*

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight.

*Not yet,* he said. *Not in the sun. Not like this. Tonight.*

*Tonight they will have burned everything.*

*Then they'll have burned everything. And tonight I'll stop them.* He held the stillness. It was like holding a door shut against a storm. *But I won't drown them. Not like that. Not like you want.*

*Why not?*

He looked down at the harbour, at the boiling sea, at the twelve white ships with their mirrors turning, and at the boats on the shore, and the white and gold figures on the stair. Two hundred Radiants, on the terraces. Two thousand more, on the ships. Men and women who had been taught, from the time they were novices, that the Tidewardens drowned children. Who had knelt for six hours a day to the Sun. Who had never once in their lives been told the truth.

*Because they don't know,* he said. *Because nobody ever told them. Because the man who sent them is sitting at the top of a tower, drinking you, and they think he's a god.*

The Deep was silent.

*And because Olaf wouldn't have wanted it,* said Edric. *He'd have wanted me to tell the Sun it isn't true.*

---

The sun went down at last, red, into the western sea.

On the twelve white ships, the great mirrors turned to follow it, drinking the last of it, and then went dark. On the terraces of Cristal, the Radiants who had come ashore drew back down the processional stair to the harbour, carrying their wounded, and waited. Tomorrow, they knew, the mirrors would drink the sun again, and they would come back up the stair and finish it. There was nothing on Cristal that could stand against a second day.

In the citadel at the top of the island, four thousand people waited in the dark. The Weir-folk had lit lamps, and set them out along the walls of the citadel, all round the broken ring of pillars, and in the windows of the library: little oil lamps, hundreds of them, burning in the dark like the candles the Weir-folk floated out on the water on Midsummer night, to light the way for the souls of the drowned.

Edric went down to the harbour alone at midnight.

He went down the processional stair, through the empty terraces, past the smoking hollow where Olaf's temple had been, past the places where the watch had fought and fallen. Nobody stopped him. The Radiants on the shore did not see him. He wore no face at all; he did not need to. He went down in the dark, in the dark of the moon, at the turn of the tide, and they did not see him, the way men on a beach do not see the tide coming in until it is around their ankles.

He went out to the end of the broken quay at the mouth of the harbour, where the tower had been, and stood at the very edge, with the sea black and slow beneath him, and the twelve white ships lying dark across the harbour mouth half a mile away, with their riding lights burning. He took off his coat and his shirt, and stood there in the cold night wind with the Tidemark glowing faint and blue on his chest and arms and throat.

He was so tired. He had so little left. He reached down into himself and found the mud, and the stones, and the weed. And under them, the small warm thing, the well with water in it. And under that, at the very bottom, so deep he had never been there, the door. The place where the sea came in.

He opened it.

---

It came in like the tide.

Not like the flood in the stillroom. Not a wall, not a burst dyke. It came in the way the real tide comes in over a flat beach on a still night: slowly, steadily, from very far away, rising, rising, so gradually that you do not notice it until it is all around you. It came up through his feet from the stone of the quay, and up through the stone from the sea, and up through the sea from the bottom of the world, where something vast was turning in the dark, three hundred miles away, at the end of seven chains of gold. It came up into him, cold, cold, colder than anything, and filled him, and went on filling him. And this time, he did not try to hold it. He let it come. He let it fill him up to the brim, and then he gave it somewhere to go.

He reached out, with the sea, to the twelve white ships.

He felt them. Their hulls, deep in the water. Their anchors, biting into the sand. The men on their decks, asleep, or on watch, two thousand of them, the water in their bodies moving in their sleep. The great mirrors, dark, mounted on their swivels of brass. He felt all of it, the way he felt his own hands.

He did not pull them down. That would have been easy. The Deep wanted it; he felt the Deep wanting it, all around him, in him, a great cold pressure, pulling, pulling. *Down. Take them down. Down into the dark, with all their mirrors and all their suns, the way they took mine. Down.*

He did not pull them down. He lifted.

He reached under the twelve white ships, under their keels, into the deep water beneath them, and gathered it, the way you gather a sheet in your hands, and lifted. A swell rose out of the dark beneath them: not a wave, not a storm, but a lifting, a vast slow heaving of the whole sea under the harbour mouth, like a giant taking a breath. It rose, and rose, and the twelve white ships rose on it, up and up, higher than any tide had ever lifted them. Their anchor cables came taut, and taut, and parted, one after another, with sounds like gunshots in the dark. Their riding lights swung wildly. Men woke screaming.

And then the swell began to move. Away from the island. West, and out, towards the open sea.

It carried them. Twelve white ships, riding the back of the sea like leaves on a river in flood, out past the reefs, out past the harbour mouth, out into the dark. He felt them go. He felt the men on their decks clinging to the rails, to the masts, to each other. He felt the great mirrors on their swivels, swinging, tilting, unbalanced, and he reached up with the sea, and the sea came over the bows of the ships in long green walls of water, and struck the mirrors, every one, and cracked them, and filled them with salt, and they went dark forever.

Two miles west of Cristal, there was a sandbank: the Long Bank, a great curving shoal of pale sand a mile long, just under the surface at low tide, where nothing grew and the seabirds rested. He knew it as he knew the harbour. He carried the twelve white ships to it, on the back of the sea, and set them down.

Gently. As gently as he could. As gently as the sea had once set him down on Gull Reef, on the morning Jac Teague found him. He set them down on the soft pale sand, one by one, and let the swell sink away beneath them, and they settled on the bank in the dark, leaning, with their masts at angles and their keels in the sand, and their mirrors smashed, and two thousand men on their decks, wet and terrified and alive.

Then he let go.

---

He let go, and the sea went out of him, the way the tide goes out.

He felt it go. All of it. Back down through his feet, through the stone, through the sea, to the bottom of the world, to the dark where something vast turned at the end of seven chains, and he heard it, as it went, very faint and very far away, speak to him for a moment in the vast slow voice.

*You could have drowned them.*

*Yes.*

*They would have drowned you.*

*Yes.*

A long silence, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea.

*Interesting,* said the Deep.

And it was gone, and he was standing on the end of the broken quay at the mouth of the harbour of Cristal in the dark, with no shirt and no coat, shaking so hard that he could not stand, and then he was not standing. He was lying on the cold stone, looking up at the stars, the same stars, and he could hear, very faintly, from the citadel high above, behind him, a sound he had not heard in a long time. Four thousand people, cheering.

He closed his eyes.

---

Cassia Vell came ashore at dawn, in a single boat, under a white flag.

She had been on the flagship. She had followed the fleet from Solace in a fast cutter, two days behind, on the King's orders, to see the work done and report it; and she had caught up with them on the afternoon of the Long Day, and come aboard the flagship, and stood at its rail, and watched. She had watched the mirrors burn the harbour tower, and the walls, and the stilt-houses. She had watched her knights go up the processional stair. She had watched a slight, straight girl in white silk stand at the top of the third terrace with a knife in her hand and say *look at what you are burning in its name*. She had watched the little whitewashed temple with the golden sun over its door glow, and blaze, and run.

And she had heard the singing.

She came up the shingle at the inner end of the harbour, where the Weir-folk's stilt-houses had been, and was met there by a short, red-haired woman with her shoulder bandaged and a cutlass in her good hand, and a lean, black-browed man with a knife, and a slight girl in white silk with blood on her sleeve.

"I want to see the Warden," said Cassia Vell.

"The Warden's asleep," said Jac Teague. "He's been asleep since midnight. He might sleep for a week. He brought your whole fleet up out of the sea in the dark and put it down on a sandbank like a cat putting down a kitten, and nobody died, and it nearly killed him." She looked the High Radiant up and down. "What do you want?"

Cassia Vell did not answer at once. She was looking past them, up the slope of the island: at the smoking ruins of the harbour, the burned warehouses, the blackened stumps of a hundred stilt-houses standing in the shallow water. At the terraces climbing to the citadel, scarred and smoking. At the smoking hollow on the third terrace where the temple had been. And at the citadel on the summit, with its broken dome, and its walls lined with little oil lamps, hundreds of them, still burning in the grey dawn: the Weir-folk's lamps, to light the way for the souls of the drowned.

"Twenty-two years," she said, in a strange voice. "Six hours a day, on my knees." She looked at Jac. "There was a man in that temple. Singing. A hymn to the Sun. The one we teach the novices." She swallowed. "We burned him. In the Sun's name. While he was singing to it."

Nobody said anything.

Cassia Vell knelt down on the shingle, in her white and gold armour, and unbuckled her sword, and laid it on the stones in front of her.

"The fleet surrenders," she said. "Every ship. Every knight. I've given the order. You'll find them on the Long Bank, waiting." She looked up. "And I have a message for the Warden. When he wakes. He needs to hear it."

"Tell us," said Hayda.

"The King has left Solace," said Cassia Vell. "He left the day after the Warden did. In secret, on a ship of his own, with the Hundred: the oldest knights of the Order, the strongest, the ones who never leave the Sunspire. I saw them go. He's gone to the Teeth. To the Drowned Keep. To the Lock." Her square plain face was very pale. "He told me before he went. He said that if the Warden would not join him, then there must be no Deep left for the Warden to draw on. He said he would go down to the Lock himself, and stand at the chains, and drink. Not a cup a day. All of it. As fast as he can. Until there's nothing left." She looked out at the harbour. "He said it would take a month, perhaps. And at the end of it, the sea would be dead, and the Warden would be nothing, and he would never be afraid of anything again."

Jac Teague looked at her. Then, slowly, she turned and looked out at the harbour, at the water lapping at the shingle in the grey dawn, among the blackened stumps of the stilt-houses.

There were fish in it. Floating. Small silver fish, a few dozen of them, belly up in the shallow water, drifting on the tide. Dead.

And as they watched, more came. Floating up from the deeper water at the harbour mouth. A few at first, then dozens, then hundreds, a slow silver tide of dead fish drifting in on the dawn, and the water around them grey, and still, and wrong.

On the shingle at the inner end of the harbour, a Weir-folk grandmother, one of the old Bettans, who had come down from the citadel at dawn to look at the ruins of her house, knelt at the edge of the water and put her hand in it, and snatched it back, and began, quite quietly, to keen: a high, thin, wordless sound, the sound the Weir-folk made for the dead.

High above them, in the library at the top of the citadel, on the floor by the fire, under Jac's cloak, Edric Vane opened his eyes.

He did not know what had woken him. He lay very still, listening. And then he felt it: far away, three hundred miles to the east, at the bottom of the world, at the end of seven chains of gold, something vast that had been turning slowly in the dark for three hundred years.

Screaming.
