## Chapter 17: The House of Moren

The house of Moren had stood on Harbour Street in Saltmere for thirty years: a tall, narrow, sober building of grey stone with the name carved over the door in plain letters, and a counting-room on the ground floor where a dozen clerks had once sat at high desks in rows, scratching at ledgers, and a window on the first floor from which Pieter Moren could watch his ships come in.

There were three clerks now. Two of the desks in the counting-room had been sold. The window on the first floor had a crack across one pane that nobody had found the money to mend. And Pieter Moren, at sixty-four, sat behind it most days with his hands folded on his desk, watching a harbour in which, for the first time in thirty years, there was not a single ship that belonged to him.

A clerk knocked on the door of his office on a grey morning in late autumn and said there was a gentleman to see him. A foreign gentleman. From Port Jasmine.

Moren closed his eyes for a moment. Then he said, "Show him up."

---

The foreign gentleman was tall and lean and dressed very well in the southern style, in a long coat of dark green silk that came to his knees, with a high collar and silver buttons, and a broad-brimmed hat that he took off at the door, revealing close-cropped hair touched with grey. He had a hard, brown, clever face, and grey eyes, and a slight, precise, foreign accent, and he carried a leather case under his arm.

"Master Moren," he said, and bowed. "My name is Wilde. I represent the banking house of Thornbury and Wilde, of Port Jasmine. I believe you know why I've come."

"I believe I do," said Moren. "Sit down, Master Wilde."

The foreign gentleman sat. He opened the leather case and took out a sheaf of papers, and laid them on the desk between them, and Moren looked at them, and knew them. He had signed every one.

"Bills of exchange," said Master Wilde. "Drawn on the house of Moren, at various dates over the past six years, in favour of various merchants and banks in Valemont and the southern ports. My house has bought them all, over the past three months. They come to a little under two hundred thousand marks. The first of them falls due at the end of this month."

"I know," said Moren.

"Can you pay them?"

Moren looked at him for a long moment. He had always, all his life, told the truth in business, even when it cost him. He saw no reason to stop now.

"No," he said. "I can't."

---

He told the foreign gentleman the whole of it. He did not know why. Perhaps because there was no one else left to tell, and a man has to tell someone. Perhaps because the foreign gentleman listened, as nobody had listened to him in years, quietly, without interrupting, with those steady grey eyes on his face.

It had begun, he said, fourteen years ago. There had been a young man, a captain of his, arrested on his wedding night on a charge of treason. "The finest young man I ever knew," said Moren. "Honest as daylight. I'd have trusted him with my life." He had gone up to the magistrate's house the next morning to plead for him. And the morning after. And again, and again, for weeks. He had written to Solace. He had used every friend he had, called in every favour he was owed.

"And then the contracts started going," he said. "One by one. The Crown contracts first, for the navy stores. Then the merchant houses that did business with the Crown. Nobody said why. Nobody ever says why. But I found out, years later, from a man who'd been a clerk in the magistrate's office. Every petition I'd made for that boy had gone into a file. A file on *me*. On the house of Moren. Suspected sympathies with the Ebb. That file followed me around for ten years, and every man in Valemont who wanted to keep on the right side of the Throne knew to steer clear of me."

He could have recovered, perhaps, from that alone. He had been cautious all his life; there was money put by. But then the sea had turned against him.

"Storms," he said. "Wrong storms, at the wrong time of year. Everybody's had them, these last ten years, worse and worse, but I had more than my share. I lost the *Heron* off the Ember Coast, and the *Seabird* in the Month of Storms off the Cape. I lost cargoes to blight, whole holds of grain and spice gone black. The fishing's failed all along the coast; the old men say they've never seen the like. Nobody has any money. Nobody's buying." He spread his hands. "And then three months ago I lost the *Gull's Promise*."

The foreign gentleman's face did not change. But his hand, on the arm of the chair, went still.

"She was my last ship," said Moren. "The best I ever had. She'd been the boy's ship, the one I told you about. Fourteen years, and I'd never been able to bring myself to sell her. She went down off Gull Reef in a squall, with everything I had left in her. The crew got off in the boats. I thank the Sun for that. But the cargo, and the ship." He shook his head. "That was the end. I borrowed against her, against what she'd bring home. And she didn't come home."

There was a long silence in the office. Outside, gulls cried over the empty harbour.

"I've a daughter," said Moren, after a while. "Julie. She's twenty-six. She's to be married in the spring to my head clerk, Emmanuel, a good boy, a good honest boy, but he has nothing, and nor will she, now. And a son, Maxim, who's nineteen and just got his commission in the King's army, and is so proud of his coat he sleeps in it." He smiled faintly. "They don't know how bad it is. I've not told them. I didn't want them to worry. I kept thinking something would turn up. A ship from nowhere. A miracle." He looked at the papers on the desk. "And instead, there's you."

The foreign gentleman said nothing for a long moment.

"What will you do," he said at last, "at the end of the month? When the first bill falls due, and you can't pay it?"

Moren looked at him with his tired, honest eyes, and did not answer. He did not need to. His right hand had moved, without his seeming to notice, to rest on the drawer of his desk.

The foreign gentleman saw it. He looked at the drawer, and then at Moren, for a long time.

Then he gathered up the papers, and put them back in his case, and closed it.

"Three months," he said.

Moren stared at him.

"My house will extend the terms of every bill," said Master Wilde, "by three months. The first will fall due not at the end of this month, but on the last day of the Month of Frosts. I'll have the papers drawn up today."

"Why?" said Moren. "Why would you do that? I've told you, I can't pay. Not in three months. Not in three years."

"Perhaps something will turn up," said the foreign gentleman. "A ship from nowhere. A miracle." He stood, and put on his hat. "You said yourself, Master Moren, you'd trusted that young man with your life. Perhaps someone once trusted you with theirs." He bowed. "Good day."

He was at the door when Moren spoke again.

"Master Wilde."

He turned.

Moren was looking at him strangely: with his head on one side, and his eyes narrowed, like a man trying to see something at a great distance in bad light.

"Have we met?" said Moren slowly. "Before? You put me in mind of someone. I can't think who."

The foreign gentleman held very still. In his face, behind the stillness, something rippled and settled, like a pool when a stone has gone in and the rings have died away.

"I don't believe so," he said. "I've only just arrived from the south." And he went out, and closed the door gently behind him, and stood on the landing for a long moment in the dark with his hand flat against the wall, breathing.

---

He went up Ropewalk Lane that afternoon, for the first time.

The tall, crooked house was still there. The steps were still there, where the street got too steep for cobbles. The window was still there, at the top, under the eaves. He stood at the bottom of the steps and looked up at it, and for a moment he saw an old man sitting behind the glass with his hands folded in his lap, looking out at the harbour, waiting for a ship.

The rooms were empty. Had been empty, the landlady said, for years. Nobody wanted them. Too many stairs, and the old man had died there, and people said things.

Master Wilde, the foreign banker, paid her two years' rent in advance in gold, and asked for the key, and asked not to be disturbed. She gave it to him, staring, and went away.

He went up the stairs. Past the door of the rooms below, where Cade Rusk had once lived, and listened through the floor. Up to the top. He opened the door.

It was very small. He had forgotten how small. Two rooms under the eaves, bare boards, a hearth, a window. Someone had taken the furniture, long ago: the table, the bed, his father's good chair. There was nothing left. Only the dust, and the light coming through the window, and the view.

He went to the window and stood where the chair had been and looked out.

The whole harbour lay below him, grey under the grey sky. The quays. The harbour wall. The light tower. The empty moorings where Moren's ships had been. To the west, the Weir, with its stilts and walkways and lamps. To the south-west, far out, a smudge of black on the horizon: the Teeth.

*He sat by that window, day after day. Like he was waiting for a ship.*

He stood there for a long time. He did not weep. He found, somewhat to his surprise, that he could not. The sea had taken that from him, perhaps, or the Keep, or the years. He only stood, and looked, and felt a great cold stillness spreading through him from the Tidemark over his heart, like ice spreading across a pond.

After a while, he took a small red silk purse from the pocket of his green coat, and laid it on the windowsill, in the corner, where the light fell. And went out, and locked the door.

---

He went to the cemetery on the hill above the town, where the Saltmere dead were buried, and found the grave. It took him an hour. It was in the poor corner, by the wall, under a yew tree: a small flat stone, already green with moss, with letters cut into it by someone who had not been able to afford a mason.

TOMAS VANE
SAILOR
HE WAITED

Someone had cut it with a chisel, by hand, unevenly. Someone had chosen those words. He knew who.

He knelt by the stone and put his hand on it. It was cold, and wet with the afternoon damp. He felt the water in it, deep in the stone, and in the earth beneath, and in the roots of the yew. He felt the sea far below the hill, breathing.

"I'm home, Father," he said. "I'm sorry I was late."

The yew tree dripped. The gulls cried. The sea breathed.

He stayed until it was dark.

---

He did not mean to go to the Weir. He told himself, walking down the hill in the dark, that he would not go to the Weir. There was nothing there for him now. She was gone, a countess in Solace with diamonds in her hair. Her mother was dead. Her family's house, the landlady had told him, had been sold years ago to strangers.

He went anyway. His feet took him, the way the tide takes a boat with no oars.

The Weir at night was the same as it had always been: the swaying walkways, the creaking stilts, the lamps hung on ropes, the black water lapping beneath. The smell of the marsh, of salt and smoke and mud. Weir-folk sitting on their landing stages with their feet in the water, talking in their soft hissing tongue, who looked up as he passed, at the tall stranger in the green southern coat, and fell silent, and watched him go.

He came to the Solens' landing stage. There were strangers living in the house now, a family with small children; he could hear them through the reed walls. The landing stage was empty. There was no boat tied to it. The garlands were long gone.

He stood on the walkway and looked at it, and remembered a girl with her feet in the water, mending a net, saying *five hours, Edric Vane*.

"You came back, then," said a voice behind him.

He turned.

At the end of the walkway, where it ran out over the open water of the channel, there was a little platform of old planks, and on it, a chair, and in the chair, wrapped in so many black shawls that she looked like a heap of laundry, sat a woman so old that her face had become nothing but wrinkles, like the bark of a tree. Her eyes were white. Quite white, both of them, like pebbles. She was looking straight at him.

"Grandmother," he said. His voice cracked on it.

Old Ysolde laughed: a small, dry, rustling sound, like leaves.

"I'd know that step anywhere," she said, in the Weir-tongue. "Fourteen years, and I'd know it. Even blind. Even with the sea so loud in you now it's like standing next to a waterfall." She held out a hand, a tiny, twisted claw of a hand, from among the shawls. "Come here, boy. Come here where I can touch you."

He went to her. He knelt on the wet planks in front of the chair. She put her hand on his face, and felt it, slowly, all over, the way the blind do: his brow, his eyes, the scar through the eyebrow, his cheeks, his mouth, his jaw.

"Hard," she said. "You've gone hard. Like a rock the sea's been beating on." Her fingers moved down his throat, to the collar of the green coat, and stopped. "Cold. Gods of the deep, you're cold. Cold as the bottom of the sea." She took her hand away, and sat back, and her white eyes looked at him, or through him. "So. It took you."

"Yes."

"I told you it was there. At the feast. I told you. Quiet, I said. Not always a gift, I said." She shook her head slowly. "It's not quiet now. I've been sitting on the end of this walkway every night for fifty years, listening to the sea. It's been dreaming badly all that time. Sick. Restless. And then, a few months ago, one night in a storm, it woke up. Just for a moment. I felt it. The whole Weir felt it; the old ones, anyway. The water in the channels stood up on end, every channel at once, and the boats all turned their heads to the south-west, like dogs hearing their master. And then it lay down again." She was quiet a moment. "That was you, wasn't it?"

"Yes."

"Hm." Old Ysolde pulled her shawls tighter. "You know what it wants?"

"Yes."

"And you know what it'll cost you?"

"Yes."

"No you don't," said Old Ysolde. "Nobody does. Not till they've paid it." She turned her white eyes away, out towards the dark sea. "She waited for you. You know that? Two years. Every spring tide. She'd come and sit there, on the landing, with her feet in the water, all night. Watching the boat. Waiting for the sea to take it out." Her voice was very soft. "It never did. Not once. She'd come home at dawn soaked to the knees and not say a word, and go to bed, and sleep all day. And then the next spring tide, she'd go back."

He closed his eyes.

"And then her mother got sick," said Old Ysolde, "and that one came back. Fenrick. With his coat and his money and his promises. And she married him. In grey, up the hill, at the Sun's temple, not here. She wouldn't have it here. Not on the water. Said the sea had already said no once, and she'd not ask it twice." The old woman turned her face back towards him. "She came to see me, before she left. The night before. She sat where you're kneeling now and cried until I thought she'd never stop. And she gave me something to keep."

She reached down beside her chair, among the shawls, and brought up a bundle wrapped in an old grey cloth, and put it in his hands.

He knew what it was before he unwrapped it.

It was a crown. A circlet of woven sea-lavender, dried now, brittle, faded from purple to the colour of dust, threaded through with small white shells. The lavender had crumbled at one side. Two of the shells were missing. It was very light, and very fragile, and it smelled, very faintly, after fourteen years, of the sea.

"I made that for her," said Old Ysolde. "For her wedding. Her real one. To you." She sat back. "She said to keep it. She said if you ever came back, I was to give it to you. And if you didn't, I was to give it to the sea." The white eyes held him. "I'm a hundred and two years old, boy. I've been waiting a long time to know which. Now I know."

He knelt on the wet planks with the crown in his hands and could not speak.

"Take it," said the old woman. "It's yours. Give it to her, or give it to the sea, or give it to the fire. It's yours to decide. I'm too old to carry it any more." She closed her white eyes. "Now go away. I want to listen to the water. And you're too loud."

---

On the last day of the Month of Frosts, three months later, Pieter Moren sat in his office on the first floor of the house on Harbour Street, in front of the cracked window, with the drawer of his desk open, and a loaded pistol in the drawer.

He had settled his affairs. He had written letters to Julie and to Maxim, and to Emmanuel, and sealed them, and put them in the top drawer where they would be found. He had told no one. He had put on his best coat. It was nearly noon. At noon, the agent of Thornbury and Wilde would come, with the first of the bills.

No ship had come from nowhere. No miracle had turned up.

He took the pistol out of the drawer, and looked at it, and put it on the desk in front of him.

Below, in the street, someone was knocking on the door. Hammering. Shouting. He heard the clerks running. He heard a girl's voice, high and breathless, coming up the stairs, and the door of his office burst open, and his daughter Julie stood in the doorway, white-faced, wild-haired, with something red clutched in her hand.

"Father," she said. "Father, look. *Look.*"

She was holding a purse. A small red silk purse, faded at the edges, as if it had sat in the sun.

"A boy came to the door," Julie said. Her voice was shaking. "An hour ago. A boy, a street boy, I didn't know him. He gave me a note. It said to go to Ropewalk Lane, to the top of the old crooked house, to the rooms where old Tomas Vane used to live, the old sailor, you remember, Edric's father, and that I'd find something on the windowsill. And the door was unlocked, and there was nobody there, and it was on the windowsill, Father, in the corner, in the sun. This."

She opened the purse and tipped it out onto the desk, beside the pistol.

Papers fell out. Bills of exchange. Every one of them. Every bill drawn on the house of Moren in the past six years, two hundred thousand marks of them, and every one of them stamped across the face in red ink: PAID IN FULL. THORNBURY AND WILDE. And after the papers, a second, smaller thing, which rolled across the desk and stopped against the barrel of the pistol: a diamond, as big as the top joint of a man's thumb, cut in the old style with a hundred faces, catching the noon light from the cracked window and breaking it into a hundred tiny rainbows.

And a card. A plain white card, with a single line written on it in a hand Moren did not know:

*For Julie's wedding. The Tide remembers.*

Pieter Moren stared at it. He picked up the card, and turned it over, and put it down again. He looked at the bills. He looked at the diamond. He looked at the pistol, lying on the desk beside them, and then, very slowly, he reached out and put it back in the drawer, and closed the drawer, and put his face in his hands.

From the harbour, through the cracked window, came a sound: shouting. A lot of people shouting at once. Bells. Someone was ringing the bell on the harbour master's office, the bell that was rung when a ship came in.

Julie ran to the window. "Father," she said, in a strange voice. "Father, come and see."

He got up. His legs were not working properly. He went to the window, and looked out, and down, at the harbour.

A ship was coming in.

She came round the harbour light under full sail, with the noon sun on her canvas, a three-masted merchantman, long and low and sleek, and she came through the narrows against the tide, without a pilot, without slowing, as if the sea itself were carrying her. She was newly built; Moren could see from here the pale gleam of new timber, the fresh black of the tar, the white of new sails. And she was built to lines he knew. Lines he had drawn himself, thirty years ago, in this office, on a table that had since been sold. Every curve of her. Every plank.

She slid alongside the quay with no more than a kiss of wood on stone. Men leapt ashore with lines. And at her stern, on the new paint, in letters of gold, was her name.

*Gull's Promise.*

"It's her," said Julie. "Father, it's her. It can't be. She sank. But it's her."

Moren did not answer. He could not. He stood at the window with his hand on the cracked glass and watched the crowd gather on the quay, and the hatches open, and a short, red-haired woman in a captain's coat come down the gangway with a paper in her hand, the cargo manifest, and ask the harbour master for the house of Pieter Moren.

He was weeping. He did not know when he had started.

"Edric," he said, without knowing why. "*Edric.*"

---

Across the harbour, in the lee of the harbour wall, a small grey skiff lay at anchor with a single man in it.

He wore a plain dark coat, not the green silk of the foreign banker, and he was sitting very still in the stern, watching the quay, where a crowd of people were cheering and an old man in his best coat had come running out of the house on Harbour Street with his daughter on his arm, and stopped at the foot of the gangway, and put his hand on the ship's new timber as if it were the flank of a horse he had thought was dead.

The man in the skiff watched until the old man had gone up the gangway and been swallowed up by the crowd on the deck. Then he raised the skiff's small sail, and let the wind take it, and slipped out past the harbour light into the open sea.

He did not look back. He looked west, towards the Reach, and Cristal, and the work.

"There," he said, to the sea, to the wind, to no one. "That's the last of the debts I owe. The good ones."

He sat with that for a while, in the stern of the little boat, with the spray in his face.

Then he said, more quietly: "Now for the others."

And under his coat, over his heart, the sea burned cold, and he felt it rise in him, eager, heaving, like a hound that has been shown the scent. And he remembered an old man in a cell, gripping his hands: *The moment it becomes sweet, Edric Vane, the moment you start to enjoy it, the sea will be in you, and you'll be lost.*

He breathed. In for eight. Out for eight.

And the little grey boat ran on into the west.
