## Chapter 23: The Desert Witness

The *Solace Courant* was printed every morning at four on a great iron press in a cellar off the Street of Gold, and sold by boys on every corner of the city by six, and by seven it had been read in every coffee house and counting-house and palace from the docks to the Sunspire. It was not supposed to print anything the Throne did not like. It mostly did not. But it had a correspondent in the Sardh, an old, sour, independent man who had lived in Mazrah for thirty years and sent dispatches by every ship. And on a morning in early autumn, it printed one of them on its front page.

It was not long. It said that a merchant of Mazrah, recently returned from the interior, had brought word of a matter long rumoured in the Sardh but never proved: the true story of the fall of Qasr, eleven years before. That the Emir Tariq had not been overthrown by his own people, as the official histories said, but betrayed by a foreign officer in his service. A Valemont captain, lent to the Emir by the King as a military adviser, who had sworn on his sword to serve the Emir faithfully, and had eaten at his table, and had been given command of the city's great gate. And who had, on the night the King's army came, opened the gate, and murdered the Emir in his bed, and sold the Emir's wife and young daughter to the slavers at Qadir for two hundred gold pieces. The merchant had seen the bill of sale, the correspondent wrote. It was signed. The signature was that of a captain whose initials were F. M., and who now held one of the highest offices in Valemont.

Nobody in Solace needed to be told whose initials those were.

---

Edric read it at breakfast, in the hall of green glass, with the fountain playing, and folded the paper, and laid it beside his plate.

It had taken him three months to arrange. The merchant in Mazrah was real; Edric's agents had found him, and paid him well, and given him a copy of the bill of sale, which Hayda had been given, along with the other papers, by an old servant of her father's, years after the fall, when she was still a slave. The correspondent was real; nobody had paid him anything, because nobody needed to. He had hated the Lord Marshal of Valemont for eleven years, and had been waiting for proof.

And the man who had carried the merchant's story to the correspondent, and the copy of the bill of sale with it, had been Baron Dorran Quell.

That had been the most delicate part. Dorran and Fenrick had not been friends for a long time, if they ever had been. They had climbed side by side for nineteen years, the sword and the purse, and they had come to dislike each other with the particular cold intensity of two men who each knew something about the other that nobody else did. Fenrick sneered at Dorran's clerkishness. Dorran despised Fenrick's stupidity. And Dorran, this autumn, was frightened, very frightened, for reasons he could tell no one. He had lost a million and a half marks in six months, and the Count of Cristal kept drawing and drawing on his bank, and the hole in the Blight Fund grew every week. A frightened man looks for someone else to fall first. So when one of the bank's agents in Mazrah, who was also one of Edric's, sent the Lord Treasurer a private letter about a merchant with an interesting story, and a copy of an interesting document, Dorran had read it, and sat very still for a long time, and then taken it, personally, in a closed carriage, to the cellar off the Street of Gold.

He had not asked himself where the agent had found it. He had not wanted to.

---

The Chamber of Lords met in an inquiry four days later, at Fenrick's own demand.

He had come to the Chamber the morning after the *Courant* printed its story, in full dress uniform, with every medal on his breast, and stood in the well of the great hall under the dome of white and gold, and demanded, in a voice that rang off the marble, that his peers clear his name. He called the story a lie. He called the correspondent a traitor and the merchant a forger. He said he would prove before the Chamber, before the Throne, before the Sun itself, that he had served the King faithfully in the Sardh, as he had served him all his life.

The Chamber agreed. It had little choice. An inquiry of peers was the right of every lord of Valemont whose honour was impugned, and Fenrick was the Lord Marshal; to refuse him would have been an insult to the army. A committee of twelve lords was named. The inquiry was set for the following week.

Edric went to watch. So did half of Solace.

The great hall of the Chamber was packed to the walls: the lords in their robes on their benches, the galleries above them crammed with ladies and merchants and clerks and journalists and anyone who could get a seat. The air was hot and thick. The Count of Cristal sat in the second gallery, in black, with his hat in his lap and his still face giving nothing away. Beside him, veiled, sat a slight, straight figure in grey.

Fenrick testified for an hour.

He did it well. Edric had to grant him that. He stood in the well of the hall in his blue and gold, with his hand on his sword, and told the story in his big, plain, soldier's voice, and the lords listened, and nodded.

Yes, he had served the Emir Tariq. The King had lent him, a young captain, to the Emir as a military adviser, in the years before the war, when Qasr was still an ally. He had served faithfully. He had been given command of the gate. But the Emir had betrayed the alliance. The Emir had been in secret negotiations with the King's enemies in the east. Fenrick had discovered it. He had sent word to the King's army. And when the army came, the Emir's own soldiers, who hated the Emir, who had been waiting for their chance, had opened the gates and murdered him. Fenrick had tried to save the women. He had been too late. They had been taken in the chaos by slavers who followed the army, and he had searched for them for weeks, and never found them, and he had grieved for them ever since. As for the so-called bill of sale, it was a forgery. A clumsy forgery, by enemies of the Throne in the Sardh, who had hated him for eleven years because he had served his King.

When he had finished, there was a silence in the hall. Then, from the benches, a murmur, and here and there, the knock of knuckles on wood, the old sign of a lord's approval.

The president of the committee, an elderly lord with a white beard, cleared his throat.

"Does any man here have evidence to set against the Lord Marshal's testimony?"

Nobody moved. Fenrick stood in the well of the hall with his hand on his sword, and his heavy face flushed, and looked up at the galleries with something like triumph.

And in the second gallery, the slight figure in grey rose to her feet.

---

"I do," said Hayda.

She did not say it loudly. But her voice was very clear, and the hall was very quiet, and it went out across the white marble like a struck bell.

Every head in the Chamber turned.

She came down the gallery stairs and into the well of the hall, slowly, with her chin up, and stood before the committee of twelve lords. And she put back her veil.

There was a sound in the galleries like wind through dry grass.

"My name," she said, "is Hayda bint Tariq. I am the daughter of the Emir Tariq of Qasr, and of the Lady Vasiliki, his wife." She turned her head, just slightly, and looked at Fenrick Morrow, standing six paces away with his hand on his sword. "And I have evidence."

She had brought it in a small cedar box. She opened it on the clerk's table in front of the committee, and took the things out one by one, and laid them down, and named them, in her clear cool voice, like a herald reading a roll of honour.

A ring of gold, with a great seal-stone of green jade, carved with the arms of the Emirs of Qasr. Her father's. Taken from his hand, she said, by the man who killed him, and sold in the market at Mazrah four years later, where her guardian's agents had found it.

A letter, in the Emir's own hand, in High Sardhi, with his seal, appointing *the Valemont captain, Fenrick Morrow, my trusted friend, whom I love as a son*, to the command of the Great Gate of Qasr. She read it aloud, first in High Sardhi, then in the common tongue. When she read the words *whom I love as a son*, her voice did not change at all.

A document on parchment, stained, creased, much handled. The original, she said; not a copy. A bill of sale from the slave market at Qadir, dated eleven years ago, the week after the fall of Qasr. *Received of Tamir ibn Rashid, dealer, two hundred gold pieces, for: one woman, of the household of the late Emir of Qasr, by name Vasiliki; one female child, of the same, by name Hayda, age six years.* And beneath it, a signature, in a big, plain, heavy hand.

*F. Morrow, Capt.*

"An old servant of my father's household kept this," said Hayda, "after he bought it from the dealer, years later, when he was free and I was not. He gave it to me when I was twelve, in the house of the man who owned me then, so that I would know. So that I would never forget." She laid it on the table beside the others. "I have not forgotten."

The hall was utterly silent.

"The Lord Marshal says," said Hayda, "that my father's soldiers opened the gates. That he tried to save my mother and me. That he searched for us for weeks." She turned, then, and faced Fenrick Morrow directly, and lifted her hand, and pointed at him. "I was there. I was six years old, and I was there. I was in my mother's room when the gates opened. I saw the fires. I heard my father shouting. And I saw this man come through the door of my mother's room with blood on his sword and on his hands. My father's blood. I saw him take my father's ring off his own finger and put it in his pocket. I saw him tie my mother's hands. And I saw him, a week later, in the market at Qadir, take two hundred gold pieces from a slaver in a striped coat, and count them, and put them in his purse, and walk away. He did not look back at us. Not once." Her hand did not waver. "That man."

---

Fenrick did not answer.

Afterwards, people would say that he had tried to. That he had opened his mouth and nothing had come out. That his face had gone from red to white to grey, like a man bleeding to death from a wound you could not see. That his hand had tightened on the hilt of his sword until the knuckles cracked, and then let go. That he had stood in the well of the Chamber of Lords, with every eye in Solace on him, and looked at the slight, straight girl pointing at him, and for one moment, just one, something had come into his heavy face that nobody in that hall had ever seen there before. Not anger. Not fear. Something like recognition. As if he had been waiting for this, for eleven years, for nineteen years, for his whole life, the way a man waits for a door to open.

Then he turned, and walked out of the hall. Not quickly. He walked the whole length of the white marble floor, between the benches of the lords, under the galleries, with his back very straight and his medals glinting, and out through the great bronze doors at the end, and the doors swung shut behind him with a sound like a gun.

The committee of twelve lords deliberated for less than an hour. Their verdict was unanimous. The Lord Marshal of Valemont, Count Fenrick Morrow, was found guilty of felony, treason against a sworn lord, and dishonour. He was stripped of his command. The King would decide the rest.

Hayda heard it standing in the well of the hall, with her chin up, without moving a muscle. When it was done, she put her veil back down, and gathered up her father's ring and her father's letter and the bill of sale, and put them back in the cedar box, and closed it, and walked out of the Chamber of Lords on the Count of Cristal's arm.

In the carriage, with the curtains drawn, she sat very straight for a long time, with the cedar box in her lap.

Then she put her face against his shoulder, and wept: silently, terribly, for a long time, the way a child weeps who has been holding it in since she was six years old. He put his arm around her and held her and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say.

---

Aren Morrow found him that evening, at the theatre.

He had not meant to go. But he had a box at the Royal Theatre, as every man of fashion in Solace had, and it was expected that he would be seen in it, tonight of all nights, and he had learned long ago that what was expected was the best disguise. So he sat in his box in the second tier, in black, with Hayda beside him in white, veiled, and watched an opera about a king who loved a mermaid, and did not hear a note of it.

In the second interval, the door of his box opened.

Aren stood in the doorway. He was white, and his eyes were red, and he was in the plain black coat of mourning, not the uniform of the Guard cadets that he usually wore. Behind him in the corridor stood two other young men, his friends, looking frightened.

"My lord Count," said Aren. His voice was shaking. "I've come to ask you a question. And I want an answer. In front of witnesses."

Edric rose. "Ask it."

"The girl. The Sardhi princess. Who stood up in the Chamber today and destroyed my father." Aren's eyes went to Hayda, and away. "She's your ward. You brought her to Solace. You've been planning this. Haven't you? All along. From the beginning." He was breathing very fast. "The *Courant*. The merchant. All of it. It was you."

"Yes," said the Count of Cristal.

There was a gasp from the corridor. Aren's face did something terrible.

"Why?" he said. "Why? What did my father ever do to you? You saved my life. You brought me home. You sat at our table. You looked my mother in the eye. And all the time—" His voice broke. "*Why?*"

Edric looked at him. At Mira's eyes, in the boy's white face, burning.

"Your father," he said quietly, "sold a six-year-old girl in a market for a hundred gold pieces. That's all I did, Aren. I let her say so."

Aren hit him.

It was not a real blow. It was a slap, open-handed, the old sign, the one that every young man of good family in Valemont was taught from childhood and none of them ever expected to use. It caught the Count of Cristal across the face, and in the boxes on either side, and in the corridor, and in the pit below, where people had begun to look up, there was a sudden absolute silence.

"I challenge you," said Aren Morrow. He was weeping now, openly, and did not seem to know it. "For my father's honour. For my name. Tomorrow at dawn. On the Pale Cliffs. Pistols. Name your second."

Edric touched his cheek, where the boy's hand had been. It stung, very slightly. Under the skin, the Tidemark had gone cold as ice.

"Tomorrow at dawn," he said. "On the Pale Cliffs. My steward, Bastien Corso, will act for me." He bowed. "Goodnight, Lord Morrow."

---

She came at midnight.

He was in the study at the back of the house with the blue door, Valtor's old bedroom, at the window, in the dark. He had not lit a lamp. He had sent Bastien and Hayda and the servants to bed, and sat down at the desk with paper and ink, and begun to write: letters, and a will. He had got as far as the will. He had left Cristal to its council, and the books in the library to Tam, and a great deal of money to Maxim Moren and Lyse Valtor, and the rest of everything to Hayda, with a paper confirming once again that she was free and no one's. He had signed it, and sealed it. And then he had sat in the dark by the window, looking at the sea, and waited for the dawn.

He heard the gate. He heard Bastien, who had not gone to bed after all, speak to someone in the courtyard, low; and a woman's voice answer. He heard steps on the stair. The door of the study opened.

He did not turn round. He did not need to. He knew the step. He would have known it anywhere, across a hundred yards of swaying walkway in the dark, fourteen years, nineteen, a lifetime.

"Edric," said Mira.

---

She stood in the doorway in a long dark cloak with the hood back, with no diamonds in her hair. Her face in the faint light from the window was very pale and very still, and her eyes were dry.

He stood up, and turned, and looked at her.

"Edric Vane," he said, "died in the Drowned Keep nineteen years ago, madam. I'm sorry. You've come to the wrong house."

"Don't," said Mira. "Don't do that. Don't wear that face at me. You couldn't do it at the dinner, and you can't do it now." She came into the room, and shut the door behind her, and leaned against it. "I knew you the moment I saw you. Across that room. I knew you the way I'd know the sea." Her voice cracked, very slightly, and steadied. "I've known you every day since. I've lain awake every night since. I've sat at my window and watched your light. I've told myself I was mad. That you were dead. That it was grief, after all these years, playing tricks." She looked at him. "But you're not dead. Are you."

He did not answer for a long time.

"No," he said at last. "Not quite."

She made a sound, a small, broken sound, and put her hand over her mouth. But she did not weep.

"Where were you?" she said through her fingers. "Nineteen years. Where were you?"

"The Drowned Keep. On the Teeth." He said it quite flatly. "Fourteen years. In a cell under the sea. Under a Sun Warrant, without a trial. And then I got out."

She stared at him. Then she slid down the door until she was sitting on the floor, with her back against it, with her cloak around her, the way she used to sit on the landing stage in the Weir, and put her face in her hands.

He did not go to her. He stood by the window.

"The Keep," she whispered. "The *Keep*. We never knew. Nobody knew. We asked. Your father asked, and Moren, and I went up to the magistrate's house every day for a month, and they told us nothing. Nothing. They said you'd been taken to Solace, for trial. They said you'd been transported. And then they stopped saying anything at all." She lifted her head. "I could see the Teeth from the end of the walkway. Every night. Every spring tide, when I sat there with the boat. I could see the light on the Teeth. And you were there. All that time. *You were there.*"

"Yes."

"Why?" said Mira. "Who? Who did it? Who wrote that letter?"

He looked at her for a long moment, sitting on the floor of his study in her dark cloak, with her face white and her eyes on his, the girl from the Weir.

"You don't know," he said slowly. "Do you."

"Know what?"

He told her.

He told her about the Three Anchors, and the corner table, and the pen passing from the right hand to the left. He told her about the crumpled ball of paper thrown behind the settle. And about the soldier who stayed when the others left, and knelt in the sawdust, and carried it up the hill to the magistrate's house in the dark. He told her about Fenrick's face by the door at the Feast of Lamps, white and wet and frightened, watching the door all night. Waiting for it to open.

He watched it go into her. He watched it the way he had watched it go into Hayda, and Cade Rusk, and Fenrick himself in the Chamber that afternoon. And he felt it, through the water in her, like a wave through deep water, and it was the worst thing he had ever felt.

She did not cry out. She did not weep. She sat on the floor with her back against the door and her hands open in her lap, and the colour went out of her face until it was the colour of ashes, and she stared at nothing.

"Fenrick," she said, after a very long time. "Fenrick did it."

"Fenrick posted it. Dorran Quell wrote it. Cade Rusk watched them and said nothing."

"I married him," said Mira. Her voice was quite calm, and quite empty, like a house with all the furniture gone. "Eighteen months after. I married him. For my mother. He came to the Weir in his lieutenant's coat, with money in his pockets, and promised me a house with a fire, and a physician for my mother, and I married him. I wore grey. I didn't smile. I thought it was the worst thing I'd ever do." She laughed, a small, dry, terrible sound. "I didn't know. I never knew. I lay beside him for eighteen years. I had his son. I never knew."

She looked up at him.

"Did you think I knew?" she said. "All this time. Did you think I *knew*?"

"No," said Edric. "Never. Not for a moment."

She shut her eyes.

---

They sat in silence for a long time: she on the floor by the door, he by the window. Outside, the sea breathed against the foot of the cliffs. Somewhere in the city, a bell rang one o'clock.

"Aren," said Mira at last, without opening her eyes. "He's going to fight you. At dawn."

"Yes."

"He's sixteen."

"I know how old he is."

"He's never fired a pistol at a man in his life. He's never fired one at all, except at targets, in the courtyard, with his father." She opened her eyes. "You'll kill him."

"No," said Edric.

She looked at him.

"I won't kill him," he said. "I'd sooner cut off my own hand." He turned back to the window. "He's going to kill me."

There was a long silence behind him.

"What?" said Mira.

"He'll fire, and I'll stand there, and I won't fire back. And that'll be the end of it. He'll have avenged his father's honour. He'll have killed the man who ruined his family. He'll be able to hold his head up in Solace for the rest of his life." He was quiet a moment. "It's not a bad trade. It's the best I can do."

"You," said Mira, very slowly, "you're going to let my son shoot you. To save his pride."

"Yes."

"You could stop it." She was on her feet now. He heard her get up. "You could stop it, couldn't you? You could stop anything. I know what you are, Edric. Don't think I don't. The Weir-folk remember. Gran told me, before she died. She told me the sea had woken up, one night in a storm, and that it was you. I've seen the lines on your throat, at the dinner, when you thought no one was looking. You could make the pistol misfire. You could turn the ball aside. You could do anything you liked."

"I could," said Edric. "I won't."

"*Why?*"

He turned round.

"Because I've taken enough from you," he said. "I came to Solace to take everything from them. From Dorran, and Valtor, and Fenrick. I didn't come to take anything from you. And today I took your husband, and your name, and your house, and your son's father, and everything you've built for eighteen years. Whatever he was. However you married him. I took it all. And I'd do it again, because of what he did, to me, to Hayda, to her mother." He was breathing hard. "But I won't take your son. And I won't take his pride either. Not the last thing he's got. Let him have it. Let him have me. It's the only thing I've got left to give you."

Mira stood in the middle of his study and looked at him for a long time. Something was happening in her face, slowly, like ice breaking on a river in spring.

"You haven't changed at all," she said at last. "Have you. Under all that. All that cold, and that stillness, and that terrible face. You're still exactly the same. You're still the boy who stood on the walkway in the Weir, with Fenrick's hand on his sword, and asked him to come to our wedding."

"That boy's dead."

"No," said Mira. "He isn't. He's standing in front of me, being an idiot." She pulled her hood up. "He's not going to shoot you."

"Mira—"

"He's not going to shoot you," she said, "because I'm going home now, and I'm going to wake him up, and I'm going to tell him the truth. All of it. About his father. About the Three Anchors, and the letter, and the Keep, and you. And then he can decide for himself whether he still wants to defend his father's honour on the Pale Cliffs at dawn." She opened the door. "I've lied to him for sixteen years about who his father was, because I didn't know. I'm not going to lie to him for one more night now that I do."

She went out. He heard her on the stair, and in the courtyard, and the gate.

He stood in the dark study for a long time after she had gone, with his hand flat on the cold glass of the window, looking out at the sea.

He did not tear up the will.

---

The Pale Cliffs were a headland of white chalk to the north of the city, where the cliffs fell sheer to the sea, and where, for two hundred years, the young men of Solace had gone at dawn to kill each other over matters of honour. There was a flat stretch of turf at the top, cropped short by sheep, with the sea on three sides and the white city on the fourth, and the sun came up over the eastern hills straight into your eyes.

The Count of Cristal arrived at a quarter to dawn, in a closed carriage, with Bastien Corso and a surgeon. Hayda had wanted to come. He had forbidden it. She had looked at him for a long time, with her desert-well eyes, and then turned and walked out of the room without a word, and he had heard her, later, through the door of her room, praying in High Sardhi to the Thirst.

Aren Morrow was already there. He was standing at the far end of the turf with his two friends, in the plain black coat, with his back to the sea. And around them, at a little distance, a crowd. It seemed half of fashionable Solace had heard about the slap in the theatre, and had got up before dawn and driven out to the Pale Cliffs to watch the son of the disgraced Lord Marshal fight the Count of Cristal.

Edric got down from the carriage and walked out across the turf.

The sky in the east was grey, turning pink. The sea below the cliffs was dark and quiet. He felt it. He felt all of it, the whole vast slow breathing body of it, from the foot of the Pale Cliffs to the horizon and beyond, waiting. If he called it, it would come. It would rise up the face of the cliffs like a hand. It would turn any ball from any pistol in the world. He knew it as surely as he knew his own name.

He did not call it. He walked out to the middle of the turf, and stopped, and stood with his hands at his sides, and waited for the boy.

Aren came forward to meet him.

He was very pale. His eyes were red-rimmed and swollen, as if he had not slept, or had wept all night, or both. He was not carrying a pistol. His friends, behind him, were carrying the case, and it was shut.

He stopped three paces from Edric, and looked at him for a long, long moment. And Edric saw in his face that he knew. Everything. The Three Anchors, and the letter, and the Keep, and the name. His mother had told him, in the dark, in the middle of the night. And he had sat with it until dawn.

Then Aren Morrow turned round, to face the crowd, and lifted his voice so that it carried across the whole of the headland, over the sound of the sea and the wind.

"I withdraw my challenge," he said.

There was a sound from the crowd like a wave breaking.

"Last night," said Aren, "in the theatre, I struck the Count of Cristal, and accused him of plotting against my father. I was right. He did. But I was wrong to strike him for it." His voice was shaking, but it did not break. "Since then, I've learned things about my father that I did not know. Things that I will not speak of here, because they are not mine to speak of. But I know now that the Count of Cristal had cause. More cause than any man should ever have. And that what he did, he did in justice." He turned back, and faced Edric, and bowed: a deep, formal, perfect bow, the bow of a young lord of Valemont to a man of higher rank. "My lord Count. I beg your pardon. Publicly. Before all these witnesses. For the insult I offered you. I was a fool. I hope you will forgive me."

Edric looked at him. At the boy's white, wet, set face. At Mira's eyes, looking out of it.

"There's nothing to forgive, Lord Morrow," he said. "You defended your father. Any son would. I honour you for it."

Aren straightened. For a moment, just a moment, his face crumpled, like a much younger boy's. Then he turned, and walked away across the turf to his friends and his carriage, through a crowd that parted for him in total silence, and did not look back.

The sun came up over the eastern hills, and shone in Edric's eyes, and he stood on the Pale Cliffs alone with the sea breathing below him, and found that he was shaking.

---

Fenrick came to the house with the blue door an hour after noon.

He came on horseback, alone, without his medals. He had not been to bed. His uniform was creased and stained, and his face was grey and unshaven and swollen, and his eyes were bloodshot and wild. He had a sword on his hip. He hammered on the blue door with the hilt of it until Bastien opened it, and then pushed past him into the courtyard, shouting.

"Cristal! *Cristal!* Come out! Come out and face me, you coward, you liar, you foreign snake! My son wouldn't fight you. My son *apologised* to you. My son, on his knees to you, in front of all Solace!" His voice cracked. "Come out and fight me yourself!"

Edric came out onto the gallery above the courtyard, and looked down.

Fenrick stood by the black marble pool, with the fountain dancing beside him, with his sword half drawn, and stared up at him, breathing like a bellows.

"Who are you?" he said hoarsely. "Who *are* you? What did I ever do to you? I never saw you before this spring. I never heard your name. You come out of nowhere with your money and your ward and your house, and in one season you take everything I've got. My command. My name. My honour." His face twisted. "My wife. My son. She took him away this morning. They went out of the house with nothing, not a jewel, not a coin, not a word to me, and she wouldn't even *look* at me. What did you tell her? What did you say to her? Who *are you?*"

Edric looked down at him for a long moment.

Then he came down the stairs from the gallery, slowly, and crossed the courtyard, and stopped in front of Fenrick Morrow, an arm's length away, by the edge of the black pool. And let the stillness go.

He felt it go out of his face like a tide going out. He felt the Count of Cristal dissolve, like a reflection when the water is disturbed, and his own face come up through it: hard, and lined, and nineteen years older, with the white scar through the eyebrow and the grey at the temples and the faint blue branching line of the Tidemark climbing his cheek. But his. Edric Vane's face, with Edric Vane's grey eyes.

"Look at me, Fenrick," he said quietly. "Look at me properly. You know me. You've always known me. You've been waiting for me for nineteen years."

Fenrick looked at him.

"The Three Anchors," said Edric. "A corner table. A jug of black rum. Dorran Quell with a pen in his left hand. A ball of paper thrown into the corner, behind the settle. And you, Fenrick. On your knees, in the sawdust, in the dark, after the others had gone."

He watched it reach the man. He watched it the way you watch a wave reach a cliff: the long, slow approach, and then the impact. He saw Fenrick's face go white, and then grey, and then something beyond grey, something that was not a colour at all. He saw his mouth open. He saw his hand fall away from the hilt of his sword. He saw him take a step back, and another, until his heels were at the edge of the black marble pool, and the fountain splashed on his creased blue coat.

"No," said Fenrick. "No. No. You're dead. You're dead. They told me. Valtor told me, years ago, at a dinner. He said the boy died in the Keep. Of a fever. He said—"

"I didn't die," said Edric Vane. "I'm sorry to disappoint you."

Fenrick stared at him. And then something happened in his face that Edric had not expected and did not, afterwards, like to remember. The fear went out of it. The anger went out of it. And what was left, underneath, was something very like relief. The relief of a man who has been carrying a weight for nineteen years and has finally been allowed to put it down.

"Edric," said Fenrick. "Edric Vane." He laughed: a short, cracked, terrible bark, like the laugh on the walkway in the Weir, a lifetime ago. "Of course. Of course it was you. Who else would it be?" He looked around the courtyard: at the fountain, the black pool, the walls of green glass. "I used to dream about it. Did you know? For years. That you'd come back. That you'd walk into the Three Anchors one night and look at me. I'd wake up shouting. She'd ask me what it was, and I'd say it was the war." He looked back at Edric. "It wasn't the war."

"No," said Edric. "It wasn't."

Fenrick stood by the pool for a long time, with the fountain splashing on his shoulder. Then he took his sword out of its scabbard, slowly, and looked at it, and Edric did not move.

But Fenrick did not raise it. He only looked at it, for a long time, the way a man looks at an old friend. Then he put it back in the scabbard, and turned, and walked out of the courtyard, and out through the blue door, and got on his horse, and rode away down the Street of Lanterns at a walk, with his head bowed, like a man following a coffin.

---

He rode home to the Marshal's palace on the great terrace. The servants told it afterwards, over and over, to anyone who would listen.

He came in by the great door and found the house empty. The Countess was gone, and the young lord, with them. They had left everything: the jewels in their cases, the gowns in the presses, the silver, the pictures, the furniture, the carriages. They had taken nothing but a few clothes, and an old grey shawl that the Countess had kept in a chest for nineteen years and never worn. They had walked out of the palace on foot, the servants said, that morning, the young lord carrying a single bag, and gone down the hill to the post-house, and taken the public coach to Saltmere.

The Marshal listened to all this standing in the great hall, with his hat in his hand. When the servants had finished, he thanked them, very politely, and told them they might go. Then he went into his study, and shut the door.

The servants heard nothing for an hour. Then they heard a sound, a single sound, not loud, like something heavy falling.

They found him on the floor of his study, by the window that looked out over the sea, with his sword through his body, and his face quite peaceful, and his eyes open, looking at the water.

---

Bastien brought the news to the house with the blue door at sunset.

Edric heard it in the study at the back of the house, at the window, where he had been standing since Fenrick left. He did not turn round. He nodded, once, and Bastien went out.

He stood at the window and waited for it.

He had waited nineteen years for this. He had dreamed it, in the dark, under the sea: Fenrick Morrow, dead, by his own hand, ruined and alone. He had thought that when it came, it would be sweet. He had been afraid that it would be sweet. He had braced himself against the sweetness, against the sea rising in him, against the thing on the beach at Cristal that wanted it.

He waited. And waited.

Nothing came.

He stood at the window and looked out at the sea, and felt the Tidemark cold on his face and throat, and felt the water in the room, in the house, in the city, the whole slow breathing body of the sea beyond the cliffs. And he felt nothing. Not sweetness. Not sorrow. Not triumph, or grief, or pity, or relief. Nothing at all. Only a great cold emptiness, like the sea on a windless night, stretching away in every direction, very deep, very still, with no bottom to it that he could find.

Hayda came in, much later, in the dark, and stood beside him at the window.

"It's done," she said.

"Yes."

"I thought I would feel something," said Hayda, very low. "When it was done. For eleven years, I thought: when he falls, I will feel my father's ghost at peace. I will feel my mother smile. I will feel *something*." She was quiet. "I don't. I feel the way I felt in the merchant's house, at night, when I couldn't sleep. Empty. Like a well." She turned her head and looked at him. "Do you feel it? Anything?"

"No," said Edric.

She looked at him for a long time in the dark, at his still face, and the faint blue line of the Tidemark glowing on his cheek, which had climbed, he would see in the morning, almost to his eye.

"Then I think," said Hayda, "that we should both be very careful. Because I think the well is deeper than we know." And she put her small hand in his, and stood beside him at the window, and they watched the sea together until the stars came out, and neither of them said anything else at all.
