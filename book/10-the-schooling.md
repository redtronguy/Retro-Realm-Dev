## Chapter 10: The Schooling

Afterwards, when he tried to remember those years, Edric found that they ran together like water. There were no seasons in the Drowned Keep, no summers or winters, no festivals, no birthdays, only the grey light coming and going through the slit in the wall, and the guards' feet on the stairs, and the sea booming under the floor. A year was a little longer than a day, and a day was a little shorter than a year, and in between there was work.

He learned to read.

He had thought he could read already. He could make out a manifest, slowly, and sign his name, and puzzle through a letter from Mira if she wrote clearly. Faro taught him that this was not reading; this was recognising. Reading, Faro said, was when the words went in through your eyes and came out somewhere behind them as pictures, and voices, and ideas, and you forgot that there had ever been words at all.

They had no books. Faro had no paper, no ink, nothing. So he wrote on the wall of his cell with a lump of soft chalky stone he had found in the tunnel, and when the wall was full, he wiped it with a wet rag and wrote again. He wrote out poems he knew by heart, and made Edric read them back to him, aloud, over and over, until the halting syllables smoothed into lines and the lines into music. He wrote out pages of history, and pages of law, and pages of philosophy in which long-dead men in faraway places argued about whether a good man could ever do a bad thing for a good reason, and whether the sea was one thing or many, and what it meant to be just. And Edric read them, and argued back, and lost the argument, and argued again.

He learned numbers. Not a sailor's numbers, which were mostly for working out where you were and how long until you got somewhere, but Faro's numbers, which were for everything. The numbers of money: how it moved, how it grew, how a debt in one place became a famine in another. The numbers of the stars. The numbers inside things, the ratios and angles and curves, that Faro said were the bones of the world.

He learned languages. High Sardhi first, because Faro loved it, with its endless twining verbs and its forty words for sand. Then the old tongue of the north. Then the speech of the southern empires, in which Edric had once bargained for spice in Port Jasmine without understanding a word, and which turned out to have a whole grammar for ways of being polite to people you were about to cheat. Then, slowly, more of the Weir-tongue than he had known: old words, sea words, that his mother had never taught him and Faro somehow knew.

He learned the ways of the powerful. That was the strangest lesson, and the one Faro returned to most often, as if it mattered most.

"You were a sailor," the old man said. "A good one. An honest man in an honest trade. You saw the world the way honest men see it: as a place where people mostly say what they mean, and do what they say, and if they don't, it's because they're wicked. That's why you're here."

"Because I was honest?"

"Because you thought everyone else was." Faro tapped the wall where he had written, in his neat chalk letters, a single line from a book he said had been written by a minister of the old southern empire: *Men are moved by two levers only: fear and self-interest.* "That's too cold, of course. That old snake never loved anybody in his life. But it's more true than you'd like it to be, and less false than an honest man thinks. Every person you meet wants something. If you know what they want, and what they fear, you know what they'll do. And then you'll never end up in a cell again because you trusted a man's face."

"I don't want to be like that," said Edric. "Cold. Always looking for the lever."

"No," said Faro. "No, you don't. And you mustn't be. That's the trick of it, and it's the hardest trick there is: to see the whole world as clearly as the coldest man in it, and still be warm." He sighed. "I've never managed it. I don't know anyone who has. But you, I think, might."

---

He changed.

He did not notice it happening, any more than a man notices his hair growing. But he noticed, one day, catching sight of his reflection in the water of his cup, that his face had changed. The boy was gone. The open, sunburned, laughing face that people had trusted before he said a word was gone. In its place was a man's face, pale from years without sun, thinner, harder, with lines at the corners of the eyes and a set to the mouth that had not been there before. The grey eyes were the same colour, but they looked out of the face differently now: more slowly, more carefully, the way Faro looked at things. Taking them apart. Turning them to the light.

He noticed that his hands had changed: hard as horn from the chisel, the knuckles thick, a white scar across the left palm where the chisel had slipped in the second year.

He noticed, when he crawled through the tunnel now, that he moved differently: silently, without thinking, flowing over the rough stone like water over a weir.

And he noticed, one day, that he had not thought about Saltmere for a week.

That frightened him. He lay on his pallet that night and made himself think of it. The quays. The *Gull's Promise*. His father's window. The Weir, and the walkways, and the lamps. Mira's face. He found that he could not see her face clearly any more. He could remember that she had freckles, and that her eyes were green, and that her hair smelled of salt and smoke. But when he tried to put the pieces together into a face, it slid away from him, like a fish in murky water.

He did not tell Faro. But Faro knew. Faro always knew.

"It's the mind protecting itself," he said, a few days later, apropos of nothing, while they were digging. "Memories are like wounds. If you keep tearing them open, they never heal. So the mind lets them scar over. You haven't lost her, Edric. She's still in there. She's just behind the scar."

"I don't want a scar," said Edric. "I want her face."

"I know," said Faro gently. "I know."

---

The tunnel, meanwhile, was going badly.

They had reached the outer wall of the tower in the second year, a little ahead of Faro's estimate. And there, the chisel had stopped.

It had struck stone that was not like the other stone. The walls of the cells, the corridors, the inner tower, were old grey rock, hard but workable, layered like the pages of a book. But the outer wall of the Drowned Keep was black, and smooth, and glassy, like the stone around a volcano. When the chisel struck it, it rang like a bell, and skidded off, and left not a mark.

"Fused," said Faro, running his fingers over it in the dark. "Sunfire. They melted the rock and let it set again. It's like trying to dig through a bottle." He was quiet a long time. "I should have known. Of course they'd seal the outer wall. Of course."

They spent a year trying to find a way through it. They tried heating the chisel and cooling it. They tried wedging. They tried Faro's Tidecraft, sweating water into hairline cracks in the hope that it might freeze in the winter and split the stone, as water split the rocks on the cliffs at Saltmere; but it was never cold enough, deep in the Keep, for water to freeze. They tried following the wall along, in the hope of finding a weak place, a join, a crack.

In the end, they went down.

"The fused stone only goes so far," Faro reasoned. "They couldn't melt the whole rock. It's a shell, around the tower. If we go down, under the foundations, we may get beneath it. And then we come up and out on the cliff face lower down, nearer the water. Which is better anyway. A shorter climb."

It was another two years. And as they went down, the sound of the sea grew louder, and louder, until in the lowest reach of the tunnel, lying on his belly in the dark with the chisel in his hand, Edric could feel it through his whole body: a vast slow pulse, coming up through the rock, like lying with your ear against the chest of a sleeping giant.

---

It was in the fifth year, on a day when they were both too tired to dig, that Faro brought up the puzzle.

They were sitting in Faro's cell, with the grey light coming and going, eating their evening porridge. Edric had been talking about nothing much, about Halloran, and the voyage south, and some trick the old captain had taught him for reading the weather from the colour of the sea. Faro had been listening with half an ear, picking at his beard. And then, quite suddenly, he put down his bowl and said:

"I've been thinking about your letter."

Edric looked up. "What letter?"

"The anonymous one. The one that brought you here." Faro's blue eyes were very bright. "I told you, the first day, that it was a good puzzle, and that we'd come back to it. Well. I've been coming back to it, quietly, for five years. I think I've solved it. Do you want to hear?"

Edric put his own bowl down very slowly.

"Yes," he said.

"Then answer me some questions, and don't think about the answers, just say them. Ready? Who knew that you were carrying a letter for Solace?"

"Nobody. Halloran. He's dead."

"Who else? Think."

"My father. I told him, the first night I was home. Only that there was a message. Not who it was for. Not what was in it."

"Where did you tell him?"

"In his rooms. On Ropewalk Lane."

"And who lives below your father's rooms?"

Edric opened his mouth, and closed it again.

"Cade Rusk," he said slowly. "The tailor. My father's neighbour."

"And the floors in a house like that, old, cheap, crooked, are they thick or thin?"

"Thin," said Edric. "Thin. You can hear everything. You can hear a man cough."

"Good," said Faro. "Next question. The letter that accused you. You said the magistrate showed it to you. What was the writing like?"

Edric closed his eyes. He had seen it for only a minute, five years ago, by the light of a lamp, and he had been too shocked to look at it properly. But Faro had taught him, these five years, how to look at memories the way you look at a chart: to unroll them, and smooth them out, and see what was actually there.

"Bad," he said. "Blotted. Big, clumsy letters. Like a child's. But the words weren't a child's. They were exact. *Act swiftly*. *The proof will be found in his sea-chest, or upon his person, or in his father's rooms on Ropewalk Lane.* It knew exactly where to look."

"So: a man who could write well, writing badly. On purpose. With the wrong hand, I'd say, so his writing couldn't be known. A clever man. A careful man. A man used to writing things down exactly." Faro leaned forward. "Who on your ship wrote things down?"

"The purser," said Edric. "Dorran. Dorran Quell. He kept all the books."

"And did Dorran Quell have any reason to want you gone?"

"No. No, he—" Edric stopped.

He saw it. All at once, the way you see a reef under the water when the light changes. The aft hold. The casks of sand where there should have been pepper. Dorran's face, at the rail, the morning they came home, smiling with only his teeth. *Every captain needs to keep good accounts.*

"The pepper," he said. "There was pepper missing. A tenth of the cargo. I found the casks, they were full of sand. I thought it was the merchants on the Ember Coast. I was going to tell Moren. I never told anyone. I never even asked him." He put his hands over his face. "He thought I knew. He thought I was going to have him thrown out. He thought I was saving it."

"He thought you were the kind of man he was," said Faro softly. "That's the trouble with clever, careful men. They can't imagine anyone who isn't." He waited a moment. "Next question. The letter was put in the magistrate's box. Somebody had to carry it up the hill and post it. Did Dorran Quell strike you as a man who would carry his own crime up a hill, in the night, and put it through a magistrate's door with his own hand?"

"No," said Edric through his fingers. "No. He'd never risk it. He'd make somebody else do it."

"Who else, then? Who else at your wedding feast wanted you gone?"

And Edric saw the other face. The one he had tried, for five years, not to think about. White as paper, and wet, and frightened, by the door of the Three Anchors. A cold, damp hand that had held his for barely a moment.

"Fenrick," he said. "Fenrick Morrow. Mira's cousin. He loved her. He asked her to marry him and she said no. He threatened me on the walkway, three nights before, with his hand on his sword. And then he came to the feast to say sorry, and he stood by the door all night, watching it. Waiting for it to open." He took his hands away from his face. "He knew. He knew they were coming. He stood there all night and *waited* for them."

Faro said nothing. He let it settle.

"And Cade Rusk?" Edric said, after a long time. "Was he part of it?"

"What did Cade Rusk do at your feast?"

Edric thought. "He sat at the end of the table, by the beer. He was quiet. He's never quiet. Every time the door opened, he jumped. And when they took me, when I looked back from the door, he had his face in his hands."

"Then I would guess," said Faro, "that Cade Rusk heard about the letter through the floor, and carried the news to someone, in a tavern, for the price of a drink, because that is what men like Cade Rusk do with news. And that he was there when the letter was written. And that he knew. And that he could have stopped it, at any time, with a word. And that he said nothing." The old man shrugged. "Which is not the worst sin in the world. But it's not nothing, either."

The grey light was fading through the slit in the wall. The sea boomed, slow and deep, beneath the floor.

"Three men," said Edric. "Dorran, for a few casks of pepper. Fenrick, for Mira. Cade, for nothing. For a drink. For not wanting trouble." He laughed, and it was not a sound he recognised. "And me in here five years, and my father, and Mira, all of it. For that."

"Not three men," said Faro. "Four."

Edric looked at him.

"There's one more piece," the old man said. "The most important piece. The one that doesn't fit. Three men accused you, out of envy and jealousy and cowardice. But accusations are cheap. Magistrates get a dozen letters like that a month, and throw most of them away. What happened to you wasn't an accusation. It was a Sun Warrant. No trial. No record. The Drowned Keep, forever. Whoever signed that warrant didn't just believe you were guilty. He needed you to vanish." He held up one thin finger. "Now. Tell me again. Exactly. What happened in the magistrate's room."

So Edric told him again, as he had told it on the first day through the wall. The warm study. The fire. The careful, still young man behind the desk, who had listened, and believed him, Edric was sure he had believed him, and then asked about the packet. Opened it. Read the letter inside. Gone very still. Asked whether Edric had read it, whether he had told anyone. And then put it in the fire.

"And the name," said Faro. "The name of the man in Solace. The one you were to give it to. Say it again."

"Severin. A judge. A house with a blue door, on the Street of Lanterns, near the Sunspire."

"And the magistrate's name?"

"Valtor," said Edric. "Corvan Valtor."

Faro closed his eyes.

He sat for a long moment without moving, his thin hands folded in his lap. When he opened his eyes again, there was something in them that Edric had never seen there before, in five years: not cleverness, not delight in a puzzle solved, but something heavier, older, sadder.

"I knew a Severin," he said. "In Solace. Thirty-five years ago. A young judge, very clever, very brave, with a house on the Street of Lanterns. He was one of the Ebb, one of the very first. It was Severin who got me into the archives under the Sunspire. He risked everything to do it." The old man's voice was very quiet. "His full name was Severin Valtor. He had a son. A little boy, five or six years old, very solemn, very quiet. Always watching. Corvan."

Edric did not move.

"Your letter was addressed to the magistrate's father," said Faro. "The man who read it and burned it was the son of the man it was written to. A son who had made himself the King's most loyal servant. A son who would have lost everything, his post, his future, perhaps his life, if anyone ever found out that his father was the Ebb. And there you sat, on the other side of his desk: the only living soul who'd seen that letter and knew where it was going." He spread his hands. "He didn't send you here because he thought you were guilty, Edric. He sent you here because he knew you were innocent. Because you were the one loose thread that could unravel him."

---

Edric did not remember standing up.

He remembered the cell, very small. He remembered the walls, very close, pressing in. He remembered a sound coming out of his throat that was not a word, and a roaring in his ears that was not the sea. He remembered a face, a young, still, careful face behind a desk, saying *I've done you a kindness*, and holding a letter in the fire until the flame reached its fingertips.

And he remembered his cup.

It was sitting on the floor beside Faro's pallet, half full of water, where he had put it down. And as he stood there, shaking, with the roaring in his ears, the water in the cup began to move.

It shivered. Then it rippled, in tight rings, from the edges in, as if something had struck the cup. Then the rings broke apart, and the water began to churn, slopping against the sides, climbing them, as if the cup were in a boat in a storm. And then it leapt: a single spout of water, jumping up out of the cup a hand's breadth into the air, and falling back, and splashing over the rim onto the stone.

The roaring in his ears stopped. Edric stood very still, staring at the cup.

Faro was staring at it too. His face had gone quite white.

"Did I—" said Edric. "Was that—"

"Yes," said Faro. "That was you."

For five years, Edric had sat in the dark with the old man every day and tried to do what Faro did. Breathe. Be still. Listen. Feel the water. Reach for it. For five years, he had felt nothing. Not a drop. Not a ripple. He had begun, privately, to believe that Old Ysolde had been wrong, that there was no sea in him at all, that Faro was being kind.

And now, in a single moment of blind, roaring hatred, he had made the water jump out of the cup.

He began to laugh. He could not help it. "It's there," he said. "Faro, it's there. It's really there. I did it."

"Yes," said Faro. He did not laugh. He was looking at the water spilled on the stone, and his face was grave. "Yes. You did it. And now you're going to sit down, and listen to me very carefully, because this is the most important thing I will ever teach you."

Edric sat down. The laugh died in him, at the look on the old man's face.

"The Deep is chained," said Faro. "You know that. Chained, and starved, and drunk from, for three hundred years. Do you know what that does to a god? It makes it angry. Not the way a man is angry. The way the sea is angry, in a storm: endlessly, mindlessly, without pity. When a Tidewarden reaches for the Deep, Edric, he reaches through all that anger. That's why it's so hard to learn, now. That's why it hurts. A calm mind can barely touch it." He pointed at the spilled water. "But an angry mind? A mind full of hate? That reaches the Deep very easily. Because they're the same. Like calls to like."

"So I should be angry," said Edric slowly. "If that's what works. If that's the only way I can—"

"No," said Faro, with such force that Edric flinched. "No. Listen to me. Hate is a tide. It will carry you a very long way, very fast. Anywhere you like. And it will never, ever bring you home." He leaned forward, and took Edric's hard, scarred hands in his own thin ones, and held them. "I've seen what you saw just now in my mother's books. In the old histories. Tidewardens who reached for the Deep with hate in their hearts, because it was quick, because it was strong. Every one of them was strong, for a while. And every one of them, in the end, became something that wasn't a man any more. Something that was mostly sea. Mostly anger. Mostly the Deep, looking out through a human face."

Edric looked down at their joined hands.

"They took everything from me," he said, very quietly. "Everything. My father. Mira. My ship. My life. Five years. They took it all, and they're out there, somewhere, now, today, living. Eating dinner. Sleeping in beds. And I'm supposed to just—"

"I'm not telling you to forgive them," said Faro. "I'm not a priest of the Sun. I'm telling you that if you ever get out of here, and if you ever go after them, you must go after them with a cold head and a warm heart, and never the other way round. You must go after them because it's just, and not because it's sweet. Because the moment it becomes sweet, Edric Vane, the moment you start to *enjoy* it, the sea will be in you, and you'll be lost."

He let go of Edric's hands.

"And now," he said, more briskly, "I think we've both had a long enough day. Go back to your cell. Sleep. Tomorrow we dig, and the day after we dig, and in between we'll start teaching you how to reach the water without hating anything. It'll be slow. It'll be very slow. Much slower than what you just did."

"How slow?"

"Years," said Faro. "Years and years." He smiled faintly. "Fortunately, we have those."

---

Edric lay on his pallet that night and did not sleep. He lay with his eyes open in the dark and said the names over to himself, one by one, as if he were learning them for an examination.

*Dorran Quell.*

*Fenrick Morrow.*

*Cade Rusk.*

*Corvan Valtor.*

Under him, under the stone, the sea boomed, slow and deep, like a great heart. And for the first time in five years, it seemed to him that it was beating in time with his own.
