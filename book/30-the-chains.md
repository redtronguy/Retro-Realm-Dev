## Chapter 30: The Chains

The longboat grounded on the lowest of the dry ledges at the far side of the cave, opposite the King, with the dead grey water between them, and Edric stepped out of it onto the salt-crusted stone.

He did not look at the Hundred, ranged on the ledges around the cave with sunfire in their hands. He did not look at the chains. He looked at the King, standing on the lowest ledge on the other side of the pool, thirty yards away, blazing, smiling at him.

"You've come a very long way," said Aurelian, "to watch me finish."

"I've come to stop you."

"With what?" The King spread his golden hands. He did not say it cruelly. He said it the way a man points out to a child that the tide is coming in. "I can feel you, Edric. I've been feeling you all the way from your island. Every mile, a little less. You're nearly empty. You couldn't raise a drop of water now. You came up the shaft in my tower and it nearly flayed you, and that was a month ago, when you still had half of it left." He tilted his head. "You've nothing. And I have everything. Three hundred years of everything, and the last of it going down my throat as we speak."

He knelt again, quite casually, at the edge of the pool, and dipped one hand into the light of the chains, and drew it out, and drank from his cupped palm, and Edric felt it go. Felt the whisper at the bottom of the world grow fainter.

"Stop it," said Edric.

"In a little while," said the King, licking his fingers. "There's not much more. A few days. A week. And then it'll be done, and the sea will be water, only water, forever. And I'll never be afraid of anything again." He stood up. "And you'll be nothing. A man who used to hear the sea. Like the old one, Faro. Sitting in a cell somewhere, listening to silence." He smiled. "I'll give you his cell, if you like. For sentiment."

---

Edric breathed.

In for eight. Out for eight. He found the stillness. It was easy to find now; there was nothing in him to disturb it. He went down into it, and down, and down, past the mud and the stones and the weed, past the small warm thing at the bottom that had woken Lyse, down to the door, the place where the sea came in.

He opened it.

Nothing came.

Or almost nothing. A trickle. A breath. Like Faro's drop of water on the floor of the cell, trembling on the stone, barely able to rise. He reached for it, and it came to him, very slowly, very faintly, and lay in him, a little cold, a little salt, and that was all. The King was right. There was almost nothing left.

He opened his eyes.

"There," said the King gently. "You see."

And he raised his hand.

---

The sunfire came across the dead water like the beam from the mirror on the flagship off Cristal: a spear of gold light, thirty yards long, burning. Edric saw it come. He reached for the sea, for the trickle in him, for anything, and pulled the water up out of the pool in front of him in a thin grey wall, the way he had pulled it up a hundred times on the beach at Cristal against the blighted things.

The sunfire went through it as if it were not there. It struck him in the chest.

He did not remember falling. He remembered the light, white and gold and blinding, and the pain, the Tidemark on his chest flaring against it, cold against hot, the way water screams on a forge, and then he was lying on his back on the salt-crusted stone of the ledge, with his coat smoking and his chest on fire and the roof of the cave very far above him, and someone screaming his name. Mira. Hayda. He could not tell.

"I'm sorry," said the King's voice, from across the water, sounding as if he meant it. "I did warn you."

He heard the Hundred move.

He heard them come down the ledges, from all round the cave, slowly, the way old men walk, with the sunfire in their hands. He heard Jac Teague curse, and the scrape of her cutlass coming out of its scabbard, one-handed. He heard Bastien, and Aren, and the Sardhi brothers, coming out of the longboat onto the ledge. He heard Hayda say something in High Sardhi, very low, that was not a prayer.

And he heard Cassia Vell get up out of the bottom of the boat, with her burned black hands, and walk to the edge of the ledge, and stand there in her white and gold armour, facing the King across the water.

"*Aurelian*," she said.

The King turned his head.

For a moment, just a moment, there was something in his golden face that Edric had not seen there before, even in the Sunspire. Surprise. And under the surprise, something almost like hurt.

"Cassia," he said. "My sword. My High Radiant." He looked at her hands. "What have you done to yourself?"

"I opened your gate," said Cassia Vell. "With my own fire. To let the Warden in." Her voice did not shake. "Twenty-two years I knelt to you. Six hours a day. I taught your histories to a hundred novices. I burned a man in his temple, three weeks ago, while he sang a hymn to the Sun, because you told me the people on that island drowned children." She lifted her black hands. "They don't drown children. You do. You're doing it now. I heard you laugh, in your tower. I've been listening to you laugh ever since."

The King looked at her for a long moment.

"Oh, Cassia," he said, quite softly. "I'm so sorry."

And he raised his hand, and the sunfire took her.

---

She did not fall at once. She stood on the edge of the ledge in the gold light as it poured into her, through her armour, through her, and she held out her own black ruined hands against it, and her own fire came up into them, faint and gold, against his. For a moment, a single moment, it held. His fire, and hers, meeting over the dead water. And Edric, lying on the stone, saw the King's face change: saw it tighten, very slightly, as if he were pushing against something heavier than he had expected.

It was enough. It was a moment. And in that moment, the Hundred, coming down the ledges towards the boat with sunfire in their hands, faltered. They stopped. They looked at their High Radiant, standing at the edge of the water with her hands raised against the King. And some of them, Edric saw, lowered their hands.

Then the King pushed, and Cassia Vell's fire went out, and she fell back onto the stone of the ledge and lay there, and did not get up.

But the moment had been enough. Because in that moment, while the King's attention was on her, Edric had rolled over, and got his hands under him, and crawled to the edge of the ledge, to the very edge, where the dead grey water lapped at the salt-crusted stone. The way he had crawled to the edge twelve years ago, with Faro behind him on the ledge above, saying *don't*.

He looked down into the water.

The chains went down through it, thirty yards away, gold, burning, down and down, into the dark. And in the dark, so far down that he could barely feel it, so faint that it was almost nothing, something breathed. Once. And then, after a very long time, again.

*Not by any Tidewarden that ever lived,* Faro had said, sitting on the ledge above, twelve years ago, with tears in his beard. *To break chains like that, you'd need the Deep's own strength. Turned against them. From the inside. If a Tidewarden could somehow take the god's own power into himself. All of it.*

*No living man could hold that. It would burn you to nothing. Or drown you. Or worse.*

Edric put his hand into the dead grey water.

---

It was cold. It was the coldest thing he had ever felt. Colder than the sea under the Teeth, colder than the bottom of the world. It came up his arm like ice, like death, and into his chest, where the sunfire was still burning, and the two met, cold and hot, and he heard himself scream.

And then he was not on the ledge any more.

He was in the dark. The full dark, the one that is not empty but full, the dark at the bottom of the sea where he had been once before, twenty years old in a burial sack, drowning. There was no up or down. There was no cold, no pain, no breath. There was only the dark, and him in it, and the attention.

But the attention was so faint now. So thin. Like a lamp burning very low.

And in front of him, in the dark, an eye opened.

It was not the eye he remembered. That eye had been as large as the sky, the colour of the sea at the very bottom of the world, with a whole ocean moving in its depths. This eye was small. Dim. Clouded, like the eyes of the blighted things that came ashore at Cristal, filmed over, grey. It opened slowly, as if it hurt, and looked at him, and he felt it look, and it was so weak, so very weak, that he could barely feel it at all.

*Little one,* said the Deep.

The voice was a whisper. A breath. A sound like the last wave on a beach before the tide turns.

*You came back.*

*I said I would.*

*You said you would break him.* The clouded eye looked at him. *You cannot. You have nothing. You gave it back, on the beach, under the white cliffs. Half of what I gave you. And the rest, he has taken, as he takes me.* A long pause. A breath. *There is nothing left in you, little one. Or in me. Soon there will be nothing at all.*

*Then give me what's left,* said Edric.

The eye did not move.

*All of it,* he said. *Everything you have. Whatever's left. Faro said it. A Warden who takes all of you, and carries it to the Lock. From the inside. Give it to me. All of it. And I'll break the chains.*

The Deep was silent for a long time.

*You know what it will do to you,* it said at last.

*Yes.*

*You will not survive it. No mortal body can hold all of me. Not even what is left of me. It will burn you out like a candle in a furnace. Or drown you. Or worse.* The whisper was very faint. *And before it does, for a little while, you will be me. All of me. Three hundred years of me. Everything I have felt, in the dark, at the end of the chains. Everything he has taken. You will feel it all. And you will want what I want.*

*I know.*

*Do you know what I want, little one?*

*Yes,* said Edric. *You want to drown them. All of them. Every coast. Every city. The guilty and the innocent. You want to take back everything you've lost, with interest. You want the King's dam to break, and the flood to come.* He looked at the clouded eye. *He told me. In his tower. He said that's what you'd do, the moment you were free. That's why he chained you. That's why he says he's holding you down.*

*And do you believe him?*

Edric was quiet for a long time, in the dark.

*I don't know,* he said. *I felt you in the stillroom. And on the roof at Cristal, when they burned Olaf. I felt what you wanted. I've heard you say it. Drown them all.* He looked at the eye. *But I also heard you say interesting. On the beach, when I gave you back. And on the quay, when I lifted the ships instead of drowning them. Interesting, you said. Twice.* He waited. *Why?*

The clouded eye looked at him for a very long time.

*Because no one ever had,* said the Deep. *In all the time I have been. No one who held my strength ever gave it back. No one who could drown their enemies ever chose not to. Not one, in all the ages of your kind. They took, and took, and drowned, and took. As I took. As the golden one takes.* The whisper was so faint now that it was barely there. *And then you. On the beach. Giving back. On the quay. Lifting, instead of drowning. And I thought: here is something I have never seen. Here is something new.*

*And I am so very old, little one. And there is so little that is new.*

---

Somewhere very far away, on the ledge, in the temple, in the world, he could hear fighting. Steel on steel. Jac cursing. Sunfire roaring. Aren shouting. Hayda's voice, high and fierce. And Mira's, calling his name, over and over.

*Give it to me,* he said. *All of it. Whatever's left.*

*And then?*

*And then I'll break the chains. And then I'll give it back.*

The eye went very still.

*All of it?*

*All of it. Everything. The way I gave back on the beach. I'll take all of you, and break the chains with it, and then I'll give it all back to you. Every drop. And you'll be free. And I'll be—* He stopped. *I'll be whatever's left.*

*There may be nothing left.*

*I know.*

*And when I am free? When the chains are gone, and I am myself again, and I remember everything he took? What then, little one? What if he is right? What if I rise up out of the dark like a hand, and drown them all?*

Edric looked at the small, dim, clouded eye in the dark, and thought about an old man in a cell, weeping because a drop of water had risen. He thought about a city on an island in the west, burned and smoking, with lamps on its walls. He thought about a girl on a landing stage with her feet in the water, and a crown in her lap, deciding.

*Then I'll have been wrong,* he said. *And I'll be sorry. But I don't think he's right. I think he's been telling himself that for three hundred years, so that he'd never have to stop drinking. I think he's afraid. He's always been afraid. Faro said so. It was the root of it, from the beginning.* He held the eye. *And I think you're tired of taking. I think that's why you said interesting. I think you want to see what happens if, just once, someone gives it all back.*

*Wait,* he said. *And hope.*

The Deep was silent for a very long time.

And then, in the dark, very faint, very far, he felt it. Something he had never felt from the Deep before, in all the times it had spoken to him. Not anger. Not hunger. Not patience.

Something like a smile.

*Take it, then,* said the Deep. *Take all of me. And let us both see what is left.*

---

It came in.

Not like the flood in the stillroom. Not like the tide on the quay at Cristal. It came in the way the whole sea would come in if the whole sea could fit through a single door. It came up out of the dark at the bottom of the world, all of it, everything that was left, three hundred years of it, faint and thin and dying, but still the sea, still the god, still older and larger than anything that has ever walked or flown. And it came into him.

He felt himself break.

Not his body. Something else. Something that had always held him together, the way the hull of a ship holds out the sea. He felt it crack, and split, and go, and the sea came pouring in through the gaps, and filled him, and went on filling him, and he was not Edric Vane any more. He was not anyone. He was the sea.

He was everywhere. He was the grey dead water in the temple under the Teeth, and the grey dead water in the harbour at Cristal, silver with fish, and the grey dead water lapping at the quays of Saltmere and the foot of the cliffs at Solace and the beaches of the southern empires and the ice of the far north. He was every drop of it, all at once. He felt every shore, every reef, every wreck on the bottom, every bone. He felt the King's chains, seven of them, wrapped around him like wire around a throat, burning, and he felt the King at the end of them, a small golden blazing point of greed and fear on a ledge in a cave, drinking.

And he remembered.

He remembered everything. Three hundred years of it. The Binding: a young prince of the Radiant Order standing at the edge of this pool, in this cave, when it was a temple full of lamps and singing, with his arms raised and fire pouring out of him, and the chains coming down, and the terrible strangling tightness, and the scream that every Tidewarden in the world had heard. The Drying: eleven years of fire, falling on every Tidewarden hall, every city on every coast where people had listened. The white city on Cristal burning, and the people standing in the sea up to their waists with their hands raised, calling *help us, help us*, and the sea trying to rise, and failing, and the fire coming down. Three hundred years of being drunk. Three hundred years of growing weaker, and sicker, while the fish thinned and the weed died and the storms went wrong. Three hundred years of the blight coming up out of him like pus from a wound. Children drowning in their beds from the blight-cough, and the sea feeling every one of them, and unable to stop it.

And the rage. Gods, the rage. It came up through him like a storm coming up out of a clear sky: vast, black, endless, three hundred years deep. He felt it fill him, fill the whole sea, every drop of it, everywhere, all at once. And he felt the sea begin to rise.

All over the world, on every coast, the sea drew back.

He felt it go. Out from the harbours, out from the beaches, out from the foot of every cliff, drawing back and back, a mile, two miles, the way the sea draws back before the great wave comes, leaving the mud and the wrecks and the dead fish bare and stinking under the night sky. He felt the people on the shores come out of their houses, and stand on the beaches, and stare at the sea going out, and out, and out, and not understand. He felt them in Saltmere, on the quays, in the Weir. He felt them in Solace, at the foot of the cliffs. He felt them on Cristal, on the burned terraces, by the lamps.

And he felt the wave begin to build.

*Yes,* said something in him that was him and was not him. *Yes. Now. Now. Let it come. Let it come in over all of them, every coast, every city, every soul, and take back everything. Every life he drank. Every year. Let them feel what I felt. Let them drown, as mine drowned. Let them all drown.*

And it would have been so easy. It would have been the easiest thing in the world. He was the sea. He only had to let go.

---

Something touched the water.

It was very small. Very far away. On a ledge in a cave under a black rock, at the edge of a pool of grey water, where seven chains went down. A hand. A woman's hand, rough with net-cord, cold, reaching down from the ledge into the water, the way the Weir-folk reached into the water to seal a vow.

And a voice, very small, very far, singing.

He knew the song. He had heard it once, a lifetime ago, on the Night of Lamps, in the Three Anchors, when an old woman with eyes like black beads stood up from her chair with a bundle in her hands and began to unwrap it, and the room went quiet. It was the Weir-folk's wedding song. The one they sang at dawn, when the tide came up the channels and the boat with no oars went out. It was very old, older than Saltmere, older than the King, in the oldest form of the Weir-tongue, the one Faro had taught him and the Deep had spoken in the dark. And it was not a song about a wedding. He understood that now, for the first time, with the whole of the sea in him. It was a song *to the sea*. A song asking the sea to agree.

*Take us out,* the song said. *Take us out on your tide, if you will have us. Carry us, and we will carry you. We give ourselves to you, and you give yourself to us. Take us out.*

And then something else touched the water. Something light, and dry, and very fragile. A circle of woven sea-lavender, faded to the colour of dust, threaded with small white shells, laid on the grey surface of the pool by a woman's hand, and floating there, very gently, turning, on the still dead water, at the foot of the chains.

He felt it. With the whole of the sea, he felt it: the smallest thing in all the water in the world. A crown that an old blind woman had made for a wedding that never happened, and kept for fourteen years, and given back, and been given back again. Floating. Waiting. Asking.

*Take us out.*

---

And he remembered who he was.

Not all at once. Slowly, the way the tide comes in over a flat beach. He remembered a landing stage in the Weir, and his feet in the water, and a girl laughing at a heron. He remembered a cell, and a scratching in the wall, and an old man's face coming out of a hole in a shower of dust: *you're bigger than I expected*. He remembered a drop of water rising off a stone floor, and an old man weeping. He remembered a small boat, past the harbour mouth of Cristal, and a gull stealing a bread roll, and laughing until he had to hold on to the side. He remembered a girl in the dark saying *I don't think you're as cold as you'd like everyone to believe*. He remembered an old man in a chair, blinking: *enough*.

He remembered a boy on the floor of a stillroom, with a wooden horse in his hand.

He remembered that he had made a promise.

*Give it all back.*

The wave was still building, far out, on every coast. He could feel it, rising, gathering, a wall of water higher than any cliff, the whole of the sea's three hundred years of rage, waiting for him to let go.

He did not let go. He reached into it, the way he had reached into the flood in the stillroom, down and down, through the black roaring cold of it, past the rage, past the hunger, past the memories, down to the very bottom of the sea, where it was still. Where it had always been still. Under everything. The stillness that Faro had said was always there, if you went deep enough.

And from the stillness, he reached for the chains.

---

He felt them. Seven of them, wrapped around the throat of the world, burning, sunfire, the strongest working there had ever been. He felt them from the inside, the way the Deep had felt them for three hundred years: the tightness, the heat, the strangling.

He took hold of the first one. Not with hands. With the sea. With all of it, everything, three hundred years of it, gathered into a single point, the way he had once gathered the water into a blade to cut a rope under the Teeth. And he pulled.

The chain did not break. It burned. It blazed against him, gold and white, the King's oldest fire, and he felt it scorch the sea, felt the water around it boil, felt the King at the far end of it, on the ledge, scream. But he did not let go. He held it, with the whole of the sea, and pulled, and pulled, and the chain began to stretch.

And then it snapped.

In the temple under the Teeth, on the lowest ledge, Aurelian, Eternal, staggered, and fell to his knees.

He felt the second chain. He took hold of it. He pulled. It burned, and blazed, and snapped.

The King screamed. On the ledges around the cave, the Hundred cried out, all at once, and the sunfire in their hands flickered and went out, like candles in a wind.

The third. The fourth. Each one burned. Each one blazed. Each one snapped. And each time a chain snapped, he felt something come back down it: not from the sea to the King, but from the King to the sea. Something golden, and old, and warm, flowing back down the broken chain into the water like blood from a wound. The life. The stolen life. Three hundred years of it, pouring back out of the King and down into the sea it had been taken from.

The fifth. The sixth.

He felt the seventh. The last. The one that had been fixed first, three hundred years ago, by a young prince standing at the edge of this pool, terrified of dying.

He took hold of it, with the whole of the sea, and pulled.

---

In the temple under the Teeth, on the lowest ledge, at the edge of the pool, the King knelt with his hands raised, and the seventh chain of gold light shuddered, and blazed, and broke.

And the light went out of him.

Mira saw it. She told it afterwards, years afterwards, to her son, and to her son's children, on the end of the walkway in the Weir, with their feet in the water. She saw the gold go out of the King like a tide going out: out of his eyes, out of his hair, out of his skin, pouring out of him and down into the water at his knees in a great bright stream, and the water drinking it, as if it were thirsty. She saw his face change. She saw the beauty go out of it, the fine bones and the grave gentle saint's face, and something else come up underneath it: lines, and hollows, and grey. She saw his hair go white. She saw his skin go thin and spotted and loose. She saw his hands, raised, shaking, go to claws.

She saw him age. Thirty years. Fifty. A hundred. Two hundred. Three hundred. In the space of a breath, a man who had looked thirty became a man who was three hundred and thirty years old, kneeling at the edge of a pool, with his ancient clawed hands raised in front of his face, staring at them.

He lifted his head, and looked across the water. Not at her. Not at the others on the far ledge. At the pool. At the place where the seven chains had gone down into the water, and where now there was nothing, only grey water, beginning, very slowly, to move.

"I was afraid," said Aurelian. His voice was a thread, a whisper, the voice of something very old. "I was so afraid. Of the dark. Of the end. Of not being." He looked at his hands. "I only wanted not to be afraid."

And then he was dust.

He did not fall. He simply was not there any more: a shape of grey dust, kneeling, for a moment, at the edge of the pool, and then the dust settling, slowly, onto the salt-crusted stone of the ledge, and onto the surface of the water, where it floated for a moment, grey on grey, and then sank.

On the ledges around the cave, the Hundred stood with their dark hands lowered, and stared.

---

And in the dark at the bottom of the sea, where Edric Vane was, the sea was free.

He felt it. He felt the chains go, all seven of them, and the tightness go, and the heat go, and the terrible strangling weight of three hundred years lift off the throat of the world. He felt the sea take a breath. The whole of it, everywhere, all at once: a vast, slow, endless breath, like a sleeper waking, like a drowning man breaking the surface. He felt it fill. He felt the life that had been stolen come pouring back down into it, golden, warm, three hundred years of it, and the sea drinking it, and growing, and waking.

And he felt the wave.

It was still there. Out on every coast, the sea still drawn back, the wall of water still building, higher, higher, the whole of the sea's rage, waiting. The sea was free, and it remembered everything, and it was so strong now, stronger than it had been in three hundred years, and it was in him, all of it, and it wanted.

*Now,* said the sea, in him. *Now, little one. I am free. Let it come.*

*No,* said Edric.

He held the stillness. He held it at the bottom of everything, under the rage, under the wave, under the whole free and furious sea. He held it the way you hold a door shut against a storm. And from the stillness, with the very last of what was his own, the small warm thing at the bottom, the well with water in it, he reached for the sea in him. All of it. And he began to give it back.

---

It was like the beach under the Pale Cliffs, and nothing like it. On the beach, he had given back the silt of five years, and it had been like tearing out his own veins. This was the whole of the sea. Three hundred years of it, and more, all the ages before the chains, all the time the Deep had been. He reached for it, all of it, and pushed it out of himself, back down, back into the dark, back where it belonged.

It did not want to go.

It clung to him. It had been in him only a little while, an hour, a moment, but it had filled every part of him, and it did not want to go. It wanted the wave. It wanted to come in over the land. It wanted to take back. It pushed against him, the whole free furious weight of the sea, and he felt himself begin to break, the last of himself, the small warm thing at the bottom, cracking.

He pushed.

*Give it back,* he said. *All of it. I promised. The sea hears.*

He pushed, and pushed, and felt the sea begin to go out of him. Slowly. The way the tide goes out over a flat beach. Out of his blood, out of his breath, out of his bones. Out of the lines of the Tidemark, that had branched across his chest and arms and throat for six years, cold and blue and burning. He felt them go dark, one by one, like lamps going out along a quay at dawn. He felt the sea draining out of him, back down into the dark, back into itself, and as it went, he felt himself grow lighter, and emptier, and smaller, until there was almost nothing left of him at all.

And far out, on every coast, he felt the wave hesitate.

He felt it hang there, at its height, a wall of water higher than any cliff, all over the world, poised, trembling, for a long, long moment.

And then he felt it begin, very slowly, to lie down.

Not to come in. Not to crash. To sink. Back into itself. The way a breath goes out. The way a man who has been about to shout lets the shout go, unspoken, and breathes out instead. All over the world, on every coast, the sea came back: not in a wave, but in a tide. A long, slow, gentle tide, coming in over the bare mud and the wrecks and the dead fish, filling the harbours, lapping at the quays, rising up the beaches to where the people stood staring, and stopping at their feet, and lying still.

The last of the sea went out of Edric Vane.

And there was nothing left.

---

He was in the water.

The real water. Grey, and cold, and moving now, very slowly, in the temple under the Teeth. He was sinking. He could feel it: the water closing over his face, the light of the cave going dim above him, the cold coming into him. He did not feel the sea. He did not feel the currents. He did not feel anything at all except the cold, and the dark, and the water in his lungs, the way any drowning man feels it.

He was very tired. He did not mind. He had kept his word.

And then, very faint, very far away, from somewhere so deep that it might have been the bottom of the world, he heard a voice. Not the vast slow voice that had spoken to him in the dark. Something smaller. Something that had been strangled for three hundred years and had just remembered how to speak.

*Little one.*

He could not answer. He did not need to.

*You gave back,* said the Deep. *All of it. Every drop.* A long silence, in the dark. *In all the ages of the world, no one ever did.*

*I will remember.*

And something lifted him. Very gently. The way the sea had once set him down on Gull Reef, on a morning when a red-haired smuggler found him. A swell, rising out of the dark beneath him, carrying him up, through the grey water, towards the light.

And hands. Hands in the water, reaching down. Rough hands, with net-cord calluses. Small hands, strong, with a knife scar across one palm. A one-armed grip, cursing. They found him, and closed on his coat, and his arms, and his hair, and pulled.

---

He came up out of the pool coughing, onto the salt-crusted stone of the lowest ledge, and lay there with the sea coming out of his lungs, grey, in great heaving gouts, and three people holding him: Mira, soaked to the skin, with her black hair plastered to her face, who had gone into the water after him without a moment's thought, because she was Weir-folk and the Weir-folk could swim before they could walk; and Hayda, with her white silk ruined and her arms round his chest; and Jac Teague, one-handed, on her knees, gripping his collar, swearing at him in a steady, furious, tearful stream that did not seem to need breath.

"You *idiot*," Jac was saying. "You stupid, stupid, *stupid*—"

He coughed, and breathed, and coughed, and breathed. The air went into him. Real air. Cold and damp and smelling of salt. He lay on his back on the stone and looked up at the roof of the cave, far above, where the rotted rope still hung from the hole he and Faro had broken through twelve years ago.

It was dark. The chains were gone. The gold light that had filled the temple for three hundred years was gone. There was only the light of the lanterns from the longboat, and the faint grey glow coming in through the sea-gate at the far side of the cave, where the bars of sunfire had been, and were not any more: the first light of dawn, coming in off the sea.

He lifted his hand, slowly, and looked at it.

It was his hand. Thin, and scarred, and shaking. He turned it over. The lines were gone. The Tidemark, that had branched across his skin for six years, pale and blue and cold, had gone. There was nothing there at all. Only skin.

He opened his shirt, with fingers that did not work properly, and looked at his chest.

Nothing. Only a scar, over his heart, white and new, where the sunfire had struck him. And under it, his own heart, beating.

He reached for the sea.

It was like Faro's drop of water. Exactly like it. A whisper. A breath. So faint he could barely feel it: the sea, very far away, the way you hear it in a shell held to your ear. But it was there. And it was not screaming. It was not crying. It was not raging, or hungry, or patient.

It was singing.

He lay on the stone with his eyes closed and listened to it, and did not know he was weeping until Mira put her hand on his face.

---

The Keep was falling.

They felt it before they heard it: a long, deep, grinding shudder in the rock under the ledge, coming up from far below, from the roots of the mountain under the sea, where the chains had been fixed. And then the sound: stone breaking, somewhere very high above them, very far, and something huge falling.

"The Teeth," said Cassia Vell.

She was alive. They had not known. She was sitting up on the far ledge, by the longboat, with Aren Morrow kneeling beside her holding her up, and her black hands in her lap, and her face grey. She was looking up at the roof.

"The King built the Keep on the Lock," she said. "Sunfire. The whole rock is sunfire, holding it together. The outer walls, fused. The foundations." Her voice was very faint. "It was all his. And he's gone."

The rock shuddered again. A great slab of stone broke away from the roof of the cave, high above, and fell, and struck the water in the centre of the pool, where the chains had been, and sent a wave across it that broke over the ledge.

"The boat," said Jac Teague. "*Now.*"

They got him into it. He did not remember how. He remembered being lifted, and carried, and laid in the bottom of the longboat beside Cassia Vell, with her burned hands and her grey face, and Mira's shawl over him, soaking. He remembered Bastien and Aren at the oars, and the Sardhi brothers, pulling. He remembered Hayda in the bow, with a lantern, shouting directions. He remembered the walls of the tunnel going past, black and dripping, and the rock shaking, and stones falling into the water around them. He remembered the sea-gate, the great arch, where the bars of sunfire had been, open now, nothing across it but the grey light of dawn.

And he remembered looking back.

Behind them, the Teeth were falling into the sea. The great black mountain that had stood over the Lock for three hundred years, with its walls and its tower and its red light, was coming apart: cracking down the middle, from the summit to the waterline, like a rotten tooth. The walls fell. The tower leaned, and leaned, and fell, and the red light at its top went out for the last time, and the tower went into the sea with a sound like the end of the world. And the sea took it. All of it. The walls, the tower, the cells, the stairs, the corridors, the courtyard where the guards had swung him in a sack and let him go. The cell where he had lain for fourteen years. The cell where an old man had dug for six years in the wrong direction, and found him.

The sea took it all, gently, and closed over it, and lay still.

And the sun came up.

---

He saw it rise from the bottom of the longboat, lying on his back with Mira's shawl over him and his head in Hayda's lap. It came up out of the sea in the east, red, then gold, the way it had come up every morning of his life, and every morning for three hundred years before that. The same sun.

It did not hurt. That was the first thing he noticed. He looked at it, full in the face, as it came up over the edge of the sea, and it did not hurt at all. It was only warm.

And the sea.

The sea was blue.

Not grey. Not dead. Blue: the deep, clear, living blue of the open ocean on a summer morning, the colour he remembered from the deck of the *Gull's Promise* off the Ember Coast, twenty years ago, when he was nineteen and believed in endings. It stretched away from them in every direction, all the way to the edge of the world, blue and glittering and alive, moving in long slow swells, breathing.

And there were fish in it.

He saw them. They all saw them. A shoal of silverfin, the great shoals that Halloran used to talk about, that turned the sea white for a mile, coming up from the deep water under the place where the Teeth had been, thousands of them, tens of thousands, rising to the surface in the dawn light and turning all together, silver, flashing, like a field of stars.

And beyond them, a mile off, something vast and dark rose out of the sea, and blew a great plume of spray into the morning air, and sank again, slowly, and its tail rose after it, black and enormous, and fell, and the sound of it came across the water like a bell.

Nobody in the boat said anything for a long time.

Then Jac Teague, at the tiller, with her one good arm, laughed. And it was a laugh Edric had not heard from her in five years. It was the laugh from the deck of the *Slip*, on the morning she found him on Gull Reef, before she knew anything about him at all.

"Well," she said, wiping her eyes. "Well. Would you look at that."
