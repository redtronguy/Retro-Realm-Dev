# Part Four: The Three Hands

## Chapter 20: The Count Arrives

Solace had seen a great many rich men in three hundred years. It had seen princes from the southern empires with elephants in their trains, and Sardhi emirs who rode through the streets on horses shod with silver, and merchant-lords from the far east who built palaces on the cliffs and filled them with gold and went bankrupt in a season. It had seen everything. It prided itself, the white city on its cliffs, on having seen everything, and on being impressed by none of it.

It had never seen anything like the Count of Cristal.

He had bought a house on the Street of Lanterns. That was the first thing people said about him, before anything else was known. The Street of Lanterns was the oldest and the finest street in Solace: a long, curving, cobbled avenue that wound along the edge of the highest cliff below the Sunspire, lined with tall old houses behind high walls, each with its lantern burning over the gate all night. Nobody bought a house on the Street of Lanterns. You inherited it, from a grandfather who had inherited it from his grandfather, or you married into it, or you waited thirty years for one of the old families to die out and then paid a king's ransom for the lease.

The Count of Cristal had bought one in a week. The old house with the blue door, at the far end of the street, which had stood empty for twelve years, since the old judge who had lived there was struck down by a palsy and taken to live with his son. He had paid three times what it was worth, in gold, without bargaining. And then he had brought in a hundred workmen from the docks and kept them at it day and night for a month, and nobody knew what they were doing, because the gate was kept shut and the walls were high. But the carts went in loaded with marble and timber and glass and things wrapped in sailcloth, and the carts came out empty, and the neighbours said they heard strange sounds at night: water running, where there had never been water, and music.

When the gate opened at last, the blue door was still blue. Everything else had changed.

---

He gave his first dinner a fortnight after he arrived. The invitations went out on cards of thick cream paper, with a single curling wave stamped in silver at the top, and every one of them was accepted within the hour.

Afterwards, the people who had been there would talk about that dinner for the rest of their lives. They would describe the house: the courtyard where a fountain played in a pool of black marble, and the water in the fountain seemed to dance in time to the music of the Sardhi musicians who sat around it; the great hall with walls of green glass, like being inside a wave; the dining room with its ceiling painted with a map of the stars, and the table that seemed to float, and the food that seemed to come from everywhere in the world at once. Oysters from the northern coast that had been alive that morning, three hundred miles away. Fruit from the southern empires that nobody in Solace had ever seen. Fish that the Count's steward said had been caught in the Shattered Reach the day before yesterday, which was impossible, which everyone knew was impossible, and which tasted as if it had come out of the sea an hour ago.

They would describe the Count: tall, grave, quiet, dressed always in black, with a high collar and a single silver pin in the shape of a wave. His still, unreadable face. His voice, with its faint foreign lilt, which never rose and never needed to. The way the room seemed to arrange itself around him without his trying, the way water arranges itself around a rock.

And they would describe his ward.

---

She came down the great stair into the hall of green glass when all the guests had arrived, a slight, straight, small figure in white Sardhi silk, with gold at her wrists and throat and black hair down to her waist, and the whole room stopped.

"My ward," said the Count, "Hayda bint Tariq, of Qasr."

There was a murmur. A princess. A real princess, from the Sardh. Everyone had heard, in the past fortnight, that the Count had a ward, but nobody had known who she was. Now people looked at her, and at the Count, and at each other, and the murmur grew.

Hayda stood at the foot of the stair and looked around the room with her dark, still eyes, as if she were looking for someone. And Edric, standing beside her, felt the moment she found him: felt it through the water in the air, in the fountain, in the glasses in people's hands, like a stone dropped into a pool. He did not turn to look. He did not need to.

Across the hall, by the door to the courtyard, stood a tall man in the dark blue and gold of a Marshal of Valemont, with a row of medals on his breast. He was in his early forties, broad-shouldered and still powerful, with black hair gone grey at the temples and a hard, handsome, heavy face that had been brooding when it was young and had become, with age and power, something more settled and more cold. He was talking to someone. He glanced up, idly, at the name, as people do. And looked at the girl at the foot of the stair for a moment, with no more than polite interest.

Then he looked away again, and went on talking.

He had not known her. Of course he had not. She had been six years old, and he had sold her in a market for a hundred gold pieces, and he had never thought of her again.

Hayda did not move. She stood quite still at the foot of the stair with her chin up and her hands folded, and only Edric, who was standing next to her, and who could feel the water in her, saw how her pulse was racing, and felt the cold, bright, terrible stillness that came over her, like a blade being drawn very slowly from its sheath.

"Not yet," he said to her, very low, in High Sardhi, without moving his lips.

"No," said Hayda, in the same language, in the same way. "Not yet." And she went forward into the room, and smiled at the first person who spoke to her, and was charming to everyone, all evening, and never once looked at the Lord Marshal of Valemont again.

---

Edric had been waiting for her all evening.

He had told himself he would not. He had told himself, all the way up the coast road from the Greyhills, with the boy riding beside him, and every day for the fortnight since, in the house with the blue door, that he was ready. That he had been ready for five years. That the girl from the Weir had died nineteen years ago, at the Feast of Lamps, the same as the boy who had loved her, and that the woman who would come through the door tonight on the Marshal's arm was a stranger. A countess. The wife of one of his enemies. Nothing more.

He had not been ready. He knew it the moment she came in.

She came in late, after the Marshal, alone. A carriage had been delayed, someone said. She stood in the doorway of the hall of green glass for a moment, looking for her husband, and Edric turned his head, as everyone turned their heads, and saw her.

She was thirty-eight. She was tall, and slender, and pale, as the ladies of Solace were pale. Her black hair was swept up in the fashion of the court, with diamonds in it. She wore a gown of dark green silk, the dark green of deep water in shade. Her face was thinner than he remembered, and finer, and older, with a stillness of its own, a guarded, careful, lady's stillness that had never been there before. The freckles were gone. Powdered over, or faded, after twenty years away from the sun and the wind.

But it was her face. He knew it, the moment he saw it, completely, as if he had seen it yesterday. As if it had never been gone. As if it had been there all along, behind the scar, waiting, the way Faro said.

He did not move. He held the stillness. He held it in his face, and his hands, and his voice, as Faro had taught him, as the books had taught him. He held it the way you hold a breath, and he felt the Still Water in him, cold and smooth, making his face a pool, making it show only what the watcher brought to it. A foreign count. A rich stranger. Nobody.

She looked across the room at him, as everyone looked at him.

And she stopped.

He saw it happen. He saw it go through her like a wave through deep water: the colour leaving her face all at once, so fast that the powder stood out on her cheeks like dust on a window. Her hand going out, blindly, to the doorframe beside her. Her lips parting. Her eyes, her dark green eyes, the colour of deep water, going wide, and wider, fixed on his face across the crowded room with an expression he would remember, afterwards, for the rest of his life. It was not recognition. It was something before recognition, something deeper and older and more terrible: the look of someone who has heard a voice they know is dead.

The Still Water broke.

He felt it go. It did not crack; it simply was not there, any more, as if he had never been holding it. He had held it in front of Cade Rusk, and Pieter Moren, and Vasco Rook, and the Lord Marshal of Valemont, and a hundred others. He had held it for days at a time. And it went out of him in a single moment, at a single look, the way a reflection goes out of a pool when someone you love leans over it.

For a moment, just a moment, across the hall of green glass, with a hundred people between them and the music playing and the fountain dancing, Mira Solen and Edric Vane looked at each other with their own faces.

Then Aren was there, beside her, taking her arm, laughing. "Mother! There you are! Come and meet him, come on. Come and meet the Count."

And she let herself be led. Across the hall, through the crowd, with her hand on her son's arm, and her face as white as her diamonds, and her eyes on his. And Edric stood and watched her come, and could not move, and did not try to find the stillness again, because he knew it would not come.

"Mother," said Aren, "this is the Count of Cristal. The man who saved me. My lord, my mother. The Countess Morrow."

She looked at him. She was close enough now to touch. He could smell her: not salt and smoke and the river, any more, but some court perfume, roses and amber. Under it, very faint, so faint that no one else in the world would have noticed it, the marsh. The Weir. Twenty years of perfume had not quite washed it out.

"My lord," said the Countess Morrow. Her voice was quite steady. Lower than he remembered, and smoother, with the soft Weir hiss on the *s* trained almost out of it. Almost. "I owe you my son's life."

"You owe me nothing, madam," said the Count of Cristal. His own voice, he was distantly pleased to find, was steady too. "Your son owes me nothing. Any man would have done the same."

"Would he?" said Mira. "I don't think so. I don't think any man would have." She held out her hand.

He took it, and bowed over it. Her fingers were ice-cold, and trembling, very slightly, all the way through, like a bird held in the palm.

She took her hand back. "Will you excuse me, my lord? I find I'm a little faint. The heat. Aren, take me into the garden. I need some air."

And she turned away, on her son's arm, and went out through the doors into the courtyard, and did not come back in.

---

She did not come back in, but the Marshal did. He came across the hall afterwards, when the dinner was announced, with his hand out, and a broad smile on his heavy face, the smile of a man who has been told to be grateful and intends to do it properly.

"My lord Count," said Fenrick Morrow. "I've been trying to reach you all evening. You've done me a service I can never repay."

"It was nothing, my lord Marshal."

"It was my son." Fenrick seized his hand and shook it, hard, a soldier's grip. "I've been trying to catch that bandit Rook for six years. Six years! And you rode into his camp and took my boy out of it like a man collecting his hat from a cloakroom." He laughed, a big, hearty, public laugh, the laugh of a man who has learned that the Lord Marshal of Valemont is expected to laugh like that. "You must tell me how. I could use a man like you."

Edric looked at him.

He had thought about this moment for nineteen years. He had imagined it a thousand times, in the dark of the Keep, in the library at Cristal, on the road north: Fenrick Morrow's face, close, in front of him. He had imagined what he would feel. He had thought it would be rage. He had been afraid that it would be rage, that the sea would come up in him like a storm and he would not be able to hold it.

It was not rage. He was surprised by what it was.

He looked at Fenrick Morrow, the Lord Marshal of Valemont, with his medals and his grey temples and his big public laugh, and he felt the water in him: felt it the way he felt the water in everyone, now, without trying, the way you feel the weather. He felt the blood moving in the man's heavy body. He felt, under the laugh and the smile and the grip of the hand, the currents. And they were not the currents of a happy man. They were cold, and sluggish, and full of things that had settled at the bottom a long time ago and never been stirred: fear, old fear, so old it had become a part of him, like a stone in a riverbed. Shame. And something else, under the shame, which he recognised because he had felt it himself, for fourteen years, in a cell under the sea. Waiting. A man who had spent nineteen years waiting for a door to open.

"I'll tell you how one day, my lord," said the Count of Cristal. "I promise."

And he let go of Fenrick Morrow's hand, and turned away, and went in to dinner.

---

He did not eat.

Nobody noticed, at first. The Count sat at the head of his own table, with the Marshal on his right hand and the Lord Treasurer's wife on his left, and talked, and listened, and was charming, and the plates came and went in front of him. But towards the end of the meal, a young man at the far end of the table, a secretary at the Ministry of the Interior with a sharp tongue and a habit of noticing things, leaned across to Hayda and said, in a low voice: "Your guardian doesn't eat, Princess. Is he ill?"

Hayda looked down the table at Edric, at his untouched plate, at the Marshal beside him, talking.

"He is Sardhi in his ways," she said, quite calmly. "He has lived a long time in my country. And in my country, there is a custom." She lifted her glass of water, and looked at the young man over the rim, with her dark still eyes. "We say: never break bread with a man you mean to kill. For once you have eaten salt with him, he is your guest, and you cannot touch him." She sipped. "My guardian never eats in company. He says it saves trouble later."

The young man laughed, uncertainly, thinking it a joke.

Hayda did not laugh. She set down her glass, and turned back to her other neighbour, and did not look down the table again.

---

Later, when everyone had gone, he went up to the roof.

The house with the blue door had a flat roof, like many of the old houses on the Street of Lanterns, and from it you could see the whole of Solace spread out below, terrace upon terrace, glittering with lamps, down to the black sea at the foot of the cliffs. Above it, behind it, so close it seemed he could reach out and touch it, the Sunspire rose into the night, white against the stars, with its gold light burning at the top. Even at night, it hurt to look at. He kept his back to it.

He stood at the parapet in the cold wind off the sea, with his collar open, and let the stillness go entirely, all of it, and felt the Tidemark burning cold on his throat and his face, and did not care who saw.

Hayda found him there, an hour later, with a cup of coffee.

"She knew you," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"The moment she saw you. Across the room. I saw her face." Hayda put the coffee in his hands and stood beside him at the parapet. "Your mask doesn't work on her."

"No," said Edric. "It never would have. I should have known." He held the cup without drinking. "The books say Still Water shows people what they expect to see. Everyone in that room expected to see a foreign count. Fenrick expected to see a man who'd done him a favour. Even Cade, at the inn, expected to see a priest." He looked down at the city. "She didn't expect anything. She looked at me, and saw me. As if she'd never stopped looking for me."

Hayda was quiet for a long time.

"What will you do?" she said at last.

"Nothing. About her. She's done nothing to me." He drank, finally, and the coffee was bitter and scalding. "Whatever happens to him, it won't be because of her. She married him to keep her mother alive. I'd have wanted her to. I did want her to, in the Keep. I made myself want it." He set the cup on the parapet. "She's the one thing in this city I'm not here for."

Hayda looked at him sideways, with her chin up.

"You keep saying that," she said. "Like a prayer. As if you'd forget it, if you didn't say it." She turned to go. Then she stopped. "My father used to say that the most dangerous water is not the storm. It's the still pool, on a hot day, that looks shallow enough to stand in." She went back down the stairs, and left him alone with the wind and the city and the light at his back.

---

Across the city, on the great terrace below the Sunspire where the palaces of the Lords of Valemont stood in their gardens, in a bedroom on the second floor of the Marshal's palace, the Countess Morrow sat at her window in the dark and did not sleep.

Her husband had come home in a good humour, and drunk a glass of brandy in his study, and gone to bed. Her son had come home talking about the Count, the Count, the Count, the house, the fountain, the ward, the Count, and had gone to bed still talking. The servants were asleep. The palace was silent.

She sat at the window with her hands in her lap and looked out across the dark terraces of Solace to the Street of Lanterns, high on the cliff, where a single house still had a light burning on its roof, and a single figure stood beside the light, at the parapet, looking down at the sea.

She sat there until the light went out, and the figure was gone, and the sky in the east began to grey. And in all that time she did not move, and did not weep, and said only one thing, once, very quietly, to the window, to the dark, in the soft hissing tongue of the Weir that she had not spoken aloud in nineteen years.

"*Edric.*"
